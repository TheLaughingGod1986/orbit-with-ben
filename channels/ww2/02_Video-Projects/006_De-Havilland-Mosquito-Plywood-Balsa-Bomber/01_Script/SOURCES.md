| **C09** | `S13`, `S14` | De Havilland's calculations showed that stripping out gun turrets, gunners, and armour saved over `2,000 lb` of weight and cut parasite drag enough to add roughly `30 to 50 mph`—allowing a twin-Merlin wooden bomber to cruise near `380–400 mph`, faster than the `360 mph` Messerschmitt Bf 109E of 1939–40. | `[TNA1]` `AVIA 15/95`; `[SH]` pp. 12–21 |
| **C10** | `S15`, `S16` | Air Ministry and Bomber Command officers initially opposed an unarmed wooden bomber (`Specification B.15/39`), demanding defensive gun turrets and a third crewman; at a conference on `29 December 1939`, Air Marshal Sir Wilfrid Freeman backed de Havilland's unarmed design—nicknamed *"Freeman's Folly"* in Whitehall—leading to **Specification `B.1/40`** and an initial contract for **50 aircraft** on **`1 March 1940`**. | `[TNA1]` `AVIA 15/95` & `AVIA 10/311`; `[PO]`; `[SH]` pp. 20–28 |
| **C11** | `S17` | In late 1939, de Havilland established its secret DH.98 design and prototype office inside **Salisbury Hall**, a moated 17th-century manor house at London Colney, Hertfordshire, five miles from Hatfield aerodrome. | `[SH]` pp. 24–31; `[DB]` |
| **C12** | `S18`, `S19` | In May 1940, newly appointed Minister of Aircraft Production **Lord Beaverbrook** ordered work halted three times on projects outside his five priority types (Spitfire, Hurricane, Wellington, Whitley, Blenheim); Sir Wilfrid Freeman and de Havilland director **Francis St Barbe** saved the Mosquito by proving it used almost zero scarce aluminium alloy during the Dunkirk and Battle of Britain crisis. | `[PO]`; `[SH]` pp. 29–35 |
| **C13** | `S20`, `S21` | The Mosquito fuselage was a true structural sandwich composite (`~7/16 inch` / `11 mm` thick): a `3/8-inch` (`9.5 mm`) core of lightweight **Ecuadorian balsa wood** bonded between two skins of three-ply **Canadian yellow birch** (`1.5–2 mm` thick, laid at `45 degrees` to take torsional shear), working like the flanges and web of an structural I-beam. | `[SH]` pp. 36–45; `[AP]`; `[BI]` |
| **C14** | `S22`, `S23` | Each fuselage was moulded in **two separate left and right half-shells** (split vertically from nose to tail) over mahogany and concrete moulds (`mandrels`), clamped with steel bands while the glue cured, then fitted out internally in the open—wiring, hydraulics, radios, and controls installed while workers stood comfortably beside the half-shells—before the two halves were scarf-jointed together like a model kit. | `[SH]` pp. 40–48; `[PO]` |
| **C15** | `S24` | Along with casein glue, de Havilland used **`Aerolite` urea-formaldehyde synthetic resin adhesive**, developed by **Dr Norman Adrian de Bruyne** (`1904–1997`) at **Aero Research Limited**, Duxford—waterproof, resistant to tropical fungus and bakteria, and cured rapidly using low-voltage electrical heating strips built into the jigs. | `[DB]`; `[SH]` pp. 42–46 |
| **C16** | `S25`, `S26` | The `54-foot 2-inch` one-piece wing was built around two full-span box spars with laminated **Douglas fir (`Oregon pine`)** booms and birch plywood webs, housing ten self-sealing fuel tanks inside the wing structure; out of a bare structural weight of `~4,000 lb`, fewer than **`280 lb` (`< 7%`)** consisted of metal forgings and fittings, and the entire wooden exterior was wrapped in doped **Madapolam** cotton fabric for a rivet-free finish. | `[SH]` pp. 44–50; `[AP]` |
| **C17** | `S27`, `S28` | Over **400 subcontractors** produced Mosquito assemblies across Britain, led by the cabinetmakers and furniture factories of **High Wycombe**, Buckinghamshire, and London—including **E. | **C18** | `S29`, `S30` | On `3 November 1940`, prototype **`W4050`** (painted prototype yellow) was trucked in secret from Salisbury Hall to Hatfield; on **`25 November 1940`**—less than eleven months after design work began—chief test pilot **Geoffrey de Havilland Jr.** (`1910–1946`) and **John E. Walker** flew `W4050` for the first time. | `[SH]` pp. 31–35; `[BI]` |
| **C19** | `S31`, `S32` | During official Service trials at **A&AEE Boscombe Down** in February–March 1941, `W4050` reached **`392 mph`** (`22,000 ft`), outrunning the contemporary **Spitfire Mk II** (`~357–370 mph`) by more than `20 mph`; in April 1941, demonstrated before US Army Air Corps chief **Major General Henry "Hap" Arnold**, `W4050` climbed and rolled cleanly on one engine with the other propeller feathered. | `[SH]` pp. 35, 65–72; `[BI]` |
| **C20** | `S33`, `S34` | The Mosquito's first operational sortie took place on **`17 September 1941`**, when photo-reconnaissance Mosquito **PR Mk I `W4055`** of `1 PRU` (RAF Benson) photographed Brest, La Pallice, and Bordeaux at `25,000 ft`, outrunning three Messerschmitt Bf 109s; its first bombing sortie took place on **`31 May 1942`** over Cologne (`No. 105 Squadron`). | `[SH]` pp. 135–138, 173–175; `[TNA3]` `AIR 27/826` |

| **C22** | `S37`, `S38` | Equipped with the **Oboe** blind-bombing radar transponder in **No. 109 Squadron** (inside Air Vice-Marshal Donald Bennett's **No. 8 Pathfinder Group** from December 1942), Mosquitoes flew at `28,000–30,000 ft` along a constant-range radio arc from the **"Cat"** station in England until triggered by the **"Mouse"** station, marking targets in the Ruhr through solid cloud within **`100 to 150 yards`** at `250 miles` range. | `[WB]` Vol. II & Vol. IV; `[TNA2]` `AIR 14/1847`; `[TNA3]` `AIR 27/853` |

| **C24** | `S45`, `S46` | No. 8 Group's **Light Night Striking Force (`LNSF`)** flew `170` Mosquito raids on Berlin (including **36 consecutive nights** in early 1945), dropping `4,000-lb` Cookies and often flying two Berlin sorties per night with the same aircraft; across **over `28,000` Bomber Command Mosquito sorties**, the Mosquito suffered a **`0.7%` (`~0.63%–0.7%`) loss rate**—the lowest of any aircraft in Bomber Command, less than a third of the heavy-bomber average (`~2.2%–3.5%`), and roughly **one-fifth the industrial cost and crew losses per ton of bombs dropped**. | `[WB]` Vols. | **C27** | `S51`, `S52` | The same wooden airframe served across five wartime roles: unarmed bomber (`B Mk IV/IX/XVI`), photo-reconnaissance (`PR Mk I/IV/IX/XVI`), radar night fighter (`NF Mk II` to `NF Mk XXX` with centimetric AI radar, destroying `~600` enemy aircraft and `~600` V-1 flying bombs), fighter-bomber (`FB Mk VI` with four `20 mm` Hispano cannon and four `.303` guns), and Coastal Command anti-shipping strike (`FB Mk XVIII` **"Tsetse"** mounting a **`57 mm` (`6-pounder`) Molins autoloading cannon**). | `[SH]` pp. 255–340; `[AP]`; `[BI]` |
