[S01 · G1 · map: the Atlantic coast of France, Norway in the top corner, one dock lit at St Nazaire]

In the early hours of 28 March 1942, an old destroyer flying a German flag steamed up the estuary of the River Loire at almost twenty knots. Her bow was packed with more than four tons of high explosive, set on fuses that could not be stopped. Her target was not a ship. It was a door.

[S02 · RAF reconnaissance photograph of St Nazaire docks]

The door was a steel gate, thirty-five feet thick, at the end of the largest dry dock on the Atlantic coast of France. Britain had decided that wrecking that one gate was worth risking more than six hundred men, because of a single German battleship sitting a thousand miles away in a Norwegian fjord.

[S03 · G2 · title card over Tirpitz silhouette]

This is the story of how one old ship was turned into a bomb, and why the Royal Navy thought it had to close a dock to keep a battleship out of the Atlantic.

[CHAPTER CARD: One Ship, One Dock]

[S04 · Tirpitz in a Norwegian fjord, RAF reconnaissance photograph]

By the start of 1942 the battleship Tirpitz was the most powerful warship in Europe. She displaced more than forty thousand tons fully loaded, carried eight fifteen-inch guns, and could outrun almost anything that could outgun her. In January 1942 she moved to Norway, to the fjords near Trondheim.

[S05 · G3 · map: North Atlantic convoy routes, Tirpitz in Norway]

Her mere presence there tied down British battleships, carriers and cruisers that were badly needed elsewhere. Winston Churchill wrote to his chiefs of staff that January that the destruction or even crippling of this ship was the greatest event at sea at the present time, and that no other target was comparable to it.

[S06 · Bismarck, photographed from Prinz Eugen, May 1941 (licence check: see SHOT_LIST)]

The Admiralty had seen what a German battleship loose in the Atlantic could do. Ten months earlier, Tirpitz's sister ship Bismarck had broken out, sunk the battlecruiser HMS Hood, and been hunted down only after a chase across the ocean. When the Royal Navy finally caught her, Bismarck had been heading for one place: St Nazaire.

[S07 · G4 · graphic: what a battleship needs after battle]

The reason was a hard fact of naval engineering. A big ship damaged below the waterline cannot be mended at sea. She has to be lifted clear of the water, onto dry blocks, so that men can reach her hull. For a ship the size of Tirpitz, that meant a dry dock big enough to hold her, and on the whole Atlantic coast that Germany controlled there was only one.

[S08 · Normandie liner in the dock before the war (licence check: see SHOT_LIST) or G5]

It had been built at St Nazaire in the early 1930s for the French liner Normandie, then one of the largest ships in the world. The Normandie dock, also called the Forme Écluse Louis Joubert, was about three hundred and fifty metres long and fifty metres wide. It worked as both a dry dock and a lock, joining the river to the inner harbour basin.

[S09 · G5 · diagram: the dock, its two caissons, pumping station, winding houses]

At each end stood a caisson: a huge hollow steel box that rolled sideways out of a recess on rails to seal the dock. Fill it with water and it sat firm. Pump the dock dry and a ship inside sat on her blocks in the open air. The caissons, the pumps that emptied the dock and the machinery that moved the gates were the heart of the whole thing.

[S10a · G3 · map: Tirpitz's sortie against convoy PQ 12, March 1942]

The danger was not imaginary. In early March 1942, while the raid was still being planned, Tirpitz put to sea to hunt the Arctic convoy PQ 12, bound for Russia. She missed the convoy in bad weather. On 9 March, torpedo bombers from the carrier HMS Victorious found her and attacked, and every torpedo missed. She ran back to her fjord unharmed. The Admiralty had been reminded how close it had come, and how little it could rely on catching her at sea.

[S10 · G3 · map animation: Tirpitz's possible route to the Atlantic, St Nazaire as her only repair yard]

The British reasoning ran like this. If Tirpitz broke out into the Atlantic and was damaged, she had to be able to reach St Nazaire, or she would face a long and dangerous run home to Germany. Take away the dock, and the German admirals would think twice before sending her out at all. Destroying the Normandie dock would not sink Tirpitz. It would keep her in her fjord.

[CHAPTER CARD: Too Strong to Bomb]

[S11 · RAF reconnaissance photograph of the Loire estuary and St Nazaire]

The first answer was bombing, and it was not good enough. A caisson that size could shrug off almost any bomb of the day, and the town of St Nazaire was packed with French civilians living around the docks. British policy in 1942 limited bombing in occupied France where it put French lives at risk.

[S12 · G6 · map: the Loire estuary, shoals, the deep-water channel, gun batteries]

The Germans had also made St Nazaire one of the best defended places on the coast. Next to the dock they had built massive concrete pens for their U-boats. Coastal guns and flak batteries lined both banks of the Loire. The deep-water channel ran close under those guns, and the wide mud flats on either side were usually too shallow for a warship.

[S13 · IWM portrait of Commander Robert Ryder]

The job went to Combined Operations, the headquarters under Lord Louis Mountbatten that planned raids on the occupied coast. Its planners proposed a raid from the sea. The naval force would be commanded by Commander Robert Ryder. The soldiers, from No. 2 Commando with demolition teams from other commando units, would be led by Lieutenant-Colonel Charles Newman.

[S14 · IWM portrait of Lieutenant-Colonel Charles Newman]

The plan had two halves. First, a ship would ram the outer caisson and blow it up with a delayed charge, so that the gate was gone for good. Second, commando demolition teams would land around the dock and wreck the pumping station, the machinery for winding the caissons, bridges and lock gates, so that even a damaged dock could not be brought back quickly.

[S15 · G6 · map: the high spring tide lifting the mud flats]

There was one way in that might work. On a high spring tide, at the end of March, there would be just enough water over the flats for a light ship to cross them, away from the main channel and the worst of the guns. A heavy warship could not do it. A light, old one, lightened even further, just might.

[CHAPTER CARD: Making a Destroyer into a Bomb]

[S16 · NHHC photograph of USS Buchanan (DD-131)]

The ship they chose had been launched in America at the end of the First World War. She was USS Buchanan, one of the long four-funnelled destroyers built for the United States Navy around 1919. In September 1940, when Britain was desperate for escorts, the United States traded fifty of these old destroyers to Britain in return for bases. Buchanan became HMS Campbeltown.

[S17 · IWM Admiralty photograph of HMS Campbeltown in British service]

By early 1942 she was an elderly, expendable convoy escort, and that was exactly why she was picked. At Devonport, she was transformed. Two of her four funnels were taken away, and the remaining two were cut at a slant, so that in the dark her outline looked like one of the German torpedo boats that used the Loire.

[S18 · G7 · cutaway: Campbeltown's bow, the charge behind the forward gun, the concrete]

Most of her heavier gear was stripped out to make her ride high enough to cross the flats. Armour plating was added around the bridge and along the deck, to give the men on deck a little cover. And in her bow, just behind where the forward gun had been, the dockyard built the charge.

[S19 · G7 · cutaway detail: twenty-four depth charges in a steel tank, encased in concrete]

It was made of twenty-four Mark Seven depth charges, holding about four and a quarter tons of the explosive amatol, sealed inside a steel tank and then set in concrete. The idea was simple and brutal. When Campbeltown hit the caisson, her bow would crumple, but the charge would sit some way back from the stem, roughly above the gate itself. Hours later, it would go off.

[S20 · G7 · fuse diagram: delay fuses, time window]

The charge was fired by delay fuses, chemical timers that were set during the run in and could not be switched off once started. Their timing was chosen so that the explosion would come long after the raiders had gone. The Germans, if they boarded, would find no ticking clock, only concrete.

[S20a · G7 · cutaway: armour around the bridge, commandos lying on the deck]

About eighty commandos were to travel aboard Campbeltown herself, lying flat on her deck behind the new armour plates until the moment she struck. Their targets were the ones nearest the gate: the pumping station and the winding machinery. If Campbeltown reached the caisson, they would be the first men ashore, and the closest to the charge in her bow.

[S21 · IWM portrait of Lieutenant-Commander Stephen Beattie]

Her captain was Lieutenant-Commander Stephen Beattie. He had to drive a disguised, stripped-out destroyer up a defended river at night, at full speed, and hit a gate that was invisible until the last minute.

[CHAPTER CARD: Eighteen Small Boats]

[S22 · IWM Admiralty photograph of a Fairmile B motor launch]

Campbeltown would not go alone. With her went sixteen Fairmile B motor launches. They were wooden boats about a hundred and twelve feet long, built for coastal patrol, and they would carry most of the commandos. Their petrol tanks sat above the waterline with little protection. Extra fuel tanks were fitted on deck so they could make the long voyage there and back.

[S23 · IWM Admiralty photograph of MGB 314]

Ryder would command from Motor Gun Boat 314. A motor torpedo boat, MTB 74, came with a special job: to fire delayed-action torpedoes at the gates of the old entrance to the harbour basin if Campbeltown failed. Two escort destroyers, HMS Atherstone and HMS Tynedale, would see the force across the Bay of Biscay and wait offshore.

[S24 · IWM H series photograph of No. 2 Commando training]

In all, just over six hundred men sailed: around two hundred and sixty-five commandos and about three hundred and fifty sailors. The demolition teams had trained for weeks in British docks, at Cardiff and Southampton, where the King George the Fifth dry dock was laid out much like the Normandie dock. Many men practised their tasks blindfolded, until they could find their targets in darkness.

[S25 · G8 · map: Falmouth to St Nazaire, the wide loop out into the Bay of Biscay]

On the afternoon of 26 March 1942 the force sailed from Falmouth in Cornwall. It took a long loop out into the Bay of Biscay, posing as an anti-submarine sweep, to avoid looking as if it was heading for the French coast. On the morning of the 27th, the escorts met a German U-boat on the surface and attacked it. It dived. Nobody yet knew whether it had reported the force. In fact, its sighting report described the British ships as heading west, away from France.

[S26 · G8 · map: the force turning in toward the Loire at dusk]

That evening the force turned east toward the Loire. At around ten that night, the submarine HMS Sturgeon, positioned offshore as a navigation beacon, showed a light to guide them in. The small boats formed up in two columns behind Campbeltown and MGB 314. The escort destroyers turned away to wait.

[CHAPTER CARD: Up the Loire]

[S27 · G6 · map animation: the force crossing the flats, minute by minute]

As the force approached the estuary, the Royal Air Force began a diversionary raid over St Nazaire, meant to draw the Germans' eyes upward. Low cloud and strict orders not to bomb blind, for fear of hitting French civilians, meant few bombs fell. Instead, the bombers circling overhead without attacking made the German commanders suspicious. The defences were brought to a high state of alert.

[S28 · G6 · map: Campbeltown touching the mud and coming free]

Campbeltown crossed the flats on the rising tide. Twice her keel touched the mud, and twice she slid over it. By about half past one in the morning of 28 March, the force was a couple of miles from the dock and undetected, already well inside a river lined with guns.

[S29 · G9 · graphic: the signal exchange, German challenge and reply]

Then a searchlight came on, and more, until the boats were lit up on the water. German signal stations challenged the ships. Ryder's force had prepared for this. A signaller, Leading Signalman Pike, answered from MGB 314 using German signalling procedure, asking the shore to wait, and claiming the ships were German vessels damaged by the enemy. For a few precious minutes, some of the guns held their fire. Every minute meant another few hundred yards closer to the gate.

[S30 · G9 · graphic: German ensign lowered, White Ensign raised]

Then the defences opened up. On Campbeltown, the German flag came down and the White Ensign went up. Every gun in the force replied. The old destroyer was hit again and again, but she kept her speed. Beattie, steering by the shapes he could pick out on shore, lined her up on the caisson.

[S31 · G10 · diagram: Campbeltown's approach, impact on the outer caisson]

At about 1.34 in the morning, four minutes behind the planned time, Campbeltown smashed through the anti-torpedo net in front of the gate and drove her bow into the outer caisson of the Normandie dock. She hit at around eighteen to twenty knots. Her bow crumpled back for more than thirty feet, and her forecastle rode up onto the top of the gate. The charge sat almost exactly where the planners had wanted it.

[S32 · G10 · diagram: the wedged ship, charge above the caisson]

Beattie is said to have remarked that they had arrived, four minutes late. His crew set the scuttling charges to sink the stern so the ship could not be towed away, and the commandos aboard her went over the bow onto the dockside.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Fight for the Dock]

[S33 · G11 · map: commando demolition targets around the dock]

The commandos from Campbeltown split into their teams and ran for their targets. One party forced its way into the pumping station, went down to the pumps deep below ground, and wrecked them with charges. Other teams blew up the machinery houses that wound the caissons in and out. Demolition parties placed charges on bridges and gates, while covering parties fought off the German gunners.

[S34 · G11 · map: the motor launches' intended landing places, the Old Mole]

For the motor launches, the night went much worse. Wooden boats with petrol on deck were caught in the searchlights at close range, and many caught fire. Several were sunk before they could land their commandos at all. Only a few managed to put men ashore at the Old Mole or the old entrance. Many of the men who reached the shore found that their boats, and their way home, had gone.

[S35 · IWM portrait of Able Seaman William Savage]

On MGB 314, Able Seaman William Savage stood at his exposed gun on the foredeck, firing at the German positions until he was killed. On ML 306, on the way out, Sergeant Thomas Durrant of No. 1 Commando kept firing his Lewis gun at a German ship though he was badly wounded many times, until the launch was captured. Both were awarded the Victoria Cross after their deaths.

[S36 · G11 · map: MTB 74 fires its torpedoes into the old entrance gate]

MTB 74 had not been needed for the caisson, so Ryder sent her to her backup target. She fired her two delayed-action torpedoes into the outer gate of the old entrance to the basin, where they sank and lay on the bottom, timed to go off later.

[S37 · G12 · map: Newman's party fighting into the old town]

Newman landed with his headquarters party and directed the fight around the dock. With most of the boats gone, the commandos had no way off. Newman gathered the survivors and decided to break out through the town, hoping to reach open country and make for Spain. They fought their way across the bridges and into the streets of St Nazaire, until, short of ammunition and with many wounded, most were surrounded and captured at dawn.

[S38 · G8 · map: the surviving boats heading out to sea]

Ryder stayed in the river until it was clear that nothing more could be done, then took MGB 314 back out to sea under fire. Of the eighteen small craft that had gone in, only a handful came back to Britain. Of more than six hundred men, about two hundred and twenty-eight returned home. One hundred and sixty-nine were killed, and more than two hundred became prisoners. Five commandos escaped on foot across France to Spain and got back to Britain.

[CHAPTER CARD: Late in the Morning]

[S39 · G10 · diagram: the wedged ship at daybreak, Germans aboard]

At daybreak on 28 March, Campbeltown was still sitting on the caisson. The fuses had not fired. German officers and men went aboard to look at the British wreck. To them it seemed the raid had failed: the ship had not exploded, and the gate was still there.

[S40 · IWM photograph of captured raiders (if Crown; otherwise G13)]

Beattie, captured, was being questioned by a German officer, who told him that the British had not understood how strong the gate was. Some time later that morning, the charge went off. It lifted Campbeltown's bow, destroyed the outer caisson, and drove the ship into the dock. Water rushed in. Many of the Germans who had gone aboard or gathered around her were killed.

[S41 · G13 · diagram: the dock flooded, caisson gone]

Two days later, on 30 March, MTB 74's torpedoes exploded at the old entrance gate. Panic broke out in the docks, and in the confusion German troops fired on their own workers and on French civilians. The raid had cost the town of St Nazaire dearly too.

[S42 · RAF reconnaissance photograph of the damaged dock after the raid]

RAF reconnaissance flights over the following days showed the result. The outer caisson had gone. The pumping station was wrecked. The Normandie dock could no longer be used, and it stayed out of use for the rest of the war. It was not working again until after 1945.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Battleship That Never Came]

[S42a · G13 · diagram: what the raid wrecked, item by item]

The teams ashore had done more than the planners had dared to expect. Pumps, winding gear and gates had all been hit. Even if German engineers had found a way to replace the caisson, the dock could not have been emptied or worked without new machinery that would take years to build. The Germans did not try to repair it during the war. The dock that had been built for the largest liner in the world sat flooded and useless, a few hundred yards from the U-boat pens.

[S43 · IWM photograph of survivors landing in Britain]

In Britain, at first, the raid was announced as a success without much detail. As reports came in from the returning boats, and later from prisoners' letters, the scale of what had happened became clear.

[S44 · G14 · board of awards: five Victoria Crosses]

Eighty-nine decorations were awarded for the night. Five were the Victoria Cross: to Commander Ryder, Lieutenant-Colonel Newman and Lieutenant-Commander Beattie, and, after their deaths, to Able Seaman Savage and Sergeant Durrant. Newman's and Durrant's awards were only announced after the war, once the full story could be told by the men who had been prisoners.

[S45 · RAF reconnaissance photograph of Tirpitz in Norway]

And Tirpitz? She never came out into the Atlantic. She spent the rest of her life in Norwegian waters, attacked again and again by midget submarines and aircraft, a threat that tied down British ships for years. In November 1944, RAF Lancasters sank her near Tromsø with heavy bombs.

[S46 · G3 · map: Norway, the Atlantic, and the one dock now marked closed]

The raid alone did not keep her in Norway. Fuel shortages, Hitler's caution after the loss of Bismarck, and the fear of British carriers all played a part. But the planners had set out to take away the one place in the Atlantic where she could be mended. They took it away, and it stayed gone.

[S47 · G15 · diagram: the old destroyer, the dock, the numbers on one card]

That was the achievement of Operation Chariot. A light American destroyer from 1919, cut down to look like a German boat and filled with concrete and explosive, did what bombs could not. She crossed the mud on a spring tide, ran the guns of the Loire, and put her bow exactly where the planners wanted it. Then, late in the morning, when the Germans thought it was over, she closed Germany's only Atlantic dry dock big enough for Tirpitz.
