# Why America's Torpedoes Failed Even When They Hit

## 1. The perfect hit

A torpedo can miss because a captain gets the angle wrong. America's submariners faced something worse. Their torpedoes could strike a ship squarely and still fail to explode. The shot that looked best through the periscope could be the very shot most likely to defeat the firing mechanism. And that was only one fault. Before the Navy could prove it, its crews had to work through two others that helped keep it hidden.

In July 1943, the submarine Tinosa found a Japanese oiler. Her opening attacks damaged the ship, leaving a target that should have been much easier to finish. More torpedoes followed. According to the Navy's official history of its Bureau of Ordnance, Tinosa fired fifteen in all and claimed twelve hits. Yet the oiler remained afloat. Only the first two spreads had produced explosions. A target had survived a succession of attacks that should have settled the matter.

Tinosa returned to Pearl Harbor with one torpedo saved for examination. Here was a chance to take a suspected failure out of the confused world of battle and into a workshop. But inspection brought another frustration. The remaining weapon appeared to be in good condition. Looking at the mechanism on a bench was not enough. The fault depended on what happened when a torpedo moving at speed met the side of a ship.

The answer would involve firing weapons into a cliff and dropping inert warheads onto steel plates. These were blunt experiments for a subtle problem. The Navy had built a weapon that could travel underwater, hold a course and sense a ship without touching it. Yet some of the evidence needed to make it work came from a net, a hard surface and a willingness to question the instruments that said everything was correct.

This was the Mark Fourteen torpedo, carried by American fleet submarines. Its story is often reduced to bad weapons and stubborn officials. There was stubbornness, and there were serious failures. But the engineering story is more revealing. A depth error concealed an unreliable magnetic device. Removing that device exposed a contact mechanism that could jam at the moment of impact. Each apparent solution changed what the men could see, until the final fault became impossible to ignore.

## 2. A weapon tested for the wrong world

[CHAPTER CARD: A weapon tested for the wrong world]

The Mark Fourteen entered the war with advantages over the older Mark Ten. It was longer and faster, with greater reach. A submarine crew could select a fast run of forty-six knots for a range of four thousand five hundred yards, or a slower setting that carried it farther. Those numbers described a capable weapon. They did not establish that its explosive charge would burst at the right place, or burst at all.

The weapon had to solve several problems in sequence. It had to leave the tube, settle onto its course, reach the depth selected by the crew and arrive near a moving target. Then its exploder had to initiate the charge. An exploder was the mechanism that told the warhead when to burst. Good performance in the first part of that journey could not compensate for failure in the last. A straight wake was not proof of a successful attack.

American designers wanted more than a collision against the side of a ship. Their magnetic influence system was intended to respond to the disturbance a ship made in the Earth's magnetic field. That offered a way to set off a charge beneath the hull. It was an ambitious attempt to attack below the protective features built into warships. The idea was not inherently foolish. The problem was proving that this particular mechanism would behave reliably outside the conditions of its tests.

Money and the desire to recover expensive weapons shaped those tests. An exercise torpedo could carry a head designed to help it float after the run, so it could be brought back and used again. A torpedo fired with its real explosive charge could not be recovered after a successful detonation. Recoverable exercises therefore made economic sense. But a test could be economical and repeatable while still leaving out the conditions that mattered most in battle.

As warheads changed, the exercise heads stopped matching them closely enough. More explosive weight altered the balance and running behaviour of the weapon. The official Bureau history describes test conditions drifting away from those of a real shot. Meanwhile, practice torpedoes were deliberately set to pass underneath target ships, protecting both ship and weapon. A run below a ship could count as an acceptable exercise without anyone establishing exactly how far below the ship it had gone.

The comparison between an exercise head and a warhead is worth keeping in view. Both could fit the same torpedo, yet changing the mass at its nose changed the object travelling through the water. A recovered exercise weapon was not automatically evidence for the performance of a weapon carrying a different load. The tests had to represent the configuration sent into battle. Otherwise, care and precision could be applied to measuring something that the fleet was not actually firing.

There was also an instrument intended to settle that question. The depth and roll recorder measured pressure and produced a record of the run. Each service torpedo came with a log of its proving performance. That looked like individual evidence rather than guesswork. Yet the recorder itself was exposed to pressure that did not correctly represent the torpedo's true depth. The system used to check the weapon could agree with the weapon and still be wrong.

This was the first trap. Two indications appeared to support each other, but both were affected by how the equipment had been installed. The depth mechanism and its measuring instrument shared a misleading picture of the run. A neat set of records could therefore reinforce confidence in a bad result. The lesson was not that measurement was useless. It was that a measurement had to be checked against something independent of the mechanism being measured.

## 3. The net that challenged the instruments

[CHAPTER CARD: The net that challenged the instruments]

War supplied the challenge. Captains reported attacks that should have hit and had not. There were genuine uncertainties in aiming at a moving ship, estimating its speed and judging the result through a periscope. That made an individual miss easy to explain away. Repeated complaints were harder to dismiss. Rear Admiral Charles Lockwood, commanding submarines in the Southwest Pacific in 1942, backed tests that compared the torpedo's actual path with its selected depth.

Torpedoes were fired into nets. The torn opening left a physical trace of where each weapon had passed. Instead of asking the torpedo's own recorder whether it had run correctly, the test put an obstacle in its path and measured the result. Nets were imperfect instruments too. Currents could move them and distort their shape. Their evidence needed care. But the tests gave investigators a way to challenge the confidence built into the earlier records.

The Bureau eventually confirmed the problem. On the first of August 1942, it informed the service that Mark Fourteens fired from submerged tubes ran, on average, ten feet deeper than the selected setting. Ten feet is about three metres. It was enough to change a shot aimed at a hull into one passing beneath it. The captain could have judged the target's course correctly and still missed because the weapon was not going where he had told it to go.

The cause was not simply one heavy component. The official history describes a depth-control arrangement moved towards the rear of the torpedo and mounted at an angle, a recording instrument affected by local pressure, and exercise heads that no longer represented the warhead properly. These faults belonged to the whole testing and control system. Treating the matter as nothing more than a mistaken setting by the crew could never explain what was actually happening.

Once the error was acknowledged, crews could allow for it when setting a shot while the underlying causes were investigated. That did not make the torpedo perfect, but it removed a major obstacle. More weapons now reached the depth at which they were meant to meet a target. This should have been a decisive improvement. Instead, it brought another kind of failure into clearer view. Getting closer to the ship did not guarantee an effective explosion.

The Mark Six exploder was supposed to respond to magnetic influence. If a torpedo passed far too deep, it might fail to produce the intended result at all. Bring it nearer the hull, and the device could respond too soon. The first fault had concealed part of the second. Correcting the depth made the magnetic mechanism's behaviour more visible, and the crew's problem changed from a torpedo that passed beneath the target to one that burst before reaching the proper position.

The Navy was not starting again with a completely new weapon. It was discovering how the parts of an existing weapon affected each other. A report of no explosion might point towards depth, the magnetic device or the contact mechanism. An explosion might still occur in the wrong place. Without knowing which part of the sequence had failed, a patrol report could support several competing explanations. The next investigation had to separate those explanations rather than count all failures together.

## 4. The secret beneath the hull

[CHAPTER CARD: The secret beneath the hull]

The magnetic exploder had a long history. The Bureau's account places the start of the project in 1922. It records a successful destructive trial against a submarine hulk in 1926. That result showed the principle could work. It did not establish reliability across all the conditions a wartime fleet would encounter. The official history says no further destructive test with a magnetic influence exploder followed during its pre-war development. Testing continued, but the most convincing kind of test had become exceptional.

The same account describes trials concentrated in a limited magnetic environment and unable to reproduce all the effects of storage, handling and different targets. A mechanism expected to respond to a ship's influence was going to encounter more than one ship, in more than one part of the world. Success at a proving ground was evidence of possibility. It was not yet evidence that a device could be issued widely and trusted under the varied conditions of a Pacific patrol.

Secrecy made the gap between designer and user wider. The Navy wanted to prevent an enemy developing countermeasures. Knowledge of the device was therefore tightly restricted, including among people who would eventually operate and maintain it. In 1941, a training programme and a limited issue to the fleet widened that knowledge. According to the Bureau history, this came too late to obtain the amount of practical testing and criticism that an earlier introduction could have supplied.

Once war made the device familiar, criticism arrived quickly. Corrected depth settings helped expose premature explosions. A burst seen through a periscope could be mistaken for a hit, even when it occurred before the weapon reached the hull. Observers and analysts were trying to interpret an event they could not inspect afterwards. The Bureau and the submariners disagreed about how common these failures were. The disagreement concerned the evidence as well as the machinery.

There were attempts to improve the mechanism, its adjustments and the conditions under which crews used it. But every extra restriction had to make sense in an attack. A submarine captain could not arrange an enemy ship's course, the magnetic conditions and the moment of firing as neatly as a test programme could. A weapon whose success depended on a narrow set of circumstances might look promising to its designers while remaining deeply unsatisfactory to the men trying to use it.

By the summer of 1943, Nimitz's Pacific command had ordered the magnetic feature disabled. That was a practical decision about the weapon in service, not a declaration that magnetic influence could never work. Lockwood wanted a torpedo his captains could trust. With the magnetic feature out of the sequence, a weapon would have to hit its target and rely on the contact mechanism. Removing one source of uncertainty should also make the remaining failures easier to diagnose.

The decision was not immediately universal. In the Southwest Pacific, Rear Admiral Ralph Christie continued to support use of the magnetic feature. The Bureau history says his command kept it until the spring of 1944. Christie had experience in torpedo design and believed the system still had potential. He must not be placed alongside Lockwood as though they made the same choice together. The difference between their commands is one reason this story has no single clean date on which everything was fixed.

## 5. Why a square hit could fail

[CHAPTER CARD: Why a square hit could fail]

Then came Tinosa's experience with the oiler. Here, the story returned to the simplest expectation of all: a torpedo that struck a ship should explode. The repeated failures made that expectation untenable. Saving one weapon for inspection was useful, but an intact torpedo could not reproduce the violence of hitting a hull. The investigation needed to subject the contact mechanism to the kind of shock it was actually receiving, rather than merely establish that its parts looked sound.

The Mark Six's firing pin moved across the torpedo's axis under the force of a spring. During a severe impact, friction could stop that movement or reduce it enough to prevent proper firing. The guides could also be distorted or displaced. The crucial point is that a harder impact did not necessarily improve the chance of detonation. It could obstruct the mechanism at precisely the moment that the mechanism was supposed to act.

A glancing hit could therefore succeed where a square hit failed. The effect was especially bitter for crews taught to secure a good firing position. Better geometry against the target could produce a harsher shock inside the exploder. The torpedo's performance and the firing mechanism's design were not properly matched. Earlier strengthening of the firing spring had helped, but subsequent increases in torpedo speed had undermined that improvement. The complete weapon needed testing again as its characteristics changed.

At Pearl Harbor, Lockwood's investigators fired into a cliff and dropped inert-loaded warheads onto steel plates. The official history records drops from ninety feet. These tests were not a way to fire live explosives casually around a base. They used inert-loaded heads to reproduce impact and examine the firing mechanism. A controlled failure could be recovered and studied. It was the opposite of a dud disappearing below a ship in the middle of a patrol.

The Bureau was investigating too. It fired torpedoes at armour plates suspended in Chesapeake Bay. Its investigators and the Pearl Harbor team reached the same central conclusion: the impact produced more resistance than the firing pin could overcome. This matters because the repair was not simply a story of one inspired commander solving a problem while everyone ashore stood still. The disagreement had delayed recognition, but work at both ends helped establish the mechanical cause.

Their immediate solutions differed. The Bureau favoured a stronger firing spring. The Pearl Harbor investigators lightened the firing pin. The Bureau then endorsed the lighter pin and combined it with a stronger spring. The aim was to make the pin complete its movement despite the forces created by impact. There was no need to invent a new reason for a warhead to explode. The existing mechanism needed to perform its intended job under the conditions the rest of the torpedo imposed.

Now the sequence could be understood. Excessive depth had carried weapons away from their intended path. The magnetic feature could trigger them before the right moment. A contact mechanism could fail when a torpedo actually struck. These were separate faults with interacting symptoms. A solution to one did not prove that the other two were gone. That is why a single successful trial, or a handful of successful attacks, could never settle the argument about the whole weapon.

## 6. What changed after the argument

[CHAPTER CARD: What changed after the argument]

The repair took time to become dependable service performance. The Naval Undersea Museum places satisfactory operation in the autumn of 1943. The Bureau's history describes performance as almost uniformly good by January 1944. Those descriptions refer to different stages in a process, and neither supports the idea that every weapon failed until one particular day and then every weapon worked. The practical change was that the principal defects had been identified and effective remedies were being applied.

Even the Bureau's account admits how badly the testing had fallen short. It records Admiral Blandy's criticism of work that could and should have been done before the war, and his instruction that a service torpedo device should not become standard until it had been tested under conditions as close as possible to battle. That is a more demanding test than showing that a component works on a bench or that a recoverable exercise weapon completes a tidy run.

The figures reaching headquarters had their own weaknesses. The official history acknowledges that reports could look reassuring without matching the truth of performance at sea. A captain judging a distant burst, a crew hearing an impact and an analyst reading a patrol report did not possess the same evidence. Some attacks succeeded; others failed for more than one possible reason. Good analysis required more than defending the average. It required finding out what those reports could and could not establish.

The official history is also the Bureau telling its own story. Its authors acknowledge that they wrote with pride in the organisation, and their defence of its work should not be mistaken for a neutral view from nowhere. Yet that makes its admissions particularly useful. It identifies poor testing, a misleading recorder and the mechanical weakness of the firing pin within the institution's own account. The case does not depend solely on the anger of captains coming back from disappointing patrols.

The same caution applies to the story now. It would be wrong to attribute every lost submarine, every escaped ship or the entire course of the Pacific war to the Mark Fourteen's defects. Such a total cannot be recovered from these tests. What the evidence does establish is serious and sufficient: crews went into combat with weapons whose depth and firing behaviour had not been adequately proved, and important opportunities were lost while the failures were disputed and corrected.

Nor was the Mark Fourteen simply thrown away as a hopeless design. Correcting its faults turned it into a useful weapon, and versions remained in submarine service after the war. The costly part was learning under combat conditions what realistic testing should have exposed earlier. The Navy already had skilled designers, experienced crews and elaborate instruments. What it lacked was a reliable connection between what those instruments appeared to prove and what the weapon actually did.

Tinosa's surviving torpedo looked sound when it came home. That is where this story's central problem lies. A weapon can be properly assembled, carefully inspected and convincingly documented, yet fail in the one condition for which it exists. The net challenged the depth recorder. Impact tests challenged the firing mechanism. The men did not make the torpedo dependable by trusting it more. They made it dependable by finding ways to show exactly where that trust had been misplaced.
