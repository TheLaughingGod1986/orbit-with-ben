## Cold open (0:00)

[S01] In the summer of 1945, the United States had two secret projects that cost more than any others in the war. [C01] One built the atomic bomb. It cost about one point nine billion dollars. [C02] The other built the aeroplane that would carry it. That one cost about three billion. [C03]

[S02] The most expensive weapon America made in the Second World War was not the bomb. It was a silver bomber called the B-29 Superfortress. And for most of its short war, it did not work the way it was meant to.

[S03] Its second prototype caught fire and fell on a meat-packing plant in Seattle. [C04] Its engines burned so often that crews joked about them. [C05] An entire fleet of new aircraft sat broken on the snow in Kansas, weeks before it was due at war. [C06] And when it finally reached Japan, the wind at its cruising height was blowing at well over a hundred miles an hour, faster than any forecaster had planned for. [C07]

[S04] This is the story of how the United States ordered more than a thousand bombers before one had flown, why that gamble cost more than the Manhattan Project, and what it got for the money.

## Chapter 1: A bomber for a war that had not started (1939–1942)

[S05] To understand the B-29, start with a map. In 1939, America was not at war. But the head of the Army Air Corps, General Henry Arnold, known as Hap, was looking at distances. [C08]

[S06] The bombers he had, like the B-17 Flying Fortress, were built to defend the American coast and reach targets a few hundred miles away. If war came in the Pacific, the distances were in the thousands. Arnold wanted a bomber that could carry a ton of bombs more than five thousand miles. [C09]

[S07] In early 1940, the Air Corps sent out its request for what it called a very long range bomber. [C09] Boeing had already been working on the idea, on its own money, for some time. Its answer was a design it called Model 345. [C10]

[S07a] Boeing was not the only company in the running. Lockheed, Douglas and Consolidated all sent in designs. [C41] The Army liked Boeing's best, but it was so unsure that any of them would work that it also paid Consolidated to build a rival, the B-32, as insurance in case the B-29 failed. [C41] The B-32 turned out to have even more trouble than the B-29, and only a handful ever saw combat. [C41]

[S08] On paper it was extraordinary. It was far bigger than the B-17, with a wingspan of a hundred and forty-one feet. [C11] It had a long, thin wing built for efficient cruising at height. And it would fly higher than almost anything else in the sky.

[S09] Flying high is where the trouble begins. At thirty thousand feet the air is thin and bitterly cold. In a B-17, the crew wore oxygen masks and electrically heated suits, and on long missions frostbite was a real danger. [C12] For a flight of twelve or fifteen hours, that was not going to work.

[S10] So Boeing's design was pressurised. The crew would sit in sealed compartments, breathing air kept at a lower altitude, warm enough to work in ordinary flying clothes. [C13] It was the first bomber to go into large-scale service built that way. [C13]

[S11] But a bomber has a problem that an airliner does not. Its middle is a pair of enormous bomb bays, and bomb bays have to open in flight. You cannot pressurise a space with doors that swing open at thirty thousand feet.

[S12] Boeing's answer was a tunnel. The pilots, bombardier and navigator sat in a pressurised nose. The gunners sat in a pressurised section behind the wing. And joining the two, laid over the top of the bomb bays, was a padded tube just wide enough for a man to crawl through on his hands and knees. [C14] The tail gunner had his own small sealed compartment at the very back, and could only get to it and from it when the aircraft was unpressurised. [C14]

[S13] Then there were the guns. With a crew sealed inside, nobody could stand at an open window with a machine gun. So the B-29's guns sat in turrets on the outside, aimed by remote control. [C15]

[S14] The gunners sat at sighting stations under clear blisters. When one of them tracked an enemy fighter, an analogue computer built by General Electric did the arithmetic. It worked out how far ahead of the target to aim, allowed for the bomber's own speed, the height, and the fact that the gunner was sitting several feet away from the gun he was firing. [C15] It also let one gunner take over a turret from another. [C15] Nothing like it had gone to war in a bomber before.

[S15] All of this was new. The pressure cabin was new. The fire control was new. And the engines, which we will come to, were barely out of development.

[S16] A normal programme would build a prototype, test it for a year or two, fix what was wrong, and then order the bomber in numbers. The United States did not have that time.

[S17] In May 1941, more than a year before the first prototype flew, the Army Air Forces ordered two hundred and fifty B-29s. [C16] After Pearl Harbor, the orders kept growing. By the time the first XB-29 left the ground in September 1942, the Army had ordered more than sixteen hundred. [C16]

[S18] It was a gamble without much precedent. And it meant building the factories at the same time as the aeroplane. Boeing would build B-29s at a new plant in Wichita, Kansas, and at Renton, near Seattle. Bell would build them in Marietta, Georgia, and Martin in Omaha, Nebraska. [C17] Thousands of subcontractors fed parts into all four. [C17]

[S19] That is where much of the three billion dollars went. Not into the bombers alone, but into building an industry from nothing to make them.

[S19a] Wichita shows the scale. Before the war, the Boeing plant there built small training biplanes. [C42] By 1944 it was turning out B-29s, and the town's population had grown so fast that whole new housing estates were built for the workers. [C42] Many of the people riveting the bombers together had never worked in a factory before, and a great many of them were women. [C42]

[S19b] Every B-29 was made of tens of thousands of parts, drawn on paper by hand, and every change to one part meant new drawings sent out to four factories and their suppliers. In that world, a change in the design was not a quick fix. It was weeks of work for thousands of people.

## Chapter 2: The engine that caught fire (1942–1943)

[S20] Every one of those B-29s needed four engines, and the engine was the Wright R-3350 Duplex Cyclone. [C18]

[S21] It was a huge radial engine, eighteen cylinders arranged in two rows, rated at two thousand two hundred horsepower, with turbo-superchargers to keep it breathing at height. [C18] No engine that powerful had ever been built in such numbers. And it was rushed.

[S22] The B-29's long wing and the engine's tightly packed cylinders made cooling hard. The rear row of cylinders sat behind the front row and got less cooling air. On a long climb with a heavy load, the engines ran hot. Valves failed. And sometimes an engine caught fire. [C19]

[S23] An engine fire in a B-29 was especially dangerous. To save weight, parts of the engine were made of magnesium alloy. Magnesium is light and strong, but once it is hot enough to burn, it burns fiercely, and water does not put it out. A fire that reached those parts could burn through the wing spar in a few minutes. [C19]

[S24] The man trying to tame this was Boeing's chief test pilot, Edmund Allen, known as Eddie. He was one of the most respected test pilots in America, and he had flown the first XB-29 on the twenty-first of September, 1942. [C20]

[S25] The early test flights were plagued by engine trouble. The first two prototypes had engines changed again and again. [C20]

[S26] On the eighteenth of February, 1943, Allen took off from Boeing Field in the second prototype. A few minutes into the flight, an engine caught fire. He turned back for the field. [C04]

[S27] The fire spread into the wing. The bomber came down short of the runway and struck the Frye meat-packing plant, a few miles south of downtown Seattle. [C04] Everyone on board was killed: Allen and ten of his crew. So were around twenty people on the ground, most of them workers in the plant, and a firefighter. [C04]

[S28] It was one of the worst moments of the programme. The Army ordered investigations into the engine, and into how Boeing tested the aircraft. [C21] Senator Harry Truman's committee on war spending looked into the R-3350, and was sharply critical of how it was being built. [C21]

[S29] But the war did not stop. Production did not stop. The fix was made in parallel. Engineers redesigned the cooling, the exhaust, and the way fuel was mixed, and every change had to be fed into aircraft that were already coming down the lines. [C22]

[S30] And that is the hidden cost of ordering a weapon off the drawing board. Every mistake you find in testing has to be corrected on hundreds of aircraft that already exist.

## Chapter 3: The Battle of Kansas (1944)

[S31] By early 1944, the plan was to send the B-29 to war that spring. The bombers would go first to India and then to bases in China, and from there strike Japan. The operation was code-named Matterhorn. [C23]

[S32] The aircraft were coming off the lines. But when the Army looked at them at the airfields in Kansas, almost none were ready to fight. [C06]

[S33] Because the design had kept changing while production went on, new B-29s rolled out of the factory needing modification before they could go to war. They went to modification centres, then to the training bases. And there, in the winter of 1944, they piled up. [C06]

[S34] General Arnold visited in March. He had promised President Roosevelt the bombers would be in action. He found that not a single one was ready to deploy. [C06]

[S35] What followed became known as the Battle of Kansas. Arnold sent a senior officer, General Bennett Meyers, to take charge. [C06] Boeing pulled hundreds of workers from its plants in Wichita and Seattle and sent them to the bases at Salina, Pratt, Great Bend and Walker. [C06]

[S36] There were not enough hangars for aircraft this big. So much of the work was done outside, on open ramps, in snow and freezing wind. Mechanics worked in shifts around the clock, changing engines, rewiring gun systems, and fitting the latest modifications by hand. [C06]

[S37] It worked, just. In the space of about five weeks, around a hundred and fifty B-29s were made ready, and began the long flight east to India. [C06]

[S38] But Operation Matterhorn had its own impossible arithmetic.

[S39] The forward bases were around Chengdu, in western China. Nothing could reach them by sea or by road in any useful amount. Every gallon of fuel, every bomb and every spare engine had to be flown in from India, over the Himalayas, a route the pilots called the Hump. [C23]

[S40] And the main thing flying that fuel over the Hump was the B-29 itself. Bombers were stripped down and used as tankers. For each mission the bombers flew from China, they first had to fly the Hump several times to stockpile the fuel for it. [C24]

[S41] On the fifteenth of June, 1944, B-29s from China struck the steelworks at Yawata, in southern Japan. It was the first raid on the Japanese home islands since the Doolittle raid two years before. [C25] But the damage was slight. And the effort of supplying China meant raids could be flown only every few weeks. [C24]

[S41a] The first combat mission had come ten days earlier, on the fifth of June, against railway yards at Bangkok, flown from India. [C25] It was a long, hard flight. Several bombers were lost to accidents and mechanical trouble rather than to the enemy, and the bombing was scattered. [C25] It was a pattern that would repeat for months.

[S42] Matterhorn showed the B-29 could reach Japan. It also showed that China was the wrong place to do it from.

## Chapter 4: The wind over Tokyo (1944–1945)

[S43] The answer was a chain of islands in the western Pacific: the Marianas. [C26]

[S44] In the summer of 1944, American forces captured Saipan, Tinian and Guam. [C26] From there, Tokyo was around one thousand five hundred miles away. Within B-29 range, with fuel and bombs coming in by ship. [C26]

[S45] Navy construction battalions, the Seabees, set to work almost at once. On Tinian, they built North Field, with four runways each eight and a half thousand feet long, carved and levelled from coral. [C27] By 1945 it was one of the busiest airfields in the world. [C27]

[S46] On the twenty-fourth of November, 1944, B-29s from Saipan flew to Tokyo for the first time. [C28] The plan for these raids was the plan the bomber had been designed for: high-altitude, daylight, precision bombing of aircraft factories, from around thirty thousand feet. [C28]

[S47] And at thirty thousand feet over Japan, the crews found something nobody had planned for.

[S48] The wind. At that height above Japan, in winter, a band of air was flowing from west to east at extraordinary speed, often well over a hundred miles an hour, and sometimes nearer two hundred. [C07] Today we call it the jet stream. In 1944, almost nothing was known about it in practice. [C07]

[S49] It wrecked the plan. A bomber flying downwind could pass over its target at a ground speed of more than four hundred miles an hour, far too fast for the bombsight to work properly. A bomber flying into the wind could almost hang in the air, an easy target for anti-aircraft guns, burning fuel it would need to get home. And crosswinds pushed the falling bombs far off their aiming points. [C07]

[S50] Add thick cloud over Japan for much of the winter, and the long climb to height with a full load that strained the engines, and the results were poor. [C29] Over the first months of raids from the Marianas, only a small fraction of bombs fell anywhere near their targets. [C29]

[S51] The most expensive aircraft programme of the war was, by its own measure, failing.

[S51a] The losses mounted too, and many of them were not caused by the enemy. A bomber damaged over Japan, or short of fuel after fighting the wind, had to fly fifteen hundred miles of open sea to get home. Many came down in the water. [C43]

[S51b] That was one of the reasons for one of the hardest-fought battles of the Pacific war. In February and March 1945, the Marines took the island of Iwo Jima, roughly halfway between the Marianas and Tokyo. [C43] Among the reasons for taking it was to give damaged B-29s somewhere to land. By the end of the war, B-29s had made more than two thousand emergency landings there. [C43]

## Chapter 5: LeMay's change, and the verdict (1945)

[S52] In January 1945, the command of the B-29s in the Marianas passed to General Curtis LeMay. [C30] He had made his name commanding bombers over Germany, and he looked at the B-29 problem as a set of hard numbers. [C30]

[S53] High altitude meant the jet stream, the cloud, and a long climb that wore out engines. So he proposed to give it up. [C31]

[S53a] It was a risky idea. Flying lower meant flying into the range of more anti-aircraft guns. LeMay's own staff were divided about it. [C31] But he judged that at night, Japan's defences were weaker than they were by day, and that the gain in accuracy and bomb load was worth the risk. [C31]

[S54] In March 1945, LeMay sent his bombers in at night, at between five and nine thousand feet. Below the worst of the wind. Below the cloud. [C31] Most of the defensive guns and their gunners were left behind to save weight, and the bombers carried incendiaries instead of high explosives. [C31] Without the long climb, the engines ran cooler, and each aircraft could carry far more. [C31]

[S55] The first of these raids, on Tokyo on the night of the ninth to the tenth of March, 1945, was the most destructive air raid in history. [C32] Around sixteen square miles of the city burned. [C32] Most estimates put the number of people killed at between eighty and a hundred thousand, most of them civilians. [C32]

[S56] Over the following months, the same method was turned on most of Japan's large cities. [C33] It was effective, by the measure the commanders used, and it remains one of the most debated decisions of the war. [C33]

[S57] And then, in August 1945, the two most expensive projects of the American war met in one aircraft.

[S58] On the sixth of August, a B-29 named Enola Gay dropped the first atomic bomb used in war, on Hiroshima. On the ninth, another B-29, Bockscar, dropped the second, on Nagasaki. [C34] Both aircraft were part of a small batch of B-29s specially modified for the job. [C34] No other aircraft the United States had could carry the bomb that far. [C34]

[S59] Japan announced its surrender on the fifteenth of August. [C35]

[S60] So was the B-29 worth three billion dollars?

[S61] By the end of the war, close to four thousand had been built. [C36] It had been rushed into service with an engine that was not ready, a fire-control system no one had used in combat, and a cabin design no bomber had used before.

[S62] Hundreds of crews were lost, many of them not to the enemy, but to engine failures and accidents. [C37] The early raids failed. And the method that finally worked was not the one it had been designed for.

[S62a] And the comparison in the title is not quite like for like. The Manhattan Project bought factories, reactors and laboratories, as well as two bombs used in war. The B-29 programme bought nearly four thousand aircraft, four factories, and the training of tens of thousands of aircrew and mechanics. [C02] [C03] But both were gambles of the same kind. Each began before anyone knew whether the thing could be made to work, and each paid for that uncertainty by building everything at once.

[S63] But the B-29 also left something behind. The pressurised cabin, the long efficient wing, and the remote-controlled guns all fed into the aircraft that came after it. Boeing turned the B-29 design into an airliner, the Stratocruiser, and into the military transports and tankers that served for years after the war. [C38] The B-50, a developed B-29, carried on as a front-line bomber. [C38]

[S64] Pressurised flight at high altitude, which seemed exotic in 1940, became ordinary. Today, millions of people cross oceans every day in sealed cabins, high above the weather, without thinking about it.

[S65] The B-29 is remembered as the bomber that ended the war. It might be better remembered as the gamble that showed what it really costs to build a new technology before you know it works: three billion dollars, an industry built from nothing, and the lives of the people who tested it.

[S66] Of the nearly four thousand built, only two B-29s still fly today. [C40]
