## Cold open (0:00)

[S01 · C01, C02, C03]
In the summer of 1945, the United States had two secret projects that cost more than any others in the war. One built the atomic bomb. It cost about one point nine billion dollars. The other built the aeroplane that would carry it. That one cost about three billion.

[S02 · C01, C03]
The most expensive weapon America made in the Second World War was not the atomic bomb. It was a silver bomber called the B-29 Superfortress. And for most of its short war, it did not work the way it was meant to.

[S03 · C04, C05, C06, C07]
Its second prototype caught fire in flight and fell on a meat-packing plant in Seattle, killing thirty-two people. Its eighteen-cylinder engines ran so hot that magnesium fires could burn through a main wing spar in ninety seconds. An entire fleet of new aircraft sat broken on the snow in Kansas, weeks before it was due at war. And when it finally reached Japan, the wind at its cruising height was blowing at between a hundred and fifty and two hundred miles an hour, faster than any forecaster had planned for.

[S04 · C16, C03]
This is the story of how the United States ordered more than a thousand six hundred bombers before one had flown, why that gamble cost more than the Manhattan Project, and what it got for the money.

[CHAPTER CARD: A Bomber for a War That Had Not Started]

## Chapter 1: A bomber for a war that had not started (1939–1942)

[S05 · C08]
To understand the B-29, start with a map. In 1939, America was not at war. But the head of the Army Air Corps, Major General Henry Arnold, known as Hap, was looking at distances.

[S06 · C09]
The bombers he had, like the B-17 Flying Fortress, were built to defend the American coast and reach targets a few hundred miles away. If war came in the Pacific, the distances were in the thousands. Arnold wanted a bomber that could carry a ton of bombs more than five thousand miles.

[S07 · C09, C10]
In January 1940, the Air Corps sent out its request for what it called a very long range bomber. Boeing had already been working on the idea, on its own money, for two years. Its answer was a design it called Model 345.

[S07a · C41]
Boeing was not the only company in the running. Lockheed, Douglas and Consolidated all sent in designs. The Army liked Boeing's best, but it was so unsure that any of them would work that it also paid Consolidated to build a rival, the B-32 Dominator, as insurance in case the B-29 failed. The B-32 turned out to have even more trouble than the B-29, and only a handful ever saw combat.

[S08 · C11]
On paper it was extraordinary. It was far bigger than the B-17, with a wingspan of a hundred and forty-one feet three inches and a loaded weight above sixty tons. It had a long, thin wing built for efficient cruising at height. And it would fly higher than almost anything else in the sky.

[S09 · C12]
Flying high is where the trouble begins. At thirty thousand feet the air is thin and bitterly cold, below minus forty degrees Celsius. In a B-17, the crew wore oxygen masks and electrically heated suits, and on long missions frostbite was a real danger. For a flight of twelve or fifteen hours, that was not going to work.

[S10 · C13]
So Boeing's design was pressurised. Air from the turbo-superchargers kept the crew compartments at an eight-thousand-foot altitude while cruising at thirty thousand feet, warm enough for ordinary flying clothes, before depressurising onto oxygen masks over the target. It was the first heavy bomber to go into large-scale service built that way.

[S11 · C14]
But a bomber has a problem that an airliner does not. Its middle is a pair of enormous bomb bays, and bomb bays have to open in flight. You cannot pressurise a space with doors that swing open at thirty thousand feet.

[S12 · C14]
Boeing's answer was a tunnel. The pilots, bombardier, engineer, navigator and radio operator sat in a pressurised nose. Three gunners sat in a pressurised section behind the wing. And joining the two, laid over the top of the bomb bays, was a padded tube twenty-eight inches wide, just big enough for a man to crawl through on his hands and knees. The tail gunner had his own small sealed compartment at the very back, and could only get to it and from it when the aircraft was unpressurised.

[S13 · C15]
Then there were the guns. With a crew sealed inside, nobody could stand at an open window with a machine gun. So the B-29's twin fifty-calibre guns sat in four turrets on the outside, aimed by remote control.

[S14 · C15]
The gunners sat at sighting stations under clear blisters. When one of them tracked an enemy fighter, an analogue computer built by General Electric did the arithmetic. It worked out how far ahead of the target to aim, allowed for the bomber's own speed, gravity drop, air density at height, and the parallax distance between the gunner's blister and the gun he was firing several feet away. It also let one gunner take over a turret from another. Nothing like it had gone to war in a bomber before.

[S15 · C15, C18]
All of this was new. The pressure cabin was new. The fire control was new. And the engines, which we will come to, were barely out of development.

[S16 · C16]
A normal programme would build a prototype, test it for a year or two, fix what was wrong, and then order the bomber in numbers. The United States did not have that time.

[S17 · C16]
In May 1941, sixteen months before the first prototype flew, the Army Air Forces ordered two hundred and fifty B-29s. After Pearl Harbor, the orders kept growing. By the time the first XB-29 left the ground in September 1942, the Army had ordered one thousand six hundred and sixty-four.

[S18 · C17]
It was a gamble without precedent. And it meant building the factories at the same time as the aeroplane. Boeing would build B-29s at a new plant in Wichita, Kansas, and at Renton, near Seattle. Bell would build them in Marietta, Georgia, and Glenn L. Martin in Omaha, Nebraska. Thousands of subcontractors fed parts into all four.

[S19 · C03, C17]
That is where much of the three billion dollars went. Not into the bombers alone, but into building an industry from nothing to make them.

[S19a · C42]
Wichita shows the scale. Before the war, the Boeing plant there built small Stearman training biplanes. By 1944 Wichita Plant II employed nearly thirty thousand workers turning out B-29s, and the town's population had grown so fast that a whole new housing community, Planeview, was built for twenty thousand people. Many of the workers riveting the bombers together had never worked in a factory before, and more than a third of them were women.

[S19b · C17, C22]
Every B-29 was made of more than forty thousand parts, drawn on paper by hand, and every change to one part meant new drawings sent out to four factories and their suppliers. In that world, a change in the design was not a quick fix. It was weeks of work for thousands of people.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Engine That Caught Fire]

## Chapter 2: The engine that caught fire (1942–1943)

[S20 · C18]
Every one of those B-29s needed four engines, and the engine was the Wright R-3350 Duplex-Cyclone.

[S21 · C18]
It was a huge radial engine, eighteen cylinders arranged in two rows of nine, rated at two thousand two hundred horsepower, with twin General Electric turbo-superchargers to keep it breathing at height. No radial engine that powerful had ever been built in such numbers. And it was rushed.

[S22 · C19]
The B-29's tightly cowled nacelles and the engine's closely packed cylinders made cooling hard. The rear row of cylinders sat behind the front row and got less cooling air. On a long climb with a heavy load, the engines ran hot. Exhaust valves warped and failed, sparking backfires. And time after time, an engine caught fire.

[S23 · C19]
An engine fire in a B-29 was especially dangerous. To save weight, Curtiss-Wright had cast the rear accessory drive housing and supercharger casing out of magnesium alloy. Magnesium is light and strong, but once burning oil or a backfire heats it enough to ignite, it burns at over three thousand degrees Fahrenheit, and carbon-dioxide extinguishers cannot put it out. A fire that reached those magnesium parts could burn straight through the aluminium main wing spar in sixty to ninety seconds.

[S24 · C20]
The man trying to tame this was Boeing's chief test pilot, Edmund Allen, known as Eddie. He was one of the most respected engineering test pilots in America, and he had flown the first XB-29 on the twenty-first of September, 1942.

[S25 · C20]
The early test flights were plagued by engine trouble. On the first XB-29, engines had to be changed sixteen times in the first twenty-seven hours of flight. When the second prototype, serial number 41-3, first flew on the thirtieth of December, 1942, an engine caught fire in the air.

[S26 · C04]
On the eighteenth of February, 1943, Allen took off from Boeing Field in that second prototype with ten crew aboard. Eight minutes into the flight, the number one engine caught fire. Allen feathered the propeller, fired the extinguishers, and turned back for the field.

[S27 · C04]
The magnesium fire spread into the wing and filled the cockpit with smoke. The bomber came down three miles short of the runway and struck the Frye and Company meat-packing plant south of downtown Seattle. Everyone on board was killed: Allen and all ten of his crew. On the ground, twenty workers in the plant and one Seattle firefighter, Luther Bonner, died—thirty-two people in all.

[S28 · C21]
It was one of the worst moments of the programme. The Army ordered investigations into the engine, and into how Boeing tested the aircraft. In July 1943, Senator Harry Truman's Senate committee on war spending investigated Curtiss-Wright's Lockland plant, and was sharply critical of how the R-3350 was being inspected and built.

[S29 · C22]
But the war did not stop. Production did not stop. The fix was made in parallel. At Boeing and at the NACA altitude wind tunnel in Cleveland, engineers fitted cooling baffles, added valve oil pipes, redesigned the exhaust, and later switched to direct fuel injection, feeding every change into aircraft already coming down the lines.

[S30 · C22]
And that is the hidden cost of ordering a weapon off the drawing board. Every mistake you find in testing has to be corrected on hundreds of aircraft that already exist.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Battle of Kansas]

## Chapter 3: The Battle of Kansas (1944)

[S31 · C23]
By early 1944, the plan was to send the B-29 to war that spring. The bombers would go first to bases in eastern India and stage through forward fields in western China to strike Japan. The operation was code-named Matterhorn.

[S32 · C06]
The aircraft were coming off the lines. More than a hundred sat at airfields in Kansas. But when the Army looked at them in March 1944, almost none were ready to fight.

[S33 · C06]
Because the design had kept changing while production went on, new B-29s rolled out of the factory needing modification before they could go to war. They went to modification centres, then to the training bases. And there, in the winter of 1944, they piled up.

[S34 · C06]
On the ninth of March, 1944, General Arnold visited Salina, Kansas. He had promised President Roosevelt the bombers would be in action that spring. He found that not a single one was combat-ready to deploy.

[S35 · C06]
What followed became known as the Battle of Kansas. Arnold sent a senior officer, Major General Bennett Meyers, to take charge on the tenth of March. Boeing pulled roughly six hundred mechanics and engineers from its plants in Wichita and Seattle and sent them to the four Kansas bases at Salina, Pratt, Great Bend and Walker.

[S36 · C06]
There were not enough hangars for aircraft this big. So much of the work was done outside on open ramps, in March snowstorms and freezing wind. Mechanics worked twelve-hour shifts around the clock, changing engines, fitting cooling baffles, rewiring gun systems, and fitting the latest modifications by hand.

[S37 · C06]
It worked, just. By the fifteenth of April, 1944, in the space of five weeks, one hundred and fifty B-29s were made ready, and began the long flight east to India.

[S38 · C23, C24]
But Operation Matterhorn had its own impossible arithmetic.

[S39 · C23]
The forward bases were around Chengdu, in western China, built by more than three hundred thousand Chinese labourers using hand tools. Nothing could reach them by sea or road in any useful amount. Every gallon of fuel, every bomb and every spare engine had to be flown in from India over the Himalayas, a route the pilots called the Hump.

[S40 · C24]
And the main thing flying that fuel over the Hump was the B-29 itself. Bombers were stripped down and used as tankers. For each combat mission the bombers flew from China, up to six or seven Hump trips had to be flown first just to stockpile the fuel and bombs for it.

[S41a · C25]
The first combat mission came on the fifth of June, 1944, flown from India against railway yards at Bangkok. Ninety-eight B-29s took off. Several bombers—five in all—were lost to engine failures and accidents rather than to the enemy, and the bombing through cloud was scattered. It was a pattern that would repeat for months.

[S41 · C25]
Ten days later, on the fifteenth of June, 1944, sixty-eight B-29s from China struck the Imperial Iron and Steel Works at Yawata, in southern Japan. It was the first raid on the Japanese home islands since the Doolittle raid two years before. Only forty-seven bombed, the damage was slight, and seven B-29s were lost, mostly to mechanical trouble. Worse, supplying China meant raids could be flown only every few weeks.

[S42 · C24, C25]
Matterhorn showed the B-29 could reach Japan. It also showed that China was the wrong place to do it from.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Wind Over Tokyo]

## Chapter 4: The wind over Tokyo (1944–1945)

[S43 · C26]
The answer was a chain of islands in the western Pacific: the Marianas.

[S44 · C26]
In the summer of 1944, American forces captured Saipan, Tinian and Guam. From there, Tokyo was around one thousand five hundred miles away. Within B-29 range, with fuel and bombs coming in directly by ship.

[S45 · C27]
Navy construction battalions, the Seabees, set to work almost at once. On Tinian, they built North Field, with four parallel runways each eight and a half thousand feet long, carved and levelled from coral, alongside two more at West Field. By 1945 it was the busiest bomber base in the world.

[S46 · C28]
On the twenty-fourth of November, 1944, a hundred and eleven B-29s from Saipan flew to Tokyo for the first time. The plan for these raids was the plan the bomber had been designed for: high-altitude, daylight, precision bombing of aircraft engine factories, from around thirty thousand feet.

[S47 · C07, C28]
And at thirty thousand feet over Japan, the crews found something nobody had planned for.

[S48 · C07]
The wind. At that height above Japan in winter, a band of air was flowing from west to east at extraordinary speed, routinely a hundred and fifty miles an hour, and sometimes over two hundred. Today we call it the jet stream. In 1944, almost nothing was known about it in practice.

[S49 · C07]
It wrecked the plan. A bomber flying downwind could pass over its target at a ground speed of four hundred and fifty to five hundred miles an hour, far too fast for the Norden bombsight to track properly. A bomber flying into the wind could almost hang in the air, an easy target for anti-aircraft guns, burning fuel it would need to get home. And crosswinds pushed the falling bombs a mile or more off their aiming points.

[S50 · C29]
Add thick cloud over Japan for much of the winter, and the long climb to height with a full load that strained the engines, and the results were poor. On that first Tokyo raid, only twenty-four bombers even found the Nakajima engine plant. Over the first two months of raids from the Marianas, fewer than five per cent of bombs fell within a thousand feet of their targets.

[S51 · C29]
The most expensive aircraft programme of the war was, by its own measure, failing.

[S51a · C43]
The losses mounted too, and most of them were not caused by the enemy. A bomber damaged over Japan, or short of fuel after fighting the wind, had to fly fifteen hundred miles of open sea to get home. Many came down in the water.

[S51b · C43]
That was one of the reasons for one of the hardest-fought battles of the Pacific war. In February and March 1945, US Marines took the island of Iwo Jima, roughly halfway between the Marianas and Tokyo. On the fourth of March, 1945, while fighting continued on the island, a damaged B-29 named *Dinah Might* made the first emergency landing there. By the end of the war, B-29s had made two thousand two hundred and fifty-one emergency landings on Iwo Jima, carrying more than twenty-four thousand airmen.

[CHAPTER CARD: LeMay's Change and the Verdict]

## Chapter 5: LeMay's change, and the verdict (1945)

[S52 · C30]
On the twentieth of January, 1945, command of the B-29s in the Marianas passed to Major General Curtis LeMay. He had made his name commanding bombers over Germany, and he looked at the B-29 problem as a set of hard numbers.

[S53 · C31]
High altitude meant the jet stream, the cloud, and a long climb that wore out engines. So he proposed to give it up.

[S53a · C31]
It was a risky idea. Flying lower meant flying into the range of light anti-aircraft guns. LeMay's own staff were divided about it. But he judged that at night, Japan's defences were weaker than they were by day, and that the gain in accuracy and bomb load was worth the risk.

[S54 · C31]
In March 1945, LeMay sent his bombers in individually at night, at between five thousand and eight thousand feet. Below the worst of the wind. Below the cloud. Defensive guns and gunners were left behind to save weight, and the bombers carried M69 and M47 incendiaries instead of high explosives. Without the long climb, the engines ran cooler, and each aircraft could carry more than double the load.

[S55 · C32]
The first of these mass raids, Operation Meetinghouse on Tokyo on the night of the ninth to the tenth of March, 1945, put two hundred and seventy-nine bombers over the city and was the most destructive air raid in history. Nearly sixteen square miles of the city burned. Most estimates put the number of people killed at between eighty thousand and a hundred thousand, most of them civilians, with fourteen B-29s lost.

[S56 · C33]
Over the following five months, the same method was turned on more than sixty of Japan's cities, alongside precision attacks on oil refineries and aerial mining of ports. It was effective, by the measure the commanders used, and it remains one of the most debated decisions of the war.

[S57 · C01, C34]
And then, in August 1945, the two most expensive projects of the American war met in one aircraft.

[S58 · C34]
On the sixth of August, 1945, a B-29 named *Enola Gay* dropped the first atomic bomb used in war, on Hiroshima. On the ninth, another B-29, *Bockscar*, dropped the second, on Nagasaki. Both belonged to the 509th Composite Group, flying Omaha-built B-29s specially modified under Project Silverplate with fuel-injected engines, reversible-pitch propellers, and stripped turrets. No other aircraft the United States had could carry a five-ton bomb that far.

[S59 · C35]
Japan announced its surrender on the fifteenth of August, and signed the formal surrender aboard *USS Missouri* on the second of September as hundreds of B-29s flew overhead.

[S60 · C03, C36]
So was the B-29 worth three billion dollars?

[S61 · C36, C17]
By the end of the war, three thousand nine hundred and seventy had been built across Wichita, Renton, Marietta and Omaha. It had been rushed into service with an engine that was not ready, a fire-control system no one had used in combat, and a cabin design no bomber had used at scale.

[S62 · C37]
In combat operations against Japan, the Twentieth Air Force lost four hundred and fourteen B-29s. Only a hundred and forty-seven fell to Japanese fighters or anti-aircraft fire; the other two hundred and sixty-seven—nearly two-thirds of combat-mission losses—were lost to engine fires, mechanical failures and accidents. The early raids failed. And the method that finally worked was not the one it had been designed for.

[S62a · C02, C03]
And the comparison in the title is not quite like for like. The Manhattan Project's one point nine billion dollars bought factories, reactors and laboratories, as well as two bombs used in war. The B-29 programme's three billion dollars bought nearly four thousand aircraft, four factories, and the training of tens of thousands of aircrew and mechanics. But both were gambles of the same kind. Each began before anyone knew whether the thing could be made to work, and each paid for that uncertainty by building everything at once.

[S63 · C38]
But the B-29 also left something behind. The pressurised cabin, the long efficient wing, and the remote-controlled guns all fed into the aircraft that came after it. Boeing turned the B-29 design into an airliner, the Stratocruiser, into the C-97 and KC-97 transports and tankers, and into the B-50 bomber, while the Soviet Union copied three interned B-29s into the Tupolev Tu-4.

[S64 · C38]
Pressurised flight at high altitude, which seemed exotic in 1940, became ordinary. Today, millions of people cross oceans every day in sealed cabins, high above the weather, without thinking about it.

[S65 · C03, C04]
The B-29 is remembered as the bomber that ended the war. It might be better remembered as the gamble that showed what it really costs to build a new technology before you know it works: three billion dollars, an industry built from nothing, and the lives of the people who tested it.

[S66 · C40]
Of the three thousand nine hundred and seventy built, only two B-29s—*FIFI* and *Doc*—still fly today.
