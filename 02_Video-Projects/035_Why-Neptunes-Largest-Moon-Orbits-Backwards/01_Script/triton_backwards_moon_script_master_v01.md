# Why Neptune's Largest Moon Orbits Backwards

If you stood on the nitrogen frost of Neptune's moon Triton — in backwards orbit — what would you see erupting above its horizon?

On 25 August 1989, NASA's Voyager 2 spacecraft capped a seven-billion-kilometre journey to Neptune — thirty times farther from the Sun than Earth — and photographed two-thirds of its largest moon, Triton. While every other large moon in our solar system orbits in the direction its planet spins, Triton circles Neptune backwards in a retrograde orbit — and at minus two hundred and thirty-five degrees Celsius, Voyager 2 discovered active geysers spewing icy material more than eight kilometres high.

Why is a world two thousand seven hundred kilometres wide — twenty-two per cent smaller than Earth's Moon — orbiting the wrong way around the solar system's windiest planet, what if you walked where fifty dark plumes trail over one hundred and sixty kilometres across solid nitrogen, and what secret is warming Triton's atmosphere today?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Brewer Who Found a World]

23 September 1846. Because Uranus was not travelling along the path astronomers expected, French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier calculated the position and mass of an unknown planet pulling on Uranus and sent his predictions to German astronomer Johann Gottfried Galle at the Berlin Observatory.

Galle found Neptune on his first night of searching — making it the first planet located through mathematical predictions rather than regular sky surveys.

Seventeen days later, on 10 October 1846, English astronomer William Lassell pointed a telescope — financed by the fortune he made in the brewery business — toward the new planet and discovered its largest moon, Triton.

How vast is the system Lassell glimpsed? With an equatorial diameter of forty-nine thousand five hundred and twenty-eight kilometres, Neptune is four times wider than Earth — if Earth were a nickel, Neptune would be as big as a baseball.

From an average distance of four point five billion kilometres — thirty astronomical units from the Sun — sunlight takes four hours to reach Neptune and Triton, arriving nine hundred times dimmer than daylight on Earth.

Neptune takes one hundred and sixty-five Earth years to complete one orbit — finishing its first lap since discovery in 2011 — with each season lasting over forty years. Why did a second moon discovered a century later reveal violence in Neptune's sky?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Moon Going the Wrong Way]

1949. For over a century after Lassell's discovery, Triton was known simply as the satellite of Neptune — until astronomer Gerard Kuiper discovered a second moon, Nereid.

Nereid follows one of the most eccentric orbits of any moon in our solar system, averaging five point five million kilometres from Neptune and requiring three hundred and sixty Earth days — nearly a full Earth year — for one orbit.

What disturbed Nereid? The culprit orbits closer in: Triton circles three hundred and fifty-four thousand seven hundred and sixty kilometres from Neptune, locked in synchronous rotation every five point eight eight days.

Tilted one hundred and fifty-seven degrees, Triton is the only large moon in the solar system orbiting opposite to its planet's sixteen-hour rotation.

Scientists think Triton was an independent world in the Kuiper Belt — sharing striking similarities with Pluto — captured by Neptune's gravity millions of years ago, disturbing Nereid onto its eccentric loop.

With a mean density of two thousand and fifty kilograms per cubic metre — about twice the density of water, higher than almost any outer-planet moon except Io and Europa — Triton packs more rock and metal inside its core than the icy moons of Saturn and Uranus. Why did it take twelve years in deep space to reach it?

[CHAPTER CARD: Twelve Years to the Blue Giant]

20 August 1977. NASA's seven-hundred-and-twenty-two-kilogram Voyager 2 spacecraft launches from Cape Canaveral on a Grand Tour of all four giant planets — flying past Jupiter in 1979, Saturn in 1981, and Uranus on 24 January 1986.

On 14 February 1986, Voyager 2 fired its largest midcourse correction to aim directly for Neptune.

At 03:56 Universal Time on 25 August 1989 — capping a twelve-year, seven-billion-kilometre journey — Voyager 2 skimmed four thousand eight hundred kilometres over Neptune's cloud tops, the closest of its four flybys, discovering six new moons and four rings.

Despite receiving nine hundred times less sunlight than Earth, Neptune is our solar system's windiest planet. Supersonic winds whip frozen methane clouds across Neptune at over two thousand kilometres an hour — three times stronger than Jupiter's winds and nine times stronger than Earth's four-hundred-kilometre-an-hour hurricanes — beside a Great Dark Spot large enough to contain the entire Earth.

Hours later, Voyager 2 dove toward Triton. Before the flyby, many expected a dead, frozen sphere. Case closed? Was Triton an inert block of ice where nothing changes?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Coldest Geysers in the Solar System]

Wrong. Mapping Triton down to nine hundred metres per pixel, Voyager 2 revealed the coldest known surface in the solar system — paired with active eruptions.

Triton's surface sits at thirty-eight kelvins — minus two hundred and thirty-five degrees Celsius, or minus three hundred and ninety-one degrees Fahrenheit.

Most of its nitrogen condenses as frost, making Triton the only satellite in the solar system with a surface made mainly of nitrogen ice, reflecting seventy per cent of the sunlight that hits it.

Yet across Triton's south polar cap, Voyager 2 photographed about fifty dark plumes trailing from vents a few miles wide.

Active geysers spewed icy material more than eight kilometres — five miles — straight up before thin winds carried dark plumes over one hundred and sixty kilometres across the frost.

Triton, Io, and Venus are the only bodies in the solar system besides Earth known to be volcanically active today. How can geysers erupt eight kilometres high at minus two hundred and thirty-five degrees Celsius?

[CHAPTER CARD: Ice Lava and the Cantaloupe World]

That brings us to the biggest misconception about outer-solar-system volcanism. On Earth, volcanoes erupt molten rock above a thousand degrees Celsius.

Let us test that with physics. Triton's cryovolcanoes erupt volatile ices from a crust of frozen nitrogen over a water-ice mantle and a rock-and-metal core.

Because of Triton's tilted inclination, both polar regions take turns facing the Sun; seasonal heating warms subsurface nitrogen until pressurized gas blasts upward, feeding one of only three nitrogen-dominated atmospheres in the solar system alongside Earth and Titan.

Across regions five hundred kilometres wide, Voyager 2 also photographed pitted cantaloupe terrain and smooth basins resurfaced by flooding, melting, and icy lava flows.

Observations from Earth since 1989 show Triton's thin nitrogen atmosphere is growing warmer, though scientists do not yet know why. What did Voyager 2 see looking back — and where is ice hiding closest to the Sun?

[CHAPTER CARD: Farewell to the Ice Giants]

Three days and six and a half hours after closest approach — on 28 August 1989, from four point eight six million kilometres away — Voyager 2 captured the glowing crescents of Neptune and Triton before plunging forty-eight degrees below the ecliptic.

In November 1998, twenty-one years after launch, nonessential instruments were turned off, and on 10 December 2018, Voyager 2 entered interstellar space — speeding from the Sun at fifteen point four kilometres per second, taking nineteen thousand three hundred and ninety years to travel one light-year.

Triton showed that a captured Kuiper Belt moon orbiting backwards at minus two hundred and thirty-five degrees Celsius can resurface itself with icy lava and fire geysers eight kilometres high.

Next, we race to the planet closest to the Sun — where daytime temperatures reach four hundred and thirty degrees Celsius, yet shadowed polar craters on Mercury preserve water ice at minus one hundred and eighty degrees Celsius.
