# Why Is Saturn's Moon Iapetus Half Black and Half White?

Orbiting two million two hundred and thirteen thousand miles from Saturn where a walnut-shaped moon of water ice and rock shows a leading hemisphere as dark as coal and a trailing hemisphere almost as bright as snow, as you cross the outer Saturnian system and fly within one thousand and twenty miles of its horizon with NASA's Cassini spacecraft, why is Saturn's moon Iapetus half black and half white?

When Cassini swept past this nine-hundred-and-twelve-mile-wide world on December 31, 2004 and returned for its closest flyby on September 10, 2007, scientists uncovered a striking paradox: dark terrain called Cassini Regio reflects just three to five percent of sunlight across forty percent of the surface, while the bright icy highlands reflect fifty to sixty percent.

Now how did a distant retrograde moon and a seventy-nine-day rotation turn a whisper of dark space dust into a global mystery of sublimating ice, what secret explains why a mountain ridge up to twelve miles high girdles Iapetus's equator like a walnut shell, and what happens next to our picture of how moons evolve?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Moon That Vanished on One Side of Saturn]

On October 25, 1671, astronomer Giovanni Domenico Cassini spotted a new moon orbiting Saturn, but through his telescope he could see it only on the west side of the planet until it vanished on the east side.

Then Cassini deduced that the moon was tidally locked so it always kept the same face toward Saturn, and that one hemisphere had to be much darker than the other — a mystery validated more than three centuries later when NASA's Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 spacecraft flew past in 1980 and 1981.

Named Iapetus after the Greek Titan suggested by astronomer John Herschel, Saturn's third-largest moon has a mean radius of four hundred and fifty-seven miles, a diameter less than a third of Saturn's largest moon Titan at three thousand two hundred miles across, and a density just one point two times liquid water because it is three quarters ice and one quarter rock.

However, orbiting two million two hundred and thirteen thousand miles from Saturn in a tilted path compared with Titan at seven hundred and fifty-nine thousand two hundred miles, Iapetus is the only large moon with a clear, tilted view of Saturn's rings. Now where did the dark stain on its leading face come from?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Retrograde Dust Cloud of Phoebe]

When NASA's Cassini spacecraft arrived in the Saturn system in 2004, its first target was Phoebe, an irregular outer moon orbiting eight million forty-nine thousand six hundred and sixty-eight miles from Saturn — almost four times farther beyond Saturn than its nearest neighbour Iapetus.

With a mean radius of sixty-six point two miles — about one-sixteenth the radius of Earth's Moon — Phoebe spins on its axis every nine hours and completes a retrograde orbit tilted one hundred and seventy-five degrees to Saturn's equator in about eighteen Earth months.

Because Phoebe reflects only six percent of sunlight and travels backwards around Saturn, scientists realized it is a captured Centaur from the outer solar system where dark material is plentiful.

As impacts knock dark, reddish dust off Phoebe and other outer moons, then those particles spiral inward toward Saturn, and because Iapetus orbits in the opposite direction, its leading hemisphere rams head-on into the incoming dust. Now why didn't that dust simply bury half of Iapetus under a thick crust?

[CHAPTER CARD: Seventy-Nine Days of Sunlight and Thermal Segregation]

Wrong. Neither a thick blanket of incoming dust nor erupting hydrocarbon ice volcanoes can explain why the boundary between black and white on Iapetus is so sharp and patchy.

When Cassini executed its only close flyby of Iapetus on September 10, 2007 — passing roughly one thousand and twenty miles above the surface at one point five miles per second — its cameras proved that a third process called thermal segregation drives the dichotomy.

Because Iapetus is tidally locked in a distant orbit, one full rotation lasts one thousand nine hundred and four hours — longer than seventy-nine days — giving the moon a remarkably slow daily temperature cycle.

Once a thin infall of dark dust from space lands on the leading hemisphere, then that dark surface absorbs more sunlight and warms up until water ice within the dark terrain sublimes into vapour and retreats to colder regions on the trailing hemisphere and poles — making the dark terrain even darker and the neighbouring cold regions even brighter. [CHAPTER CARD: A Dark Blanket Only One Foot Thick]

During the September 10, 2007 encounter, Cassini's narrow-angle camera captured its closest mosaic of Iapetus from distances between one thousand and eleven and one thousand two hundred and sixty-eight miles, resolving details just thirty-three feet — ten metres — per pixel.

Across the dark plains of Cassini Regio, you see bright white spots sixty-six to one hundred and sixty-four feet — twenty to fifty metres — across where small meteoroids punched through the dark layer to reveal clean ice beneath.

Because those bright craters are so small and shallow, scientists discovered a deeper secret: the dark blanket is only on the order of a foot thick — a few tens of centimetres, where ten centimetres equals four inches.

Meanwhile across the trailing hemisphere mosaic captured from forty-five thousand miles, dark carbonaceous material and cyanides coat low crater floors and warm equator-facing slopes like spots on a Dalmatian, proving bright water ice forms the moon's bedrock. Now why does a giant mountain wall ring its equator?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Twelve-Mile-High Mountains on the Equator]

On December 25 and December 31, 2004, as Cassini flew within one hundred and seven thousand one hundred and twenty-four miles of Iapetus, images revealed a mountain ridge coinciding almost exactly with the moon's equator.

Spanning twelve miles wide at its base and topped in places by a cratered plateau nine miles wide, the ridge runs over eight hundred and eight miles — one thousand three hundred kilometres — across the dark hemisphere before breaking into isolated peaks on the anti-Saturn side.

While the ridge averages six miles — ten kilometres — in height, its tallest peaks reach eight to twelve miles — thirteen to twenty kilometres — above the plains, rivalling Olympus Mons on Mars at three times the height of Mount Everest.

However, finding mountains up to twelve miles high on a moon nine hundred and twelve miles across baffled geologists, because Iapetus is nearly five times smaller than Mars and nearly nine times smaller than Earth. Now how could a small icy world keep such a ridge?

[CHAPTER CARD: Ancient Giant Basins and a Collapsed Ring]

Across both hemispheres, Cassini mapped at least nine giant impact basins scarred into the ancient crust, led by the three-hundred-and-sixty-mile-wide Turgis basin in Cassini Regio and the three-hundred-and-thirteen-mile Engelier basin destroying half of the two-hundred-and-seventy-six-mile Gerin basin on the trailing side.

Because the equatorial ridge is heavily cratered and Iapetus orbits two million two hundred and thirteen thousand miles beyond Saturn's strong tidal forces, its icy crust escaped the melting episodes that smoothed moons closer to Saturn.

Scientists debate two theories for why this ancient walnut ridge formed: either Iapetus froze its ridge in place when the young moon rotated much faster than its current seventy-nine-day spin, or the ridge is made of material left from the collapse of a ring.

Whether shaped by a faster early spin or a collapsed ring and painted by Phoebe's dust and thermal segregation, Iapetus preserves four billion years of outer-solar-system history — and next, we travel toward Earth to ask why NASA's DART spacecraft slammed into an asteroid moonlet and changed its orbit by thirty-three minutes.
