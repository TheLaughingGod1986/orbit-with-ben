### 2.5 Primary Scientific & Agency Fact Sheet (Pre-Verified for `SOURCES.md`)
- **Discovery of the "Squashed Comet" (`24 March 1993`, IAU Circular `5725`, `26 March 1993`; `Shoemaker et al. 1993`):**
  - On the stormy night of **24 March 1993** at **Palomar Observatory** in California, planetary geologist **Eugene M. Shoemaker** (`1928–1997` — founder of the USGS Astrogeology Research Program, whose ashes were later carried to the Moon on `Lunar Prospector` in `1999`), astronomer **Carolyn S. Shoemaker** (`1929–2021`), and science writer **David H. Levy** (`born 1948`) exposed water-damaged Kodak `IIa-D` photographic films on the **`0.46-metre` (`18-inch`) Palomar Schmidt telescope**.
  - Examining the stereomicroscope pairs on **25 March 1993**, Carolyn Shoemaker spotted an elongated, bar-like smudge (`~1 arcminute` long) near Jupiter (`4°` away on the sky) and said, *"I don't know what this is, capability-wise, but it looks like a squashed comet."* Designated **Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 (`1993e` / `D/1993 F2`)** — the `9th` periodic comet discovered by the trio.
- **Jupiter Capture & the `7 July 1992` Roche-Limit Tidal Breakup (`Chodas & Yeomans 1996`; `Asphaug & Benz 1994`, *Nature* `370`, `120–124`; `Sekanina et al. 1994`):**
  - Backward orbital integration by **Brian G. Marsden** (`1937–2010`, Minor Planet Center) and **Paul W. Chodas** (`born 1952`) & **Donald K. Yeomans** (`born 1942`) at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory proved that `SL9` was not orbiting the Sun directly: Jupiter had captured it around **`1929 ± 9 years` (or `~1960s–1970`)** into a highly eccentric **2-year Jovicentric orbit** (`apojove ~0.33 AU` / `49 million km`).
  - On **7 July 1992**, `SL9` passed perijove at **`~1.36` Jovian radii (`~97,000 km` from Jupiter's centre, just `~25,000–40,000 km` above the `71,492-km` equatorial cloud tops)** — deep inside **Jupiter's fluid Roche limit (`~2.0–2.4 R_J`)**.
  - Hydrocode N-body simulations by **Erik Asphaug** and **Willy Benz** (`Asphaug & Benz 1994`, *Nature* `370`, `120–124`) demonstrated that Jupiter's differential tidal gravity tore a **strengthless, rubble-pile parent nucleus `~1.5–1.8 km` in diameter** (`bulk density ~0.5–0.6 g/cm³`) apart during perijove, after which self-gravity re-condensed the debris cloud into a linear chain of **21 distinct fragments (labelled `A` through `W`, omitting `I` and `O`)** spanning **`>1.1 million km` (`~3×` the Earth–Moon distance)** by July 1994.
- **The 6-Day Bombardment Observed by `Galileo` & Repaired `Hubble` (`16–22 July 1994`; `Hammel et al. 1995`, *Science* `267`, `1288–1296`; `Martin et al. 1995`, *Science* `268`, `1875–1879`):**
  - In **May 1993**, `Marsden` and `Chodas/Yeomans` calculated a `>99%` certainty that all `21` fragments would collide with Jupiter's southern hemisphere (`~43°–45° S` latitude) between **16 and 22 July 1994** at an impact velocity of **`~60 km/s` (`216,000 km/h`)** and a `~45°` entry angle.
  - Because each fragment struck on Jupiter's night side just **`~4°–10°` behind the morning limb** as seen from Earth, ground telescopes and Hubble could not see the initial entry flash directly — except NASA's Jupiter-bound **`Galileo` spacecraft**, positioned **`1.6 AU` (`240 million km`) away** at a `~42°` phase angle with a direct view of the impact site (`Martin et al. 1995`, *Science* `268`, `1875–1879`, recording `7,500–24,000 K` bolide flashes lasting `~6–40 seconds`).
  - Just **`7 months` after Space Shuttle `Endeavour` (`STS-61`, December 1993) repaired Hubble's blurry optics with `WFPC2`**, a Hubble imaging team led by planetary astronomer **Heidi B.
