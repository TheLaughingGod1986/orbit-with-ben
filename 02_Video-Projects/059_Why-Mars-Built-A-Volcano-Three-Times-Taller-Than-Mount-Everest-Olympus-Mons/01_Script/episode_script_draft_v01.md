# Why Did Mars Build a Volcano Three Times Taller Than Mount Everest?

Look down from Mars orbit past the dusty horizon of our neighbor, where one shield volcano — Olympus Mons — rises so high into the thin red sky that its summit survives above planet-wide dust storms. Why did a world half the diameter of Earth build a 27-kilometer Mars shield volcano — nearly three times the height of Mount Everest and wider than Arizona — and what secret deep beneath the Martian crust let one mountain grow to 600 kilometers across while Earth's volcanoes stop smaller? Because while Earth's crust is broken into moving tectonic plates that carry Hawaii off its mantle plume, Mars locked a stationary 70-kilometer-thick crust over a single hotspot under 38 percent of Earth's surface gravity.

Not because Mars was hotter inside, but because since Mars formed 4.5 billion years ago, the mystery of a stalled crust and low gravity let basaltic lava pile onto one spot until it towered beyond every mountain on Earth.

What would you see and feel standing below the 8-kilometer perimeter cliff scarp of Olympus Mons, or looking deeper into its 60-by-80-kilometer summit caldera where air pressure drops to 72 pascals — 12 percent of the Martian average? Even though Mount Everest rises 8,848 meters — 29,029 feet — above sea level and Mauna Loa rises 9 kilometers from the sea floor, in 1999 NASA's Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter measured Olympus Mons at 21.287 kilometers — 69,840 feet — above the Martian datum and 26 to 27 kilometers above Amazonis Planitia. Now how did astronomers first spot this giant through a global dust storm?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Snow Patch That Survived a Global Dust Storm]

In the late 19th century, Italian astronomer Giovanni Virginio Schiaparelli — who lived from 1835 to 1910 — observed a bright Martian patch named Nix Olympica, "Olympic Snow."

As English astronomer Patrick Moore — who lived from 1923 to 2012 — pointed out, Schiaparelli found that Nix Olympica stayed visible during global dust storms, and guessed that it must be high.

When Mariner 9 entered Mars orbit in 1971 during a global dust storm, the clearing Tharsis summits proved Nix Olympica was a volcano — Olympus Mons — rising 27 kilometers high and 600 kilometers — 370 miles — wide across 300,000 square kilometers, the size of Italy, the Philippines, or Arizona.

Then on March 9, 1979, Jet Propulsion Laboratory geophysicist William L. Sjogren reported in *Science* Viking Orbiter 2 Doppler gravity data at 500-kilometer resolution from 30 degrees south to 65 degrees north, modeling Olympus Mons as a 600-kilometer surface disk weighing 8.7 times 10 to the 21st grams. Now why didn't that volcano break into an island chain like Hawaii?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why a Stationary Crust and 38 Percent Gravity Built a Giant]

On May 28 and October 1, 1999, NASA Goddard scientist David E. Smith — born in 1937 — and MIT geophysicist Maria T. Zuber — born in 1958 — reported in *Science* global Martian topography and gravity from Mars Global Surveyor's laser altimeter.

Then on March 10, 2000 and March 30, 2001, Maria T. Zuber, Columbia geophysicist Sean C. Solomon — born in 1945 — and Washington University geophysicist Roger J. Phillips — who lived from 1940 to 2020 — showed in *Science* how the Tharsis rise emplaced 3 times 10 to the 8th — 300 million — cubic kilometers of magma across a stiffening lithosphere.

On Earth, under 9.82 meters per second squared gravity, moving plates carry crust across plumes until each volcano shuts off, capping Mauna Loa at 9 kilometers — 5.5 miles — from the sea floor.

But on Mars, under 3.73 meters per second squared gravity — 38 percent of Earth's — without mobile plates, a 70-kilometer-thick — 43-mile — lithosphere stayed fixed over one hotspot while spreading on weak clays suddenly carved an 8-kilometer perimeter cliff scarp — Olympus Rupes, 6 to 8 kilometers or 5 miles tall — ringed by a 2-kilometer-deep moat and the 750-kilometer Lycus Sulci landslide aureole. [CHAPTER CARD: The Invisible Summit and Its 72-Pascal Near-Vacuum]

Above that 6-to-8-kilometer cliff, you might expect a steep peak.

However, as MIT planetary physicist Gordon H. Pettengill — who lived from 1926 to 2021 — and NASA Goddard scientist James B. Garvin helped map with the Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter, the average flank slope of Olympus Mons is only 5 percent — a gentle shield profile like a circus tent, where an observer sees the horizon just 3 kilometers away.

At the summit — 21.287 kilometers, or 69,840 feet, above datum and nearly 26 kilometers, or 16 miles, above Amazonis Planitia 1,000 kilometers northwest — six nested calderas span 60 by 80 kilometers and 3.2 kilometers deep, 32 kilometers — 105,000 feet — above a magma chamber whose floors formed 350 million to 150 million years ago.

Across an 11.0-kilometer atmospheric scale height from Mars's 600-pascal — 6.36-millibar — surface average, the peak experiences a 72-pascal summit air pressure, 12 percent of the Martian average, compared with 32,000 pascals — 32 percent of Earth sea level — atop Mount Everest, while its basalt holds 44 percent silicates, 17.5 percent iron oxides, 7 percent aluminum, 7 percent sulfur dioxide, 6 percent magnesium, and 6 percent calcium. [CHAPTER CARD: 2-Million-Year-Old Lava and Hidden Tropical Glaciers]

You might assume a volcano whose calderas collapsed 350 million to 150 million years ago — and whose flanks carry 15.6-kilometer Karzok crater and 10.4-kilometer Pangboche crater — has been dead for eons.

Wrong.

On December 23, 2004, German planetary scientist Gerhard Neukum — who lived from 1944 to 2014 — Brown University geologist James W. Head — born in 1941 — and colleagues reported in *Nature* three-dimensional images from the European Space Agency's Mars Express High Resolution Stereo Camera at 10 to 20 meters per pixel.

They showed that calderas on five major Martian volcanoes resurfaced during the last 20 percent of Martian history, while lava on Olympus Mons ranges from 115 million years down to 2-million-year-old Martian lava flows — suggesting it is potentially still active today — alongside snow and ice deposition above 7,000 meters and scarp-base glaciers active 4 million years ago.

Then on March 17, 2005, James W. Head, Gerhard Neukum, and colleagues reported in *Nature* that rock glaciers at the Olympus Mons scarp formed during high spin-axis obliquity when polar ice moved to the tropics, leaving water ice beneath insulating dust today. Now what does Olympus Mons reveal about rocky worlds?

[CHAPTER CARD: How a One-Plate Planet Built the Solar System's Greatest Peak]

Looking at a 21.287-kilometer volcano ringed by an 8-kilometer cliff scarp and a 72-pascal summit, you might think Mars is only a frozen museum.

But Olympus Mons proves that when a rocky world loses plate tectonics and pulls with 38 percent of Earth's gravity, one mantle plume can build a mountain Earth can never match — while sheltering buried glacial ice for future explorers.

From Schiaparelli's 1835-to-1910 sightings of Nix Olympica, Mariner 9's 1971 reveal of a 27-kilometer, 600-kilometer-wide shield across 300,000 square kilometers, and Sjogren's March 9, 1979 8.7-times-10-to-the-21st-gram disk, to Smith, Zuber, Pettengill, Garvin, Solomon, and Phillips's May 28 and October 1, 1999, March 10, 2000, and March 30, 2001 maps of the 21.287-kilometer peak, 70-kilometer lithosphere, and 300-million-cubic-kilometer Tharsis magmas, and Neukum and Head's December 23, 2004 and March 17, 2005 discoveries of 2-million-year-old lava and 4-million-year-old glaciers, spacecraft unlocked the solar system's largest volcano.

Olympus Mons shows how gravity and crustal motion sculpt the tallest mountains in the cosmos — and next, we travel beyond Mars out to the Saturn system to ask why Saturn's moon Hyperion looks like a giant tumbling space sponge.
