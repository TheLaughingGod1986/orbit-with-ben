# Why Did NASA's Parker Solar Probe Fly Inside the Sun's Corona Without Melting?

Look at the white-light images NASA's Parker Solar Probe captured inside the solar atmosphere, and you see a spacecraft flying directly through glowing coronal streamers just 3.8 million miles from the Sun's surface at 430,000 miles per hour — fast enough to travel from Philadelphia to Washington, D.C., in 1 second.

Not because the spacecraft carries some impossible refrigerator, but because of a physical secret in the near-vacuum of the Sun's corona: particle density is so low that a 4.5-inch-thick carbon-composite heat shield blocks the intense sunlight and keeps the spacecraft body sitting in its shadow at a comfortable 85 degrees Fahrenheit.

What would you see and feel if you rode behind that 8-foot-wide shield into the mystery of a solar atmosphere where sunlight is about 475 times stronger than in orbit around Earth? If Earth sat at one end of a yardstick and the Sun at the other, you would skim within 4 inches of the solar surface while the front of your shield glowed near 2,500 degrees Fahrenheit and the instruments beside you stayed at room temperature. Now why doesn't a million-degree solar atmosphere melt the spacecraft on contact?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Vision of Eugene Parker]

In the mid-1950s at the University of Chicago, American astrophysicist Eugene Parker developed a mathematical theory predicting the solar wind — the constant supersonic outflow of solar material — and proposed an explanation for why the Sun's upper atmosphere, the corona, is hotter than the surface of the Sun itself.

On October 3, 2017, Eugene Parker visited the spacecraft bearing his name at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory in Laurel, Maryland — the first NASA mission ever named for a living researcher.

Then on August 12, 2018, at 7:31 UTC, Parker became the first person in history to witness the launch of a spacecraft carrying his name when a Delta IV-Heavy rocket lifted the 1,510-pound — 685-kilogram — probe from Cape Canaveral, Florida, nearly four years before he died on March 15, 2022, at age 94.

However, to answer the questions Eugene Parker raised more than 60 years ago, the probe had to fly more than 7 times closer to the Sun than any spacecraft before it. Now how can any machine survive inside an atmosphere heated to millions of degrees?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Oven and the Boiling Pot]

To understand what keeps the spacecraft safe, NASA thermal engineers start with the difference between temperature and heat: temperature measures how fast particles are moving, whereas heat measures the total amount of energy those particles transfer.

Inside the corona, particles move at speeds corresponding to several million degrees Fahrenheit, yet because space is mostly empty and the corona has very low density, very few particles collide with the spacecraft to transfer energy.

Think of the difference between putting your hand in a hot oven versus putting it in a pot of boiling water: in the oven, your hand withstands significantly hotter temperatures for longer than in the water, where it interacts with many more particles.

Then because the spacecraft interacts with so few hot coronal particles, the sun-facing surface of its heat shield only gets heated by sunlight to about 2,500 degrees Fahrenheit — about 1,400 degrees Celsius, comparable to volcanic lava between 1,300 and 2,200 degrees Fahrenheit — while the spacecraft body sits at 85 degrees Fahrenheit, or 30 degrees Celsius. [CHAPTER CARD: Four and a Half Inches of Carbon Foam]

Before Parker Solar Probe was built, you might have assumed a heat shield facing 475 times Earth's sunlight would have to be a heavy slab of solid metal.

Wrong.

Designed by the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory under lead thermal engineer Betsy Congdon and built at Carbon-Carbon Advanced Technologies, the Thermal Protection System is 8 feet — 2.4 meters — in diameter, 4.5 inches — 11.43 centimeters — thick, and weighs just 160 pounds, or 73 kilograms, sandwiching lightweight carbon-composite foam between two carbon-carbon plates finished with white ceramic paint and tested to 3,000 degrees Fahrenheit, or 1,650 degrees Celsius.

To keep its two solar arrays — spanning 17.2 square feet and producing 388 watts, enough to run a kitchen blender — from overheating as they retract into the shield's shadow, pumps circulate about 1 gallon — 3.7 liters — of pressurized deionized water between 50 and 257 degrees Fahrenheit with the cooling power of an average living room.

However, because light takes 8 minutes to reach Earth, half-cell-phone-sized sensors along the shadow's edge steer the spacecraft autonomously if sunlight touches them. Now what happens to the one sensor that has to poke out past the shield into the raw solar wind?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Cup That Pokes Past the Shield]

Poking out over the heat shield, the Solar Probe Cup — a Faraday cup in the instrument suite led by principal investigator Justin Kasper at the University of Michigan — faces the solar atmosphere directly to measure ion and electron fluxes and flow angles.

To survive outside the shadow, the cup itself is made from sheets of Titanium-Zirconium-Molybdenum alloy with a melting point of about 4,260 degrees Fahrenheit — 2,349 degrees Celsius — while its electric-field grids are made from tungsten, the metal with the highest known melting point at 6,192 degrees Fahrenheit, or 3,422 degrees Celsius, etched with acid because lasers could not cut the gridlines.

Then to keep its wiring from melting, engineers grew sapphire crystal tubes to suspend wires made of niobium, and tested the cup under a particle accelerator, jury-rigged IMAX projectors, and the 10,000 adjustable mirrors of the Odeillo Solar Furnace — where the radiation burned away contamination so the cup cleaned itself. [CHAPTER CARD: Crossing the Alfvén Critical Surface]

Unlike Earth, the Sun has no solid surface, and its superheated atmosphere stays bound by gravity and magnetism only up to the Alfvén critical surface — named for Swedish plasma physicist Hannes Alfvén — where escaping solar plasma becomes the solar wind and moves so fast that waves can never travel back to the Sun.

Using 7 Venus flybys to shrink its orbit, Parker Solar Probe cut across the Alfvén critical surface on April 28, 2021, during its eighth flyby at 18.8 solar radii — 8.127 million miles — above the solar surface, touching the Sun for the first time.

As the probe dipped to 14.97 solar radii — 6.47 million miles — it entered a coronal ribbon called a pseudostreamer, where particles slowed and quieted like the eye of a storm, while instruments led by Stuart Bale at the University of California, Berkeley traced magnetic zig-zags called switchbacks — spotted at 34 solar radii in 2019 and narrowed inside 25 solar radii — to helium-rich magnetic funnels between supergranule convection cells. [CHAPTER CARD: Twenty-Nine Dives and the Orbit Ahead]

You might wonder from dramatic headlines whether skimming 3.8 million miles from the Sun means Parker Solar Probe is losing altitude on every loop and is about to crash into the solar surface.

Here is why orbital mechanics prove that won't happen: Parker Solar Probe only shrank its perihelion during its 7 gravity-assist flybys of Venus, which placed the spacecraft into its final orbit for its record approach on December 24, 2024 at 3.8 million miles — 8.86 solar radii — and 430,000 miles per hour.

With no more Venus flybys to pull its orbit lower, Parker has matched that exact 3.8-million-mile distance and 430,000-mile-per-hour speed across 7 more passes through its 29th close approach on September 4, 2026 — sampling almost 40 percent of the solar circumference within 1 day during a 9-day autonomous blackout, while models and barrier-blanket readings show its heat shield reaches only about 1,700 degrees Fahrenheit with zero degradation, earning a mission extension into 2029.
