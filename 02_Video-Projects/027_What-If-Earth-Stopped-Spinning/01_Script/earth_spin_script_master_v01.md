# What If Earth Stopped Spinning?

Right now, you are moving. If you are in Britain, the ground under you is carrying you east at about a thousand kilometres an hour. At the equator it is faster than a jet airliner. And you feel nothing at all.

So what would happen if Earth stopped spinning? Not slowly. All at once.

In this film I will show you how fast you are really going, what a sudden stop would do, what a slow stop would do, and the strange part: Earth is already slowing down, and your clocks have been keeping score.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Speed You Cannot Feel]

Why don't you feel it? Because the speed is steady. Sit in a smooth airliner and you can pour a drink without spilling a drop. Nothing pushes you, because nothing is changing. Your body only notices a change in speed, never the speed itself.

You can still see the spin, every single day. The Sun does not rise. Your side of Earth turns towards it. Earth turns from west to east, and that is why the Sun always comes up in the east, and why the stars wheel across the sky at night.

The spin does leave clues. Because the ground at the equator swings round so fast, you weigh about half of one percent less there than at the poles. And the whole planet bulges. Earth is about forty-three kilometres wider across the equator than it is from pole to pole.

In 1851, in Paris, Léon Foucault hung a heavy ball from a wire sixty-seven metres long and set it swinging. Over the hours, the line of its swing slowly turned. The pendulum was not turning. The floor was. Anyone could stand there and watch Earth spin without looking at the sky.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Sudden Stop]

Now imagine the impossible. Earth's rock stops turning in an instant. But the air does not. Nothing has told it to stop.

At the equator the atmosphere would keep going east at more than one and a half thousand kilometres an hour. That is faster than the speed of sound, and several times faster than the strongest gust of wind ever measured. The oceans would keep moving too, and rise up against every eastern coast.

Why east? Because that is the way we were all already going. Anything not fixed to the ground would carry on east as well, just as you lurch forwards when a bus brakes hard. Here in Britain the wind would arrive at about a thousand kilometres an hour.

But here is a strange detail. Stand exactly at the North Pole, and you would hardly notice. You were barely moving in the first place. You were just turning on the spot, once a day.

And it cannot happen. There is no brake. The energy stored in Earth's spin is as much as all the sunlight that reaches Earth in about forty thousand years. Nothing in our solar system could take that away in a moment.

So a sudden stop is a thought experiment. What about a slow one?

[CHAPTER CARD: A Slow Stop]

Suppose Earth slowed down gently, over thousands of years, until it did not turn at all.

Remember that bulge at the equator? The spin is what holds the oceans out there. Without it, the water would drain away towards the poles. Mapmakers have modelled it. The seas gather into two great polar oceans, and a single enormous band of dry land wraps around the middle of the world.

And the day itself would change. With no spin at all, the Sun would cross the sky just once as Earth went round it. One day would last a whole year: six months of daylight, then six months of night. The day side would bake under a Sun that never sets, and the night side would freeze in the dark, with the wind always pouring from one side to the other.

We have a neighbour that comes close. Venus turns once every two hundred and forty-three Earth days, and it turns backwards. Its day is longer than its year. If you saw our Venus film, you know how strange that world became.

[CHAPTER CARD: Earth Is Already Slowing]

Here is the twist. Earth is slowing down already, right now.

The Moon pulls on our oceans and raises the tides. As Earth turns beneath those tides, the water drags a little against the seabed, like a hand resting on a spinning wheel. Every day, a tiny amount of spin is lost.

Where does it go? Into the Moon. The tides tug the Moon forwards, and it drifts about four centimetres further from us every year. If you saw our Moon film, this is the other half of that story.

And the Moon shows us where this road leads. Long ago, Earth's pull slowed the Moon's own spin until one turn took exactly one orbit. That is why the Moon always shows us the same face. It did not stop spinning. It got locked.

How do we know? Ancient eclipses. Astronomers in Babylon, China and medieval Europe wrote down where and when the Sun went dark. If Earth had kept spinning at today's rate, those eclipses would have been seen from places hundreds or thousands of kilometres away. The records only fit if the day has been growing longer, by nearly two thousandths of a second every century.

That sounds like nothing. But give it time. Corals lay down a fine growth line every day, and a thicker band each year. Fossil corals about three hundred and eighty million years old show about four hundred daily lines in a year. Back then, a day was only about twenty-two hours long.

[VISUAL MUST: A real fossil coral under a lens (museum or USGS imagery), growth lines visible. Then a text-free code graphic: a clock dial whose hand sweeps a slightly shorter day, then today's 24 hours. Then a real clay tablet from a museum collection.]
[TEACH: Tidal friction transfers Earth's spin to the Moon's orbit (the Moon recedes about 3.8 cm a year; see film 013). Eclipse records from 720 BC to AD 2015 give an average lengthening of the day of about 1.8 ms per century (Stephenson, Morrison and Hohenkerk 2016). Devonian corals show about 400 daily growth lines per year, giving a day of about 22 hours (Wells 1963). The Moon is tidally locked: its rotation period equals its orbital period, 27.3 days.]

[CHAPTER CARD: Keeping Score]

So who notices two thousandths of a second? Your phone does.

Since the late 1960s the second has been set by atomic clocks, which do not care how fast Earth turns. But the day, measured by the Sun, does not quite fit the clocks. So since 1972, timekeepers have added an extra second to the world's clocks twenty-seven times, to keep noon where it belongs. The last one came at the end of 2016.

And the day wobbles. Earth's molten core, its oceans and even melting ice all nudge the spin. In the last few years the planet has actually been running a touch fast, and some days have come in more than a millisecond short. Timekeepers now plan to stop adding leap seconds by 2035.

So Earth will never stop spinning. Even the Moon's slow brake would need tens of billions of years to lock our day to its month, and long before then the Sun will have changed beyond recognition. Your days will stay twenty-four hours, to within a blink.

What if the most surprising thing about your day is not that it might end, but that it has always been changing?

The bigger question is the one underneath all of this. Something set Earth spinning in the first place, about four and a half billion years ago. Every sunrise you have ever seen is that first push, still running down.
