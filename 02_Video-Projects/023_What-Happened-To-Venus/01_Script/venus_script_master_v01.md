# What Happened to Venus?

Venus is the planet most like Earth. So what happened to it?

This is the ground on Venus, seen through the clouds by radar. Why radar? Because ordinary light cannot get through. It is hot enough to melt lead. The air presses down like the sea nearly a kilometre deep. No machine we have landed there could survive it for much more than two hours.

And this world is almost the size of yours. It is made of the same rock. It even takes in less sunlight than Earth does, and it is still the hottest planet in the solar system. In this film I will show you how that can be true, where its water went, and why the answer is still open.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Twin Next Door]

Start with what the two planets share.

Venus is ninety-five percent as wide as Earth, with about four-fifths of its mass. It formed from the same kind of rock, in the same young solar system, at nearly the same time. From a distance, it is the closest thing to a second Earth that we know.

It is also closer to the Sun. Venus receives almost twice the sunlight that reaches us. So the easy answer is that it is simply nearer the fire. But that answer breaks on one number.

Venus is wrapped in bright cloud that reflects about three-quarters of that sunlight straight back to space. So Venus actually soaks up less sunlight than Earth does. Less light in, and still a surface at four hundred and sixty-five degrees. It sounds like a paradox. It stops being one when you look at the air.

So the heat is not about how much light arrives. It is about what happens to the heat once it is there. It cannot get out.

What would you see if you stood there? Not darkness. The clouds let through a dim, orange daylight, like a heavy evening that never ends. You would never see the Sun itself. The wind at the ground moves at little more than a slow walk, but in air that thick, even a slow wind pushes like a river.

[CHAPTER CARD: A Blanket With No Way Out]

The air of Venus is ninety-six percent carbon dioxide, and there is a great deal of it.

Carbon dioxide lets sunlight through but blocks the heat trying to leave. On Earth it is a thin trace in the air, and even that trace keeps the planet warm. On Venus the whole sky is made of it, ninety times thicker than ours. The heat goes in. It does not come out. Day and night, pole and equator, the ground sits at nearly the same temperature.

Here is the turn. Earth has roughly as much carbon dioxide as Venus. It is just not in the air.

It is in rock. Limestone, chalk and seabed, built over billions of years. Rain and rivers carry it to the sea, and sea life and chemistry lock it into the floor. The thing that pulled Earth's carbon out of the sky was water.

So the question changes. It is not why Venus has so much carbon dioxide. It is why Venus has no water to put it away.

The water is the mystery. And Venus left a clue in the air about where it went.

[CHAPTER CARD: Where Did the Water Go?]

In 1978 a NASA probe fell through the clouds of Venus and tasted the air on the way down. It found a trace of water, and something odd inside it.

Some hydrogen is heavier than the rest. It is called deuterium. On Venus, the share of heavy hydrogen is around a hundred times higher than on Earth.

That number is a fingerprint. High in the air, sunlight breaks water apart. The light hydrogen escapes to space more easily than the heavy kind. Lose enough water that way, over a long enough time, and the heavy hydrogen is what remains. Venus has lost a great deal of water.

How much, and in what form, is still unknown. Was it ever an ocean?

Spin matters here. Venus turns once every two hundred and forty-three Earth days, and it turns backwards. A single day there is longer than its whole year. That slow turn decides where the clouds gather.

One model says it may have been. In 2016 a team at NASA's Goddard Institute ran early Venus with that slow spin and found that thick daytime clouds could shade the ground. In their model, Venus may have had shallow oceans and mild weather for billions of years.

Another model says the water never came down. In 2021 a team led by Martin Turbet found that clouds gathered on the night side instead, trapping heat like a lid. The steam stayed in the sky and the oceans may never have formed.

Both are careful models, and they disagree. Nobody has yet read the rocks that could settle it.

Either way, Venus ended in the same place. Water gone. Carbon in the sky. Heat with nowhere to go.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Line Earth Hasn't Crossed]

So why Venus and not Earth?

Distance is part of it. Nearer the Sun, there is more light to start the climb. Warmer air holds more water vapour, and water vapour is itself a greenhouse gas. Past a certain line, the steam reaches high enough for sunlight to split it, and the water starts leaking away to space. Venus sits on the wrong side of that line.

Earth sits on the safe side, for now. Last week we saw the Sun getting brighter, about one percent every hundred and ten million years. That climb moves the line outward. In about a billion years, it reaches Earth.

This is not a warning about next century. The warming measured on Earth today is real, and it is a different ledger. Studies of Earth's climate find that burning our fossil fuels is very unlikely to start a full runaway like this. Venus is a story told in hundreds of millions of years.

What it shows is how thin the difference is. Same rock, same start, and one turn with the water.

[CHAPTER CARD: Going Back]

The answer is waiting on the ground.

Venus has repainted most of its surface with lava in the last billion years or so. But some high, crumpled regions called tesserae look older. If water ever shaped Venus, rocks like those may still remember it.

People have landed there. In 1982 a Soviet lander photographed the ground and lasted about two hours before the heat won. In 2021 a probe on its way to the Sun looked at the night side of Venus and saw the faint glow of the hot surface through the clouds.

The next step is to read the rocks properly. NASA chose two missions, DAVINCI to drop through the air and photograph the old highlands of Alpha Regio, and VERITAS to map the surface and its rock types from orbit. Europe's EnVision is planned to follow.

The planet next door is the clearest record we have of how a world like ours can turn. Not by falling apart. By keeping its heat, and losing its water.

What if Venus was never the monster, but a twin that simply ran the experiment first?

The bigger question hangs in your own sky, the brightest thing at dawn or dusk after the Moon. How close did Earth come to the same turn?
