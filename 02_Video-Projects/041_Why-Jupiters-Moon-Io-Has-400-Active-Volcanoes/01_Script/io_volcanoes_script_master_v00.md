# Why Does Jupiter's Moon Io Have 400 Active Volcanoes?

High in orbit above the yellow, orange, and black plains of Jupiter's moon Io, as you follow the gravity signal recorded when NASA's Juno spacecraft dived within one thousand five hundred kilometres of its surface, why is a rocky moon only slightly larger than our Moon blasting lava seeking an escape route from four hundred active volcanoes?

Jupiter's rocky moon Io is the most volcanically active world in the solar system — home to an estimated four hundred volcanoes that shoot plumes up to three hundred and thirty kilometres into space, fill impact craters with lakes of molten silicate lava, and turn the moon into a four-hundred-thousand-volt electric generator.

Why did an eight-March-1979 photograph taken four point five million kilometres away reveal the first active volcano beyond Earth, what if a gravitational tug-of-war heaved solid ground up and down by one hundred metres beneath your feet, and how did Juno solve a forty-four-year mystery about what hides deeper beneath Io's crust?

[CHAPTER CARD: Four Dots Around Jupiter]

7 January 1610. Pointing his telescope at Jupiter, Italian astronomer Galileo Galilei spotted points of light beside the giant planet — unable to differentiate between Io and Europa until the next night, 8 January 1610, when he discovered Io alongside three other Jovian moons.

That was the first time a moon was discovered orbiting a planet other than Earth, proving that planets orbit the Sun. Galileo called them the Medicean planets and numbered them I, II, III, and IV until the names Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto were officially adopted in the mid-1800s.

However, at three thousand six hundred and forty-three kilometres across — compared with three thousand four hundred and seventy-five kilometres for Earth's Moon and twelve thousand seven hundred and fifty-six kilometres for Earth — Io is about one-quarter the diameter of Earth, with a mean surface temperature of minus one hundred and fifty-five degrees Celsius.

Orbiting four hundred and twenty-two thousand kilometres — two hundred and sixty-two thousand miles — from Jupiter, Io whips around the giant planet every forty-two point five hours, or one point eight days, at seventeen point three kilometres per second. Why isn't this small world frozen solid?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Umbrella Cloud on the Limb]

8 March 1979. Three days after its historic encounter with Jupiter, NASA's Voyager 1 looked back four point five million kilometres — two point six million miles — at Io, where Jupiter looms almost forty times larger and two hundred times brighter in Io's night sky than our full Moon.

Analysing that image at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, engineer and imaging scientist Linda Morabito spotted a bright curved volcanic cloud rising more than two hundred and sixty kilometres — one hundred and fifty miles — above Io's limb, while a second plume caught the rising Sun along the terminator.

But nobody had seen anything like it before: Morabito had discovered the first extraterrestrial volcanic eruption beyond Earth, with volcanoes so powerful they are seen with large telescopes on Earth.

Then in 2007, as NASA's New Horizons flew by Jupiter, it captured a five-frame sequence over eight minutes showing a plume erupting from Io's Tvashtar volcano three hundred and thirty kilometres — two hundred miles — high. What hidden engine melts this moon from within?

[CHAPTER CARD: Solid Rock Rising One Hundred Metres]

Although Io always points the same side toward Jupiter in its one-point-eight-day orbit at four hundred and twenty-two thousand kilometres, it is caught in a tug-of-war with two neighbouring moons: Europa, orbiting every three point six days at six hundred and seventy-one thousand kilometres, and Ganymede, orbiting every seven point two days at one million and seventy thousand kilometres.

However, those precisely timed pulls from Europa and Ganymede perturb Io's orbit into an irregularly elliptical one. As Io's distance from Jupiter varies, Jupiter's gravity relentlessly squeezes the moon in an extreme case of tidal flexing.

Then those tidal forces cause Io's solid surface to bulge up and down by as much as one hundred metres — three hundred and thirty feet.

On Earth, where tides are highest, the difference between low and high tide is only eighteen metres — sixty feet — and that is for water, whereas Io's hundred-metre tides heave solid ground, keeping much of its subsurface crust liquid and filling impact craters with molten lava lakes. [CHAPTER CARD: A Two-Hundred-Kilometre Lake of Obsidian Glass]

On 30 December 2023 and 3 February 2024, NASA's solar-powered Juno spacecraft made two close flybys of Io within about one thousand five hundred kilometres — nine hundred and thirty miles — before a 9 April 2024 flyby within sixteen thousand five hundred kilometres.

In findings announced on 16 April 2024, Juno captured close-ups of a two-hundred-kilometre-long — one-hundred-and-twenty-seven-mile-long — lava lake called Loki Patera, with islands embedded in a lake rimmed with hot lava where specular reflection shows parts of the surface are as smooth as obsidian glass.

Then in findings published on 22 July 2026, Juno's six-antenna Microwave Radiometer — detecting wavelengths from one point three to fifty-one centimetres — probed from inches down to tens of feet, finding temperatures rising by more than forty degrees Fahrenheit just several feet into the surface.

Now that heat represents either a conductive flow of one to three watts per square metre — like a small nightlight under every square yard, releasing up to thirty times Earth's average energy — or cooling lava flows capped by nine to eleven metres of crust covering ten percent of Io, alongside smooth ash plains stretching one hundred kilometres or more. [CHAPTER CARD: The Forty-Four-Year Magma Ocean Mystery]

Wrong. For forty-four years after Linda Morabito's 1979 discovery, planetary scientists wondered whether Io's volcanoes were fed by a shallow global ocean of white-hot magma or by localized sources.

During the December 2023 and February 2024 approaches within one thousand five hundred kilometres, Juno used NASA's Deep Space Network to acquire dual-frequency Doppler data — measuring Io's gravity by tracking how tidal flexing affected the spacecraft's acceleration.

But if Io had a global magma ocean, its tidal deformation would be much larger than a more rigid, mostly solid interior.

When the Juno team published their study in Nature on 12 December 2024, they found tidal deformation consistent with Io not having a shallow global magma ocean — proving its estimated four hundred volcanoes are each likely powered by their own chamber of roiling hot magma. [CHAPTER CARD: The Four-Hundred-Thousand-Volt Moon]

That brings us to a final paradox: orbiting four hundred and twenty-two thousand kilometres from Jupiter, how does a rocky moon inflate Jupiter's magnetosphere to over twice the size we would expect?

Cutting across Jupiter's magnetic lines of force, Io turns into an electric generator — developing four hundred thousand volts across itself and creating an electric current of three million amperes along Jupiter's magnetic field lines to create lightning in Jupiter's upper atmosphere.

As Jupiter rotates, its magnetic field sweeps past Io and strips off about one ton — one thousand kilograms — of Io's material every second, ionizing it into a doughnut-shaped radiation cloud called a plasma torus.

Now ions escaping that torus create auroras in Jupiter's upper atmosphere and inflate Jupiter's magnetosphere to over twice the size we would expect.

Next, we turn from Jupiter's volcanic moon to our four-point-six-billion-year-old Sun — which in about five billion years will run out of energy and expand into a red giant that will engulf Mercury and Venus, and possibly Earth as well.
