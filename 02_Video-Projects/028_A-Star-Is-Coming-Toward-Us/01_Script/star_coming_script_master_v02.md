# A Star Is Coming Into Our Solar System

There is a star coming towards us. So how close will it get, and should you worry?

This is not a guess, and not a rumour. The star has been measured, and it is heading almost straight at the Sun. Its name is Gliese 710. In about one point three million years it will pass closer to us than any star we know of. It will not hit anything. But it will sail right through the outer edge of our solar system.

In this film I will show you where that star is now, how we found out how close it will come, what it will pass through, and what our night sky will look like when it arrives.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Star Heading Our Way]

Right now, Gliese 710 is about sixty-two light-years away, in the constellation of Serpens, the serpent. It is an orange dwarf, a little smaller and cooler than the Sun, with about sixty percent of the Sun's mass.

You cannot see it. It is far too faint for your eyes on even the darkest night. You would need binoculars just to find it.

So why does it matter? Because of the way it moves. Most stars drift slowly sideways across our sky, year after year. Gliese 710 hardly drifts at all. It is coming towards us at about fourteen kilometres every second, more than fifty thousand kilometres an hour, and almost none of that motion is sideways.

When something barely moves across your view but keeps getting closer, it is heading for you. Like a car on a long straight road at night: its headlights do not slide left or right. They just grow.

If you saw our film on the nearest star, you met Proxima Centauri, about four light-years away. Proxima is our nearest neighbour today. But stars move, and the title of "nearest" does not last forever.

[CHAPTER CARD: How Close Will It Come?]

How do we know where it will be in a million years? For a long time, we did not. We were not even sure it would come close at all.

In the late nineteen-nineties, a satellite called Hipparcos measured the star's position and motion. The best guess then was that it would pass about a light-year from the Sun, and the uncertainty was huge.

Then came Gaia, the European spacecraft that has measured more than a billion stars. With every new release of its data, the path came into sharper focus. And the star kept coming closer.

The latest Gaia measurements put the closest approach at about a fifth of a light-year. That is about ten to thirteen thousand times further than Earth is from the Sun. Five times closer than the old estimate.

That still sounds far. So let's shrink it. Make the gap between the Sun and Earth one centimetre. Neptune, our outermost planet, is then thirty centimetres away, about the length of a ruler. And Gliese 710 passes more than a hundred metres away, the length of a football pitch.

So the star will not come anywhere near the planets. But what is out there, a hundred metres from the Sun? Something we have never seen.

[CHAPTER CARD: Into the Oort Cloud]

Far beyond the planets, the Sun is wrapped in a vast cloud of ice. Trillions of frozen bodies, the leftovers from when the planets formed, drifting so far out that the Sun's pull on them is barely a whisper.

It is called the Oort Cloud, and it is still one of the great unknowns of our solar system. Nobody has ever seen it directly. We know it is there because of the comets that fall out of it, on journeys that take them hundreds of thousands of years or more.

Its inner edge is thought to start a few thousand times further out than Earth. Its outer edge may reach a hundred thousand. Sunlight, which gets to us in eight minutes, would take months to reach the place where Gliese 710 will pass.

And that is exactly where the star is going: straight through the Oort Cloud.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Comet Shower]

A star passing through the cloud is like a hand brushing through a bowl of floating marbles. Its gravity nudges the comets nearest its path. Some get flung out into the galaxy. Others are knocked onto new orbits that fall in towards the Sun.

The calculations suggest that Gliese 710 will send about ten extra comets a year falling in close enough for us to see. And it will keep happening for three to four million years.

But here is the strange part. The comets do not arrive while the star is passing. Why? Because they are so far out that falling in takes hundreds of thousands of years. By the time the shower is at its height, Gliese 710 will already be heading away again, shrinking back into the dark.

Should anyone worry? Not much. Most comets that fall in never come close to Earth. Jupiter and Saturn throw many of them back out, or steer them away. What the people of that far future get is a sky with more comets in it, for a very long time.

[CHAPTER CARD: A New Brightest Star]

So what would you see? Imagine standing on Earth on the night of the closest pass.

A new star has been brightening for thousands of years. Now it is the brightest star in the whole night sky, about three times brighter than Sirius, and as bright as Jupiter at its best. And it glows orange.

It will not light up the night like the Moon, and it will not warm anyone. It is still billions of times fainter than our Sun. But it will move. Most stars look fixed for a lifetime. This one will creep across the sky by about the width of the full Moon every thirty-five years.

Then, slowly, it will fade, and drift away for good.

[CHAPTER CARD: It Has Happened Before]

Is this the first time a star has come this close? Not at all. Gliese 710 will not be the first star to pass this way.

About seventy thousand years ago, a dim red pair of stars now called Scholz's star skimmed through the outer Oort Cloud, less than a light-year from the Sun. It was too small and too faint to stir up much.

And about two point eight million years ago, a star much like our Sun, called HD 7977, may have passed as close as Gliese 710 will. Gaia's measurements cannot yet pin down exactly how close.

So this is not a rare disaster waiting to happen. It is how life works in a galaxy. The Sun is one star among billions, all of them moving, and every few million years one of them brushes past.

What if the night sky you have always known is not fixed at all, but a slow traffic of stars, still on the move?

The bigger question is the cloud itself. Some astronomers think the Sun was born in a crowd of sister stars, and that many of the comets in our Oort Cloud were once theirs. If they are right, every star that passes is part of a very old exchange. Some of the ice falling in from the dark may have started out around another sun.
