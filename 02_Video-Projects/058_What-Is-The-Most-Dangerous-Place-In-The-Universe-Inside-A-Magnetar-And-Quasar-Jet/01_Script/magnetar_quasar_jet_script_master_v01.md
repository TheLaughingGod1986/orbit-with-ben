# Why Are Magnetars and Quasar Jets the Most Dangerous Places in the Universe?

Look out beyond the far orbit and icy horizon of Jupiter's moon Callisto into the deep cosmos, where the crushed cores of dead stars and feeding supermassive black holes create danger that can strip atoms apart and outshine galaxies. Why can a neutron star just 20 kilometers — 12 miles — across become a 1-quadrillion-gauss magnetar whose flare rattled Earth's atmosphere, and what secret makes a black hole's billion-degree corona and 40-trillion-Sun quasar jet even deadlier across 26 million light-years? Because when gravity compresses half a million Earths into a sphere the length of Manhattan Island or feeds 800 million to 12 billion solar masses into an accretion disk, magnetic fields amplify into the most violent engines in space.

Not because these cosmic monsters hunt down worlds, but because the physical mystery of magnetic reconnection and relativistic particle jets turns stellar collapse and black hole gravity into radiation beams that sterilize vast regions of space.

What would you see and feel hovering near a magnetar whose 0.2-second giant flare suddenly releases as much energy as our Sun radiates in 250,000 years, or looking deeper down a quasar jet firing particles at close to the speed of light across hundreds of thousands of light-years? Even though a magnetar spans only 20 kilometers across, its 100-trillion to 1-quadrillion-gauss field — 10 to the 14th to 10 to the 15th gauss — is at least 10 times stronger than a normal pulsar's. Now how does a dying star build the strongest magnet in the universe?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Manhattan-Sized Star with a Quadrillion-Gauss Field]

When a massive star runs out of fuel, collapses under its own weight, and explodes as a supernova, its crushed core becomes a neutron star packing 500,000 Earths into a sphere 20 kilometers — 12 miles — across, the length of Manhattan Island.

As astrophysicists Robert C. Duncan — born in 1942 — and Chryssa Kouveliotou — born in 1953 — showed, soft gamma-ray repeaters and anomalous X-ray pulsars are each a 20-kilometer magnetar: a neutron star rotating every 5 to 12 seconds with a surface magnetic field of 100 trillion to 1 quadrillion gauss — 10 to the 14th to 10 to the 15th gauss.

In August 1998, a giant flare from magnetar SGR 1900+14 blasted a dust-free infrared cavity ring into its surrounding star cluster, imaged by NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope.

And in October 2022, NASA's NICER on the International Space Station and NuSTAR watched magnetar SGR 1935+2154 rapidly slow its spin as it lost mass and fired a laser-like fast radio burst — releasing in a fraction of a second as much energy as our Sun radiates in 1 year. Then if one burst rivals a year of sunlight, what happens when a magnetar's crust snaps at once?

[CHAPTER CARD: The 0.2-Second Flare That Hit Earth's Atmosphere]

On December 27, 2004, spacecraft across the solar system — including NASA's Swift observatory — were suddenly blinded by a 380-second gamma-ray wave from magnetar SGR 1806-20.

As space physicist Kevin Hurley, astrophysicist David M. Palmer — born in 1962 — and Swift principal investigator Neil Gehrels — who lived from 1952 to 2017 — reported in *Nature* on April 28, 2005, SGR 1806-20 suffered a global crust failure and magnetic reconnection.

In its first 0.2 seconds, that 0.2-second giant flare released as much energy as our Sun radiates in a quarter of a million years — 250,000 years — reaching a total isotropic energy of 2 times 10 to the 46th ergs, 100 times higher than any prior giant flare and 1,000 times a typical burst.

Then in the same April 28, 2005 *Nature* issue, radio astronomer Bryan M. Gaensler — born in 1973 — and Chryssa Kouveliotou showed that the blast caused an ionospheric disturbance in Earth's upper atmosphere and, from day 6 to day 19, drove a polarized radio nebula of 4 times 10 to the 43rd ergs expanding at a quarter of the speed of light, 500 times brighter than any earlier afterglow and visible as a short gamma-ray burst out to 40 megaparsecs. Now what happens when two neutron stars crash together?

[CHAPTER CARD: When Two Neutron Stars Collide in 1.7 Seconds]

You might assume that a magnetar's 0.2-second crust quake is the fiercest outburst a neutron star can produce.

Wrong.

On August 17, 2017, at 12:41:04 UTC, the Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo detectors observed GW170817 with a signal-to-noise ratio of 32.4 — the inspiral of two neutron stars in galaxy NGC 4993, located 40 megaparsecs, or 130 million light-years, from Earth.

As LIGO and Virgo reported in *Physical Review Letters* on October 20, 2017 and October 19, 2018, the two colliding stars had radii of 11.9 kilometers each and masses of 1.17 to 1.60 Suns — totaling 2.74 solar masses — with core pressures of 3.5 times 10 to the 34th dynes per square centimeter at twice nuclear saturation density.

Then 1.7 seconds after coalescence, NASA's Fermi telescope detected GRB 170817A — a 1.7-second gamma-ray burst arrival — and as Caltech astronomers Gregg Hallinan and Mansi M. Kasliwal reported in *Science* on December 22, 2017, radio observations 16 days later revealed an ultrarelativistic jet and kilonova cocoon forging heavy elements across space. Now what cosmic engine keeps firing a lethal beam for millions of years?

[CHAPTER CARD: Inside a Quasar's Billion-Degree Corona and 40-Trillion-Sun Jet]

Even a magnetar flare or neutron star merger is a brief flash, but the most persistently dangerous place in the universe is a quasar — a feeding supermassive black hole whose event horizon casts a shadow roughly twice as big as the black hole's surface, surrounded by an accretion disk and a turbulent billion-degree magnetic corona.

In 1918, astronomer Heber Doust Curtis — who lived from 1872 to 1942 — noticed "a curious straight ray" shooting from galaxy Messier 87, 55 million light-years from Earth, and in 1963 astronomer Maarten Schmidt — who lived from 1929 to 2022 — measured quasar 3C 273 at 2.4 billion light-years, or 749 megaparsecs, outshining its host galaxy by more than 16 times.

Near the inner accretion disk, magnetic fields suddenly reroute infalling gas into twin particle jets blasting outward at close to the speed of light across hundreds of thousands of light-years.

Then on February 26, 2015, astronomer Xue-Bing Wu and colleagues reported in *Nature* quasar SDSS J0100+2802 at redshift 6.30, whose 12-billion-solar-mass black hole carved an ionized proximity zone 26 million light-years across, and on January 25, 2018, Eduardo Bañados and colleagues reported in *Nature* quasar ULAS J1342+0928 at redshift 7.54 — an 800-million-solar-mass black hole shining at 40 trillion — 4 times 10 to the 13th — solar luminosities in a 40-trillion-Sun quasar jet when the universe was 690 million years old, just 5 percent of its current age. Now how does Earth survive in a cosmos with engines this extreme?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why Distance Is Our Greatest Shield]

Looking at a 1-quadrillion-gauss magnetar or a 40-trillion-Sun quasar, you might think the universe is too violent for living worlds.

But the same extreme physics that makes those places lethal also forged heavy elements and lit up the earliest galaxies.

Across a century of discovery — from Heber Curtis's 1918 M87 jet at 55 million light-years and Maarten Schmidt's 1963 quasar 3C 273 at 2.4 billion light-years, to Duncan and Kouveliotou's 20-kilometer magnetars of 500,000 Earth masses and August 1998 SGR 1900+14 ring, SGR 1806-20's December 27, 2004 0.2-second giant flare of 250,000 Sun-years and 2 times 10 to the 46th ergs reported on April 28, 2005, Wu's February 26, 2015 12-billion-solar-mass quasar ionizing 26 million light-years, the August 17 and December 22, 2017 and October 19, 2018 measurements of the 11.9-kilometer, 2.74-solar-mass, 1.7-second GW170817 merger at 40 megaparsecs, Bañados's January 25, 2018 800-million-solar-mass, 40-trillion-Sun quasar 690 million years after the Big Bang, and October 2022's SGR 1935+2154 burst — astronomers mapped the cosmic lethality ladder.

Those extreme beacons show how vast cosmic distances shield our quiet solar neighborhood — and next, we return from the deadliest places in the universe to our planetary backyard to ask why Mars built a volcano three times taller than Mount Everest, Olympus Mons.
