# Why Is There Ice on Mercury, the Planet Closest to the Sun?

If you stood on the cratered rim of Mercury's north pole — in orbit closest to the Sun — what would you see glittering inside the darkness below its horizon?

At an average distance of fifty-eight million kilometres from the Sun — zero point four astronomical units — sunlight on Mercury is up to seven times brighter than on Earth, baking daytime rock to four hundred and thirty degrees Celsius. Yet between 2011 and 2015, NASA's MESSENGER spacecraft verified a stunning paradox: deep polar craters in permanent shadow drop to minus one hundred and eighty degrees Celsius and harbour abundant water ice.

Why does a scorched world with a radius of two thousand four hundred and forty kilometres — slightly larger than Earth's Moon, or the size of a blueberry if Earth were a nickel — preserve water ice inside craters like one-hundred-and-twelve-kilometre-wide Prokofiev, what if you lived through a single Mercury day that lasts two Mercury years, and what dark secret insulates that ice from the Sun now?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Day That Lasts Two Years]

1965. For decades, astronomers assumed Mercury kept one face locked toward the Sun — until Italian mathematician Giuseppe Colombo at the University of Padua proved Mercury is trapped in a three-to-two spin-orbit resonance.

Mercury spins on its axis once every fifty-nine Earth days, but races around the Sun every eighty-eight Earth days — rotating three times for every two revolutions around the Sun.

Its egg-shaped orbit carries Mercury between forty-seven million and seventy million kilometres from the Sun at nearly forty-seven kilometres per second — faster than any other planet — where sunlight takes three point two minutes to arrive.

However, because of that spin and orbit, one Mercury solar day lasts one hundred and seventy-six Earth days — just over two years on Mercury.

Near closest approach, the morning Sun appears to rise briefly, then set, and then rise again. Why did it take a planetary slingshot to reach this world?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Slingshot to an Iron World]

3 November 1973. NASA's five-hundred-and-three-kilogram Mariner 10 launched — the first spacecraft sent to Mercury and the first to use a gravity assist, flying within five thousand seven hundred and sixty-eight kilometres of Venus on 5 February 1974 before diving deeper inward.

Across three flybys — skimming seven hundred and three kilometres above Mercury on 29 March 1974, forty-eight thousand and sixty-nine kilometres on 21 September 1974, and three hundred and twenty-seven kilometres on 16 March 1975 — Mariner 10 returned over two thousand seven hundred pictures covering nearly half the surface down to one hundred metres wide.

Those images revealed cliffs soaring up to a mile high that rose as Mercury's interior cooled and contracted, and magnetometers uncovered a deeper secret: an offset magnetic field with one per cent the strength of Earth's.

That field points to a partly molten metallic core with a radius of two thousand and seventy-four kilometres — eighty-five per cent of the planet's radius — beneath an outer shell only four hundred kilometres thick, making Mercury the second densest planet after Earth at five thousand four hundred and twenty-nine kilograms per cubic metre. Why did radar dishes on Earth spot an unknown anomaly at Mercury's poles sixteen years later?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Seven-Year Braking Maneuver]

1991. The Arecibo radio telescope in Puerto Rico beamed radar waves at Mercury, and then detected unusually radar-bright patches at the poles — reflecting radio waves like water ice inside large craters mapped by Mariner 10.

However, because Mariner 10 saw less than half the planet, scientists needed an orbiter to look straight down into those polar shadows.

On 3 August 2004, NASA launched the one-thousand-one-hundred-and-eight-kilogram MESSENGER spacecraft — led by Principal Investigator Sean Solomon — to become the first mission to orbit Mercury.

To brake against the acceleration of falling toward the Sun, MESSENGER spent six and a half years executing six gravity assists — one flyby of Earth in August 2005, two of Venus in October 2006 and June 2007 skimming three hundred and thirty-eight kilometres high, and three of Mercury in 2008 and 2009 — until entering a twelve-hour orbit of two hundred by nine thousand three hundred kilometres on 18 March 2011.

Before MESSENGER peered inside the craters, sceptics asked whether bright radar echoes on a four-hundred-and-thirty-degree planet were just cold silicate rock. Case closed? Were Mercury's poles dry rock?

[CHAPTER CARD: Shadowed Craters at the Poles]

Firing more than ten million laser pulses from its Mercury Laser Altimeter, counting neutrons with its Neutron Spectrometer, and acquiring over two hundred and fifty thousand images, MESSENGER delivered what Sean Solomon called a unanimous affirmative verdict: Mercury's polar deposits are dominantly water ice.

Why doesn't that ice boil away? The secret is geometry: Mercury's axis of rotation is tilted just two degrees with respect to its orbital plane.

Because Mercury spins nearly upright and has no atmosphere to carry heat, it experiences no seasons, and deep polar crater floors never see sunlight — staying near minus one hundred and eighty degrees Celsius.

Inside Prokofiev crater — a one-hundred-and-twelve-kilometre-wide basin near the north pole named in August 2012 for Russian composer Sergei Prokofiev — MESSENGER used faint sunlight scattered off crater rims to capture the first visible-wavelength images of water ice exposed right on the shadowed floor. [CHAPTER CARD: Dark Blankets and Strange Hollows]

That brings us to the biggest misconception about ice near the Sun. People assume polar ice must gleam bare on the surface everywhere.

Let us test that with physics. Across most radar-bright craters, MESSENGER's Neutron Spectrometer and Laser Altimeter revealed a hydrogen-rich layer of nearly pure water ice more than tens of centimetres thick buried deeper beneath a ten-to-twenty-centimetre surficial layer of unusually dark material.

Comets and volatile-rich asteroids delivered both water and complex organic compounds that darkened under radiation, forming a ten-to-twenty-centimetre thermal blanket that insulates buried ice.

Meanwhile, across the one-thousand-five-hundred-and-fifty-kilometre Caloris Basin and three-hundred-and-six-kilometre Rachmaninoff Basin — named for composer Sergei Rachmaninoff — MESSENGER found thousands of bright depressions called hollows, eighteen to one thousand six hundred metres wide and twenty-four metres or more deep.

Averaging just one hundred thousand years old inside four-billion-year-old craters, these hollows form now as heat and solar wind vaporise sulfur and other volatiles out of rock. How did MESSENGER's mission end — and what arrives next?

[CHAPTER CARD: Crash Into Mercury and the Next Arrival]

30 April 2015. After more than four years, four thousand one hundred and five orbits around Mercury, and a final hover campaign between five and thirty-five kilometres above the surface, MESSENGER ran out of propellant.

At 19:26 Universal Time, the spacecraft slammed into Mercury at fourteen thousand and eighty kilometres per hour in Suisei Planitia, carving a new crater estimated at fifteen metres wide.

Then, on 20 October 2018, the European Space Agency and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency launched BepiColombo — only the third mission to visit Mercury — carrying two orbiters that completed six Mercury flybys between October 2021 and January 2025 ahead of orbital insertion in November 2026.

Mercury showed that even on a world where sunlit rock bakes at four hundred and thirty degrees Celsius, a two-degree axial tilt and dark organic blankets can preserve pure water ice inside permanently shadowed polar craters.

Next, we travel twenty-six thousand light-years to the centre of the Milky Way — where multi-million-degree gas swirls around the supermassive black hole Sagittarius A-star, packing four million times the mass of the Sun.
