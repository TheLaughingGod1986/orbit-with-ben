### 2.5 Primary Scientific & Agency Fact Sheet (Pre-Verified for `SOURCES.md`)
- **The Kármán Line (`100 km`) vs the `80.47-km` (`50-mile`) US Boundary (`von Kármán 1957` / `1967` *The Wind and Beyond*; `McDowell 2018`, *Acta Astronautica* `151`, `668–677`; NASA Armstrong Fact Sheet *X-15*):**
  - Earth's atmosphere has a total mass of **`~5.15 × 10¹⁸ kg`** and thins exponentially with altitude (`barometric scale height H ≈ 8.5 km`, with **`50%` of atmospheric mass below `5.6 km`**, **`90%` below `16 km`**, and **`99.99997%` below `100 km`**).
  - In **1956–1957**, Hungarian-American physicist and aerospace engineer **Theodore von Kármán** (`1881–1963`) calculated the physical altitude where aerodynamic lift $L = \frac{1}{2} \rho v^2 S C_L$ and radiative skin-cooling limits can no longer support an aircraft without the aircraft reaching **Keplerian orbital velocity (`v_orb ≈ 7.84 km/s` / `28,200 km/h`)** where centrifugal force alone keeps it in orbit (`referencing Captain Iven C. Kincheloe Jr. [1928–1958], who flew the Bell X-2 rocket plane to 38.47 km [126,200 ft] on 7 September 1956`). Von Kármán's `1956–1957` calculation (`and 1967 autobiography The Wind and Beyond, Ch. 43`) placed this boundary near **`275,000 feet` (`~83.8 km` / `~52.1 statute miles`)**, which space-law pioneer **Andrew G. Haley** (`1904–1966`) named the **"von Kármán primary jurisdiction line"** in `1957`; in the `1960s`, the **Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (`FAI`)** adopted the rounded metric value of **`100 kilometres` (`62.14 statute miles` / `328,084 feet`, `FAI Sporting Code, Section 8`)** as the international **Kármán line** separating aeronautics (`airplanes`) from astronautics (`spaceflight`).
  - Meanwhile, the **US Air Force, NASA, and FAA** set the US astronaut boundary at **`50 statute miles` (`80.47 kilometres` / `264,000 feet`)**. During the `1959–1968` **North American `X-15`** hypersonic rocket-plane program (`199` flights), `8` pilots flew above `80.47 km` (`50 miles`) on `13` flights to earn astronaut wings, while NASA chief test pilot **Joseph A. Walker** (`1921–1966`) flew the `X-15` above the `100-km` Kármán line twice — reaching **`105.98 km` (`347,800 ft`) on `Flight 90` (`19 July 1963`)** and **`107.96 km` (`354,200 ft` / `67.08 miles`) on `Flight 91` (`22 August 1963`)**.
  - In **October 2018**, Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics astronomer **Jonathan C. McDowell** (`born 1960`, `McDowell 2018`, *Acta Astronautica* `151`, `668–677`, *"The edge of space: Revisiting the Karman Line"*) analyzed **`43,000` satellite orbital histories (`NORAD` catalog)** and re-evaluated the aerodynamic-to-orbital force ratio $\mathcal{K} = 1$ using realistic spacecraft ballistic coefficients (`B ≈ 0.005–0.05 m²/kg`, yielding `70–90 km`), demonstrating that elliptical satellites repeatedly survive perigees down to **`~80 km`** (`72–85 km`, whereas any satellite dipping below `~70–80 km` burns up on that single revolution) and proposing **`80 km`** as the physical and dynamical lower boundary of space.
- **Earth's Five Principal Atmospheric Layers (`U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976`, NOAA/NASA/USAF; NASA Earth Fact Sheet):**
  1. **`Troposphere` (`0 to ~8–18 km`; `~12 km` mean altitude):** Contains **`~75–80%` of Earth's atmospheric mass** and **`~99%` of its water vapour and aerosols**. Heated from below by Earth's surface, temperature drops at an average environmental lapse rate of **`6.5 °C per kilometre`** from `+15 °C` (`288.15 K`, `1,013.25 hPa` sea-level standard) down to **`-56.5 °C` (`216.65 K`)** at the **tropopause** (`above Mount Everest at 8.849 km`).
  2. **`Stratosphere` (`~12 to 50 km`):** Temperature *rises* with altitude (temperature inversion) from `-56.5 °C` up to **`-2.5 °C` (`270.65 K`)** at the `50-km` **stratopause** because the **ozone layer (`O₃`, concentrated at `15–35 km`)** absorbs solar ultraviolet (`UV-B` and `UV-C`, `200–315 nm`) radiation. At **`~19 kilometres` (`~62,000–63,000 feet`)**, air pressure drops to **`6.3 kPa` (`47 mmHg`)** — the **Armstrong limit** (`discovered in 1947 by US Air Force physician Harry G. Armstrong [1899–1983]`), where exposed water, tears, and saliva boil at normal human body temperature (`37 °C` / `98.6 °F`). High-altitude helium balloon jumpers in full pressure suits reached the upper stratosphere on three famous missions: **Joseph W. Kittinger Jr.** (`1928–2022`, `Excelsior III`, **`31.33 km` [`102,800 ft`]** on **16 August 1960**), **Felix Baumgartner** (`born 1969`, `Red Bull Stratos`, **`38.97 km` [`127,852 ft`]**, reaching **`1,357.6 km/h` [`843.6 mph` / `Mach 1.25`]** on **14 October 2012**), and **Alan Eustace** (`born 1957`, **`41.42 km` [`135,890 ft`]** world record on **24 October 2014**).
  3. **`Mesosphere` (`50 to ~85 km`):** With almost no ozone to absorb UV light and rapid radiative cooling by carbon dioxide (`CO₂`), temperature plunges with altitude to **`-85 °C to -90 °C` (`183–188 K`, and down to `-100 °C` / `173 K` at the summer polar mesopause)** — making the **`~85-km` mesopause the coldest natural place on Earth**. Here **noctilucent (polar mesospheric) ice clouds** form at `~76–85 km`, **red sprites** discharge at `50–90 km` above thunderstorms, and **`>99%` of incoming meteors** ablate and vaporize by ram-pressure compression heating (`1,650 °C+`) between **`100 and 75 km`**.
  4. **`Thermosphere` (`~85 to 600 km`, varying `500–1,000 km` with solar activity):** Solar extreme-UV (`EUV`) and X-ray photons photodissociate and ionize sparse `O`, `O₂`, and `N₂` (`forming the F-layer ionosphere`), driving kinetic gas temperatures up to **`500 °C to 2,000 °C`** (`though air density is so low — ~10⁻¹² kg/m³ at 400 km, with a mean free path of >1 kilometre between molecular collisions — that it transfers negligible heat`). Solar-wind electrons and protons guided by Earth's magnetosphere excite atomic oxygen and nitrogen to create the **aurora borealis and aurora australis** (`green oxygen 557.7 nm emission at 100–300 km; red oxygen 630.0 nm emission above 300 km`), while the **International Space Station (`~400 km`)** and **Hubble (`~535 km`)** orbit inside the thermosphere (`and on 3–4 February 2022, a geomagnetic storm heated and expanded the thermosphere enough to increase drag by ~50% at 210 km and bring down 38 of 49 newly launched SpaceX Group 4-7 Starlink satellites; Fang et al. 2022, Space Weather 20, e2022SW003193`).
  5. **`Exosphere` (`~600 to 10,000 km`) & the `630,000-km` Hydrogen `Geocorona` (`Baliukin et al. 2019`, *JGR Space Physics* `124`, `861–885`; `Carruthers & Page 1972`, *Science* `177`, `788–791`):** Above the **exobase (`~500–1,000 km`, thermopause)**, collisions between atoms virtually cease and light hydrogen and helium atoms follow ballistic trajectories up to **`~10,000 km`**. However, in **February 2019**, **Igor I. Baliukin** (`born 1990`), **Jean-Loup Bertaux** (`born 1942`), **Quémerais**, **Katushkina**, **Izmodenov**, and **Schmidt** (`Baliukin et al. 2019`, *J. Geophys. Res.: Space Physics* `124`, `861–885`) re-analyzed `1996–1998` solar Lyman-alpha (`121.6 nm`) scattering maps recorded by the **`SWAN` instrument on the ESA/NASA `SOHO` spacecraft** (`at L₁, 1.5 million km sunward`), proving that Earth's outer cloud of neutral hydrogen atoms — the **`Geocorona`** (first imaged from the Moon in **April 1972** by `Apollo 16` astronauts **John Young** [`1930–2018`] and **Charles Duke** [`born 1935`] using **George Carruthers**'s [`1939–2020`] and **Thornton Page**'s [`1913–1996`] gold-plated Far UV Camera/Spectrograph) — actually reaches **`630,000 kilometres` (`391,000 miles`, `~100 Earth radii`)** from Earth, nearly **twice the `384,400-km` distance to the Moon** (`with ~70 H atoms/cm³ at 60,000 km and ~0.2 H atoms/cm³ at the Moon's orbit`).
