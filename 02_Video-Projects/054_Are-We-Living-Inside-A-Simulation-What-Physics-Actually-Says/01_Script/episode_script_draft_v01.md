# Why Do Physicists Say We Are Not Living Inside a Simulation?

Look inside Fermilab's Holometer tunnel in Illinois in December 2015, where twin lasers tested whether space is made of digital pixels. Could a shift a thousand times smaller than a proton expose a computer grid? Probing movements that last a millionth of a second across a billionth of a billionth of a meter, those lasers searched for the tracks of a simulated cosmos.

Not because physicists suddenly believed we are trapped inside a science-fiction video game, but because a physical secret of any finite computer is that it must chop smooth space and time into discrete steps, leaving measurable tracks in laser light, high-energy cosmic rays, and quantum electrons.

What would you see and feel if you zoomed down past atoms and protons searching for the mystery of a cosmic pixel grid beneath your feet? If a single proton were inflated to the size of the entire observable universe, the fundamental Planck length would barely span the width of a human hair — yet long before you reached that scale, a simulated grid would twist incoming cosmic rays and make laser beams jitter. Now why did so many researchers take the simulation argument seriously, and what happens when you test it against real physics?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Trilemma of Nick Bostrom]

In 2003, at the University of Oxford, philosopher Nick Bostrom published a paper in *Philosophical Quarterly* — first circulated in May 2001 — that turned the simulation question from late-night speculation into a rigorous counting problem.

Instead of claiming glitches in the sky, Bostrom calculated how much computing power it takes to emulate the firing synapses of a human brain: between ten to the fourteenth and ten to the seventeenth operations per second.

Then he multiplied that brain rate across all 100 billion humans who have ever lived, assuming 50 years per human and 30 million seconds per year, showing that the entire mental history of humanity requires about ten to the thirty-third to ten to the thirty-sixth operations — a job a planet-sized computer running at ten to the forty-second operations per second could finish in less than a millionth of a second.

But Bostrom's argument was actually a three-part mystery — a trilemma where at least 1 of 3 statements must be true: either civilisations almost always go extinct before building planetary computers, or advanced civilisations lose interest in running ancestor simulations, or simulated minds vastly outnumber original biological ones. [CHAPTER CARD: The Pixel Myth and the Cosmic-Ray Grid]

As German physicist Max Planck showed when he defined his natural units of length and time, there is a scale at 1.616 times ten to the minus 35 meters where quantum mechanics and gravity collide, and many popular accounts assume that number proves space is a digital grid of pixels.

Inside real physics laboratories, however, the secret to testing whether the universe runs on a grid comes from looking at how human supercomputers actually simulate subatomic particles today.

In October 2012, nuclear physicists Silas Beane, Zohreh Davoudi, and Martin Savage pointed out that when physicists simulate the strong nuclear force using lattice quantum chromodynamics, they replace smooth spacetime with a cubic grid just a few femtometers across — where 1 femtometer is ten to the minus 15 meters, the scale of an atomic nucleus.

Then the three physicists proved how we know whether our universe sits on a cubic grid: the highest-energy cosmic rays reach a natural energy cutoff around 6 times ten to the twentieth electronvolts, and if they travelled across a cubic lattice coarser than ten to the minus 27 meters, they would cluster along the cube's axes instead of arriving evenly from every direction in the sky. Now could a laser experiment on Earth test whether spacetime jitters directly?

[CHAPTER CARD: Twin Lasers in the Illinois Prairie]

Before Fermilab switched on its Holometer experiment, you might have assumed that any quantum pixelation of spacetime would be far too small for an instrument on Earth to ever detect.

Wrong.

University of Chicago astrophysicist Craig Hogan proposed that if a volume of space holds only a finite amount of holographic information, spacetime should suffer a collective sideways blur — a jitter that a pair of high-frequency laser interferometers could catch even at scales much larger than the Planck length.

Built under project manager Aaron Chou at Fermilab, the Holometer placed two identical laser interferometers side by side, each sending a 1-kilowatt laser beam through a beam splitter down two perpendicular arms 40 meters — 39 meters in optical baseline — long.

But when Aaron Chou and the collaboration cross-correlated the light returning from both 40-meter instruments at megahertz frequencies — sampling movements lasting a millionth of a second down to a billionth of a billionth of a meter — they announced on December 3, 2015 that they had ruled out Hogan's pixelated universe to high statistical significance. Now what if a simulator simply uses an even finer grid — or does quantum mechanics forbid classical simulation altogether?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Quantum Wall of Three Hundred Electrons]

Even after Bostrom himself noted in 2003 that simulating the entire universe down to the quantum level is obviously infeasible without radically new physics, a deeper mystery remained: could a clever algorithm approximate quantum matter on a classical supercomputer?

Back in 1982, American physicist Richard Feynman warned that when quantum particles entangle, the memory needed for a classical computer to track them grows exponentially with every particle you add.

To see if any shortcut could beat that exponential wall, theoretical physicists Zohar Ringel and Dmitry Kovrizhin at the University of Oxford and the Hebrew University examined Quantum Monte Carlo simulations of electrons experiencing gravitational responses — thermal Hall conductance in fractional quantum Hall metals — and published their proof in *Science Advances* in September 2017.

Then Ringel and Kovrizhin showed why such a simulation is impossible as a matter of principle: quantum wave-function phases cancel in what physicists call the sign problem, forcing the classical computer to double its processors and memory for every single extra particle, so storing the quantum state of just a few hundred electrons requires a memory built from more atoms than exist in the entire Universe. [CHAPTER CARD: What Physics Actually Says About Reality]

You might wonder whether an advanced civilisation could simply build a computer the mass of a planet or use quantum physicist Seth Lloyd's ultimate 1-kilogram computer limit of 5 times ten to the fiftieth operations per second on ten to the thirty-first bits to run our universe anyway.

Here is why the physics we have tested settles that question: even Seth Lloyd's ultimate 1-kilogram machine holds only ten to the thirty-first bits, whereas simulating quantum matter on any classical machine doubles its memory with every added particle and hits an impossible wall at just a few hundred electrons.

Across every physical test researchers have run between 1982 and 2017 — from cosmic rays showing no cubic grid down to ten to the minus 27 meters, to Fermilab's 40-meter Holometer finding zero pixel jitter down to ten to the minus 18 meters, to Ringel and Kovrizhin's quantum sign-problem proof — the universe refuses to behave like a classical computer approximation.

Because the only machine capable of simulating a quantum universe one-to-one without running out of atoms is a full-scale quantum universe itself, the world around us is not a cheap digital illusion — and next, we step inside the real biological stakes of space exploration to ask what spaceflight does to the human body, and what humans will become in 1,000 years.
