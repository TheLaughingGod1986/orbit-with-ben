# Why Do Physicists Say We Are Not Living Inside a Simulation?

Look inside Fermilab's Holometer tunnel in Illinois in December 2015, where twin lasers searched for a hidden signal testing whether space — from a lab on Earth to a spacecraft in orbit — is made of digital pixels. Could a shift a thousand times smaller than a single proton expose a computer grid? Probing movements lasting a millionth of a second across a billionth of a billionth of a meter, those lasers hunted for tracks of a simulated cosmos.

Not because physicists believed we are trapped inside a video game, but because a physical secret of any finite computer is that it must chop smooth space and time into discrete steps, leaving measurable tracks in laser light, cosmic rays, and quantum electrons.

What would you see and feel if you zoomed down past atoms and protons searching for the mystery of a pixel grid beneath your feet? If a single proton were inflated to the size of the observable universe, the Planck length of 1.616 times ten to the minus 35 meters would barely span the width of a human hair — yet long before that scale, a simulated grid would twist cosmic rays and make lasers jitter. Now why did researchers take the simulation argument seriously, and what happens when you test it against physics?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Trilemma of Nick Bostrom]

In 2003, at Oxford University, philosopher Nick Bostrom published a paper in *Philosophical Quarterly* — first circulated in May 2001 — turning the simulation question into a counting problem.

Instead of claiming sky glitches, Bostrom calculated the computing power needed to emulate a human brain's synapses: ten to the fourteenth to ten to the seventeenth operations per second.

Then he multiplied that rate across 100 billion humans who have ever lived, assuming 50 years per human and 30 million seconds per year, showing all human mental history requires ten to the thirty-third to ten to the thirty-sixth operations — compared against a sugar-cube computer at ten to the twenty-first instructions per second, or a planet-sized computer at ten to the forty-second operations per second finishing in under a millionth of a second.

But Bostrom's argument was a three-part mystery — a trilemma where at least 1 of 3 statements is true: civilisations go extinct before building planetary computers, or lose interest in ancestor simulations, or simulated minds outnumber biological ones. [CHAPTER CARD: The Pixel Myth and the Cosmic-Ray Grid]

As German physicist Max Planck showed when he defined his natural units of length at 1.616255 times ten to the minus 35 meters and time at 5.391 times ten to the minus 44 seconds, there is a scale where quantum mechanics and gravity collide, and many accounts assume those numbers prove space is a pixel grid.

Inside physics laboratories, however, the secret to testing whether the universe runs on a grid comes from looking at how human supercomputers simulate subatomic particles today.

In October 2012 — in research published in *The European Physical Journal A* in 2014 — nuclear physicists Silas Beane, Zohreh Davoudi, and Martin Savage noted that when physicists simulate the strong nuclear force using lattice quantum chromodynamics, they replace smooth spacetime with a cubic grid a few femtometers across, where 1 femtometer is ten to the minus 15 meters, the scale of an atomic nucleus.

Then the three physicists proved how we know whether our universe sits on a cubic grid: the highest-energy cosmic rays reach a cutoff around 6 times ten to the twentieth electronvolts — about ten to the eleventh gigaelectronvolts — and if they travelled across a cubic lattice coarser than ten to the minus 12 femtometers, or ten to the minus 27 meters, they would cluster along the cube's axes instead of arriving evenly from every direction in the sky. Now could a laser experiment on Earth test whether spacetime jitters directly?

[CHAPTER CARD: Twin Lasers in the Illinois Prairie]

Before Fermilab switched on its Holometer experiment, you might have assumed that any quantum pixelation of spacetime would be far too small for an instrument on Earth to detect.

Wrong.

University of Chicago astrophysicist Craig Hogan proposed that if a volume of space holds only finite holographic information, spacetime should suffer a collective sideways blur — a jitter that high-frequency laser interferometers could catch at scales much larger than the Planck length.

Built under project manager Aaron Chou at Fermilab, the Holometer placed two identical laser interferometers close together, each sending a 1-kilowatt laser beam through a beam splitter down two perpendicular arms 40 meters — 39 meters in optical baseline — long.

But when Aaron Chou and the collaboration cross-correlated a year of light returning from both 40-meter instruments at megahertz frequencies — sampling movements lasting a millionth of a second down to a billionth of a billionth of a meter — they announced on December 3, 2015 and published in *Physical Review Letters* in 2016 that they had ruled out Hogan's pixelated universe to high statistical significance. Now what if a simulator uses an even finer grid — or does quantum mechanics forbid classical simulation altogether?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Quantum Wall of Three Hundred Electrons]

Even after Bostrom noted in 2003 that simulating the entire universe down to the quantum level is obviously infeasible without radically new physics, a deeper mystery remained: could a clever algorithm approximate quantum matter on a classical supercomputer?

Back in 1982, American physicist Richard Feynman warned that when quantum particles entangle, the resources needed for a classical computer to track them grow exponentially with every particle added.

To see if any shortcut could beat that wall, theoretical physicists Zohar Ringel and Dmitry Kovrizhin at the University of Oxford and the Hebrew University examined Quantum Monte Carlo simulations of electrons experiencing gravitational responses — thermal Hall conductance in quantum metals — publishing their proof in *Science Advances* in September 2017 and announcing it in October 2017.

Then Ringel and Kovrizhin showed why such a simulation is impossible in principle: quantum quantities acquire negative or complex signs in the sign problem, forcing a classical computer to double its processors, memory, and electricity for every extra particle, so storing the information about just a few hundred electrons requires a memory built from more atoms than there are in the Universe. [CHAPTER CARD: What Physics Actually Says About Reality]

You might wonder whether an advanced civilisation could build a computer the mass of a planet or use MIT physicist Seth Lloyd's ultimate 1-kilogram computer confined to 1 liter — capable of 5 times ten to the fiftieth logical operations per second on ten to the thirty-first bits — to run our universe anyway.

Here is why the physics we have tested settles that question: even Seth Lloyd's 1-kilogram machine holds only ten to the thirty-first bits, whereas simulating quantum matter on any classical machine doubles its memory with every added particle and hits an impossible wall at just a few hundred electrons.
