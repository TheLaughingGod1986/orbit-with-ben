# What Lies Hiding at the Centre of the Milky Way?

If you peered through the thick clouds of cosmic dust in the constellation Sagittarius — twenty-six thousand light-years from Earth in orbit around the heart of our Milky Way galaxy — what secret signal would you see whipping around the event horizon at the centre?

For decades, those interstellar dust clouds blocked visible-light telescopes from seeing our galaxy's core, until radio, infrared, and X-ray observatories peered deeper and uncovered a gravitational monster called Sagittarius A-star: a supermassive black hole packing four million times the mass of the Sun into a region no larger than our solar system, surrounded by multi-million-degree gas and stars screaming past at twenty-five million kilometres per hour.

Why did this unknown giant first reveal itself as static on a telephone antenna in 1932, what if a star dives within twenty billion kilometres of its gravity well, and why did X-ray telescopes now catch an echo showing our sleeping black hole woke up just two hundred years ago?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Hiss From Sagittarius]

1918. American astronomer Harlow Shapley mapped the halo of globular star clusters surrounding our galaxy and discovered that the Milky Way's centre lies in the constellation Sagittarius — but thick clouds of interstellar gas and dust absorbed visible light and hid what lay inside.

Then, in 1932, Bell Telephone Laboratories engineer Karl Jansky built a directional antenna to hunt down static interfering with short-wave radio telephone calls.

Alongside thunderstorm noise, Jansky heard a steady hiss whose time of day changed with the seasons, concluding in December 1932 that it came from outside the solar system — and announcing in April 1933 that the radio waves arrived from our galaxy's centre of gravity in Sagittarius.

However, it took until 1974 for astronomer Bruce Balick and his colleague at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory's Green Bank Interferometer to pinpoint a bright, compact radio source inside that region — later named Sagittarius A-star, adding the asterisk for an excited state. Why couldn't radio dishes alone prove that pinpoint was a single black hole?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Rivalry to Track Star S2]

Since the early 1990s, two rival astronomy teams raced to peer through the Milky Way's dust veil using infrared wavelengths: German astrophysicist Reinhard Genzel using European Southern Observatory telescopes in Chile, and American astronomer Andrea Ghez using the Keck Observatory in Hawaii.

However, Earth's turbulent atmosphere blurred the stars together — until both teams refined new infrared techniques and unique instruments to compensate for atmospheric distortions, bringing the galactic centre into razor-sharp focus.

At the very heart of the jumble, both teams tracked a bright star called S2 locked in a sixteen-year elliptical orbit around an invisible pivot.

Then, in 2002, after watching S2 whip around a tight hairpin turn, Genzel and Ghez independently solved the mystery: four million solar masses sit packed inside a region no larger than our solar system — a discovery that earned them a share of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics. What happened when S2 plunged back in for its next closest approach in May 2018?

[CHAPTER CARD: Twenty-Five Million Kilometres Per Hour]

May 2018. Culminating a twenty-six-year observation campaign, astronomers used the GRAVITY, SINFONI, and NACO infrared instruments on the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope in Chile as star S2 dived deeper toward Sagittarius A-star.

At closest approach, S2 skimmed within less than twenty billion kilometres of the black hole — one hundred and twenty times the distance from Earth to the Sun, or about four times the distance from the Sun to Neptune, corresponding to roughly one thousand five hundred times the black hole's Schwarzschild radius.

Then, whipped by the strongest gravitational field in our galaxy, S2 accelerated to a speed in excess of twenty-five million kilometres per hour — almost three per cent of the speed of light.

As S2 climbed out of that gravity well, the black hole stretched its starlight to longer wavelengths — the first detection of Albert Einstein's gravitational redshift around a supermassive black hole, published on 26 July 2018.

However, even at twenty billion kilometres, infrared telescopes still could not resolve the dark event horizon itself. Case closed? Was Sagittarius A-star forever too tiny and fast-changing to photograph directly?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why the Monster Starves]

That brings us to the biggest misconception about the black hole at the heart of our galaxy. People often fear a four-million-solar-mass monster acts like a cosmic vacuum cleaner that sucks in everything nearby.

Let us test that paradox with physics. In 2012, NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory collected five weeks of observations across a region half a light-year wide around Sagittarius A-star, mapping multi-million-degree gas blown off a disc of young massive stars.

Chandra discovered that Sagittarius A-star is extraordinarily faint in X-rays because less than one per cent of the material initially captured within the black hole's gravitational influence ever reaches the event horizon, or point of no return.

Before captured gas can plunge into the black hole, it must lose heat and angular momentum — and ejecting more than ninety-nine per cent of that inflowing matter carries the energy away, leaving our galaxy's giant on a starvation diet. Why did surrounding gas clouds suddenly shine with X-rays?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Echo From Two Hundred Years Ago]

2010. Processing observations from NASA's Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope, scientists discovered two gigantic gamma-ray lobes — the Fermi Bubbles — emerging above and below the Milky Way's centre and spanning fifty thousand light-years from end to end, roughly half the diameter of our galaxy, likely produced by ancient jets from Sagittarius A-star.

Then, during February and March 2022, NASA's Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer and Chandra observed giant molecular clouds near Sagittarius A-star that were shining unexpectedly brightly in X-rays.

Using the polarization angle of those reflected X-rays like a compass needle pointing straight back to Sagittarius A-star, astronomers proved the clouds are echoing an intense X-ray flare from about two hundred Earth years ago — around the start of the nineteenth century — when our sleeping black hole briefly woke to devour passing gas.

Sagittarius A-star showed that at the heart of the Milky Way, twenty-six thousand light-years away, four million Suns sit inside a starving supermassive black hole whose gravity bends starlight and whose past eruptions still echo across the galaxy.
