# Why Did NASA's DART Spacecraft Change an Asteroid's Orbit by 33 Minutes?

Six million eight hundred thousand miles from Earth where a five-hundred-and-sixty-foot flying rubble pile of rocky debris loops around a half-mile spinning-top asteroid, as you cross deep space with NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test spacecraft and race toward its rocky horizon at three point eight miles per second on September 26, 2022, why did NASA's DART spacecraft change an asteroid's orbit by thirty-three minutes?

Before the collision, it took the moonlet Dimorphos eleven hours and fifty-five minutes to complete one circular orbit around its larger parent asteroid Didymos, and NASA had defined minimum mission success as shortening that orbital period by at least seventy-three seconds.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Twin Asteroid System of Didymos and Dimorphos]

On April 11, 1996, Spacewatch researchers at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona discovered the near-Earth asteroid Didymos, whose name means twin in Greek, and then on November 23, 2003, optical light curves and Arecibo radar images confirmed its small moonlet Dimorphos.

Orbiting the Sun every two point one one years — seven hundred and seventy days — on a path tilted three degrees between ninety-four million and two hundred and eleven million miles out beyond Mars, the pair passed within four point five million miles of Earth in 2003, but poses no hazard to Earth.

Didymos measures about half a mile — eight hundred and five metres — across and spins rapidly once every two point two six hours, while Dimorphos measures about five hundred and sixty feet — one hundred and seventy metres — wide and orbited three thousand nine hundred feet away.

However, geological studies published in Nature Communications on July 30, 2024 solved a deeper mystery: Didymos's bearing capacity is at least one thousand times lower than dry sand on Earth, and its surface is twelve point five million years old until sunlight torque spun off Dimorphos less than three hundred thousand years ago — forty to one hundred and thirty times younger. Now how did NASA hit such a tiny target?

[CHAPTER CARD: Seven Miles and Two Seconds Before Impact]

After launching from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California on a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket on November 24, 2021, NASA's half-ton DART spacecraft navigated deep space using a single onboard camera called DRACO, based on the New Horizons LORRI instrument.

Fifteen days before impact, on September 11, 2022, flight controllers in Turin, Italy detached the Italian Space Agency's shoebox-sized companion satellite LICIACube to trail behind and photograph the collision.

During the final hours on September 26, 2022, DART's optical navigation locked onto the five-hundred-and-sixty-foot moonlet from six million eight hundred thousand miles away and steered straight into Dimorphos at roughly fourteen thousand miles — twenty-two thousand five hundred and thirty kilometres — per hour.

Just two seconds before impact, when DART was seven miles — twelve kilometres — above the surface, DRACO streamed back its last complete image showing a one-hundred-foot-wide patch of boulders before the signal vanished at 7:14 p.m. Eastern time. [CHAPTER CARD: Thirty-Five Million Pounds of Rocket Debris]

Wrong. Dimorphos is not a solid monolith of stone, and the push that moved its orbit did not come mainly from the mass of the spacecraft itself.

As LICIACube barrelled past Dimorphos at fifteen thousand miles per hour just two to three minutes after the crash — approaching within fifty-three miles during a sixty-second window and snapping a photo every three seconds — its cameras revealed a sunlit cone of rocky debris.

In a study published in the Planetary Science Journal on August 21, 2025, NASA and Italian Space Agency scientists calculated from eighteen LICIACube images that the crash spewed an estimated thirty-five point three million pounds — sixteen million kilograms — of dust and rocks into space, thirty thousand times greater than the mass of the spacecraft.

With almost forty-five percent of that mass hidden inside the opaque inner plume, then the escaping rubble acted like a short burst from a rocket engine — or a jet of air streaming out of a balloon — doubling the punch created by the spacecraft alone. [CHAPTER CARD: From Squashed Ball to Oblong Watermelon]

On October 11, 2022, NASA confirmed from ground telescopes and Goldstone radar that DART had shortened Dimorphos's eleven-hour-and-fifty-five-minute orbit by about thirty-two minutes, to eleven hours and twenty-three minutes.

Then on March 19, 2024, a Jet Propulsion Laboratory study in the Planetary Science Journal combined DRACO images, Goldstone radar, and global light-curve dips to track how the orbit evolved after impact.

Immediately after the crash, Dimorphos's orbital period dropped by thirty-two minutes and forty-two seconds, and as the asteroid shed more rocky material it settled at eleven hours, twenty-two minutes, and three seconds — thirty-three minutes and fifteen seconds shorter than before impact, accurate to within one and a half seconds.

As JPL senior research scientist Steve Chesley and his colleagues showed, the crash pulled Dimorphos one hundred and twenty feet closer to Didymos — from three thousand nine hundred feet down to three thousand seven hundred and eighty feet — and reshaped the rubble pile from a symmetrical squashed ball into an oblong watermelon. [CHAPTER CARD: Thirty-Seven Boulders Moving Like a Giant Tortoise]

On December 19, 2022 — nearly four months after impact — NASA's Hubble Space Telescope stared at Dimorphos and spotted thirty-seven free-flung boulders drifting away into space.

Reported on July 20, 2023 by planetary scientist David Jewitt of the University of California at Los Angeles, these faint boulders range from three feet to twenty-two feet across and make up about zero point one percent of Dimorphos's mass.

Shaken off from about two percent of the surface boulders — excavated from a circle roughly one hundred and sixty feet wide, the width of a football field — the rocks drift away at a little more than half a mile per hour, roughly the walking speed of a giant tortoise.

Because they move so slowly, then the boulder cloud expands like a swarm of bees that the European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft will inspect when it arrives in late 2026. Now could hitting a small moonlet actually move both asteroids around the Sun?

[CHAPTER CARD: Shifting an Asteroid's Orbit Around the Sun]

You might worry that slamming into an asteroid could accidentally knock it onto a collision course with Earth, or that hitting a tiny moonlet would leave the larger parent asteroid untouched.

Between October 2022 and March 2025, volunteer astronomers around the globe recorded twenty-two stellar occultations — when Didymos passed in front of a star and made its pinpoint of light blink out for a fraction of a second.

In a study published in Science Advances on March 6, 2026, researchers proved that because Didymos and Dimorphos are linked by gravity around a shared centre of mass and lost escaping debris to space, DART's impact also changed the binary pair's seven-hundred-and-seventy-day orbit around the Sun by zero point one five seconds.

That shift altered the binary system's orbital speed by eleven point seven microns per second — one point seven inches per hour — marking the first time a human-made object has measurably changed a celestial body's path around the Sun while keeping Didymos safely clear of Earth — and next, we travel to Uranus to ask why its moon Miranda has an ice cliff twelve miles high.
