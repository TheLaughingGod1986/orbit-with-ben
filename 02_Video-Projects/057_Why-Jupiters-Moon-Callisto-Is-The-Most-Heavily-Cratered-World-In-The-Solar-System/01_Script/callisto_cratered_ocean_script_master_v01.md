# Why Is Jupiter's Moon Callisto the Most Heavily Cratered World in the Solar System, and How Does It Hide a Deep Ocean?

Look out beyond Jupiter's horizon to the far orbit of its four great moons, 1,883,000 kilometers — or 1,170,000 miles — from the giant planet, where Callisto circles as the third-largest moon in our solar system at 4,821 kilometers across, with a 15,144-kilometer — 9,410-mile — equatorial circumference that makes it 98.8 percent as wide as the planet Mercury at 4,879 kilometers. Why did Callisto's 4-billion-year cratered ice survive as the oldest and most heavily cratered surface in the solar system, and what secret did NASA's Galileo spacecraft uncover 250 kilometers, or 155 miles, deeper beneath those scars? Because Callisto stays in a distant orbit outside the gravitational resonance that heats its neighbors, no volcanoes or tectonic plates ever erased 4 billion years of comet and asteroid impacts.

Not because Callisto is a dry ball of stone, but because the physical mystery of its magnetic signature revealed a salty liquid-water ocean buried beneath that battered ice lid, interacting with rock where life could exist.

What would you see and feel standing under Callisto's carbon dioxide and oxygen sky in 1.24-meter-per-second-squared gravity beside the 4,000-kilometer concentric rings of the Valhalla impact basin, 778 million kilometers — 484 million miles — from the Sun, where sunlight takes 43 minutes to arrive? Even though Callisto spans 98.8 percent of Mercury's diameter, its 1,830-kilogram-per-cubic-meter rock-and-ice body has barely one-third of Mercury's 5,427-kilogram-per-cubic-meter density. Now why did Callisto freeze 4 billion years of impacts in place while hiding an ocean below?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Outer Moon Outside the Resonance]

On January 7, 1610, Italian scientist Galileo Galilei — who lived from 1564 to 1642 — pointed his telescope at Jupiter and discovered Callisto alongside Io, Europa, and Ganymede, proving that worlds orbit a body other than Earth.

As French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace — who lived from 1749 to 1827 — later explained, the inner three moons are locked in a 4-to-2-to-1 orbital resonance: for every 1 orbit Ganymede completes in 7.155 Earth days at 1,070,000 kilometers, Europa completes 2 orbits in 3.6 days at 671,000 kilometers, and Io completes 4 orbits in 1.8 days at 422,000 kilometers.

That rhythmic gravitational tug-of-war flexes Io into erupting hundreds of volcanoes and resurfaces Europa and Ganymede with fresh ice.

But Callisto orbits alone at 1,883,000 kilometers — 1.8 times farther from Jupiter than Ganymede, 2.8 times farther than Europa, and 4.5 times farther than Io — taking 16.689 Earth days, or 400.5 hours, to complete one tidally locked orbit at 8.2 kilometers per second with a 0.007 eccentricity while the Jovian system circles the Sun every 12 Earth years. Then because Callisto orbits outside the resonance, no tidal furnace ever recycled its crust until every impact stayed frozen in place. Now what happens to a planet-sized icy world when 4 billion years of impacts are never erased?

[CHAPTER CARD: Valhalla and Four Billion Years of Scars]

When NASA's Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 flew past Callisto in 1979 and the 2,223-kilogram Galileo orbiter — launched on October 18, 1989 and orbiting Jupiter from December 7, 1995 to September 21, 2003 across 34 orbits — completed 8 Callisto flybys, their cameras revealed a surface where every new crater sits on top of an older one.

Without volcanoes or shifting plates, Callisto's icy shell recorded 4 billion years of bombardment, glittering with white water-ice peaks and dark hydrated minerals and organic tholins at minus 155 degrees Celsius.

Its largest scar is the Valhalla multi-ring impact structure, whose 4,000-kilometer Valhalla rings — 2,500 miles across — span over 80 percent of Callisto's 4,821-kilometer diameter. On November 4, 1996, on its third orbit, Galileo flew 1,219 kilometers — 757 miles — above Callisto and imaged an 11-kilometer, or 7-mile, swath of Valhalla's center at 60-meter, or 200-foot, resolution, showing how slushy ice slumped inward.

Then on June 5, 1998, planetary scientists John D. Anderson and Gerald Schubert — who lived from 1939 to 2025 — reported in *Science* that radio Doppler tracking from Galileo's C3, C9, and C10 encounters proved Callisto's rock and ice only partially separated, leaving an outer clean-ice layer less than 350 kilometers thick and any rock-metal core smaller than 25 percent of Callisto's radius. Now if Callisto is only partially separated inside, why did scientists suspect an ocean deeper below?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why the "Ugly Duckling Moon" Hides an Ocean]

For decades, planetary scientists dismissed Callisto as a boring "ugly duckling moon" — a dead hunk of rock and ice where nothing had changed for 4 billion years.

Wrong.

As Galileo swept past Callisto with its 11-meter — 36-foot — magnetometer boom, space physicist Krishan Khurana, magnetometer principal investigator Margaret Kivelson — born in 1928 — and planetary scientist David J. Stevenson — born in 1948 — noticed that although Callisto has no internal magnetic dynamo, Jupiter's spinning magnetosphere creates magnetic perturbations around the moon.

On October 22, 1998, Khurana, Kivelson, Stevenson, and their colleagues published the explanation in *Nature*: Jupiter's periodically varying magnetic field drives electromagnetic eddy currents inside Callisto, generating an induced magnetic field that requires a global layer of high electrical conductivity beneath the surface.

Because solid ice is a poor electrical conductor, the most probable explanation is a 250-kilometer-deep salty ocean — 155 miles beneath the surface, within Callisto's less-than-350-kilometer outer ice shell — interacting with a layer of rocks where the chemical ingredients for life meet. Now how can a moon with a buried ocean also breathe a carbon dioxide and oxygen sky?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Four-Year Atmosphere and Europe's Icy Moon Explorer]

Above that 250-kilometer-deep salty ocean, Galileo's instruments uncovered a second surprise clinging to Callisto's frozen surface.

On February 5, 1999, planetary physicist Robert W. Carlson — born in 1941 — reported in *Science* that an off-limb scan by Galileo's Near-Infrared Mapping Spectrometer during 1997 observations detected a 100-kilometer carbon dioxide airglow in the 4.26-micrometer nu-3 band above Callisto's surface.

That glow revealed a tenuous carbon dioxide exosphere at 150 kelvin with a surface pressure of 7.5 trillionths of a bar — 7.5 times 10 to the minus 12 bar — alongside oxygen and hydrogen detected in later studies. Because photoionization and Jupiter's magnetosphere sweep that thin gas away on a timescale of about 4 years, Carlson proved that Callisto must be actively supplying fresh carbon dioxide today.

To map that active exosphere and hidden ocean, the European Space Agency and NASA launched the 6,000-kilogram Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer — or JUICE, with a 2,400-kilogram dry mass — on April 14, 2023 with 10 solar panels spanning 85 square meters and 10 instruments, arriving at Jupiter in July 2031 for a 4-year mission to Callisto, Europa, and Ganymede. Now what does Callisto's quiet survival tell us about habitable worlds around giant planets?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Time Capsule That Hid a Sea]

You might think a world has to look geologically alive on the outside — with erupting plumes or shifting plates — to hold an ocean on the inside.

But Callisto proves that even the solar system's oldest, most heavily cratered surface can shelter liquid water 250 kilometers beneath the ice.

Across four centuries of discovery — from Galileo Galilei's January 7, 1610 telescope observation and Pierre-Simon Laplace's 4-to-2-to-1 orbital resonance math, to Voyager's 1979 flybys of the 4,000-kilometer Valhalla rings, Galileo's November 4, 1996 60-meter imaging and Anderson and Schubert's June 5, 1998 partial-differentiation gravity data, Khurana, Kivelson, and Stevenson's October 22, 1998 induced-magnetic-field ocean discovery, Carlson's February 5, 1999 100-kilometer carbon dioxide airglow detection, and JUICE's April 14, 2023 launch toward its July 2031 arrival — Jupiter's quiet outer moon turned from an "ugly duckling" into a potential habitat.

Callisto shows that calm orbits can preserve both a 4-billion-year time capsule and a hidden sea — and next, we leave Jupiter's quietest moon for the most violent environments in the cosmos to ask what is the most dangerous place in the universe, inside a magnetar and a quasar jet.
