1. **What is up there (`Ch 1`):** The largest structure ever built in space — **`419,725 kg` (`925,335 lbs` ≈ `420 tonnes`; over `430,000 kg` / `945,000 lbs` with visiting hardware)**, **`109 m` (`356 ft`) end-to-end** (one yard shy of an American football field including end zones; `94 m` truss, `73 m` solar array length, `67 m` pressurized module axis), orbiting at **`~400–415 km` (`257 miles`)** in the thermosphere at **`5 miles/s` (`~17,500 mph` / `~28,000 km/h`)**, completing **`16 orbits a day`** (`1` every `90 minutes`) over **`90%` of Earth's population**, continuously occupied since **`November 2000`** (`assembly started 1998`).
2. **Why it can't stay aloft forever (`Ch 2`):** At `415 km`, thin upper-atmospheric air still creates constant drag; **without regular propulsive reboosts, the station would naturally fall back to Earth in just `1 to 2 years`** depending on solar activity. Its primary structure (modules, radiators, and truss) faces a finite technical lifetime from **dynamic loading (dockings/undockings)** and **orbital thermal cycling**. NASA evaluated disassembly (infeasible without the Space Shuttle cargo bay and requiring `161+` spacewalks) and boosting to a higher storage orbit (rejected because orbital debris impact risk rises from once every `~51 years` at `415 km` to `<4 years` at `800 km`, and high orbits require `120–760 m/s` delta-V vs `57 m/s` for controlled deorbit).
3. **The Deorbit Plan — Baseline Schedule vs Guaranteed Date (`Ch 3`):** NASA, ESA, JAXA, and CSA have committed to operate the ISS **through `2030`** (Roscosmos through at least `2028`) while commercial LEO stations are developed. **Crucial distinction (`GAO-26-107805`, June 2026):** `2030/2031` is the **baseline plan, not a guaranteed date** — in **`2027`**, NASA will assess commercial station readiness to decide whether to launch the deorbit craft in **mid-`2029`** or extend ISS operations.
