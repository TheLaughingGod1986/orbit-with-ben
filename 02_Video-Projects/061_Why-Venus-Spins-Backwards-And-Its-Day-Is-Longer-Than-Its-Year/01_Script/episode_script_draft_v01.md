# Why Does Venus Spin Backwards and Have a Day Longer Than Its Year?

Look from Venus orbit past the golden cloud horizon at Earth's twin — a rocky planet 12,104 kilometers across circling the Sun at 108.21 million kilometers. Why does Venus take 243 Earth days to complete a single rotation on its axis — longer than its 224.7-day year around the Sun — and what secret force inside its crushing 92-bar atmosphere makes the planet spin backwards until the Sun rises in the west and sets in the east? Because solar heating of Venus's dense carbon dioxide atmosphere creates powerful thermal tides that pull against gravitational tides from the Sun and friction at its core-mantle boundary, braking the planet and driving a slow clockwise spin.

Not because Venus is tidally locked with one side facing the Sun forever, but because the physical mystery of solar gravitational tides, atmospheric thermal tides, and internal friction locked Earth's closest neighbor into a retrograde equilibrium state with an axial tilt of 177.36 degrees.

What would you see and feel hovering beneath sulfuric acid clouds that whip around the globe in just 4 Earth days — 60 times faster than the ground below — or standing on a 464-degree-Celsius surface where sunrise to sunrise takes 116.75 Earth days and the day length suddenly wobbles by up to 20 minutes? Even though Venus has 95 percent of Earth's 6,378.1-kilometer equatorial radius at 6,051.8 kilometers and 81.5 percent of Earth's mass at 4.8673 times 10 to the 24th kilograms, its sidereal rotation period of 5,832.6 hours is 243.69 times slower than Earth's 23.9345-hour day. Now how did radar astronomers pierce those clouds to discover it?

[CHAPTER CARD: How Radar Pierced Venus's Clouds and Found a Backwards Spin]

In 1610, Italian astronomer Galileo Galilei — who lived from 1564 to 1642 — observed the phases of Venus across its 584-day cycle, and then during the 1761 transit Russian polymath Mikhail Lomonosov — who lived from 1711 to 1765 — found the first evidence that Venus had a dense atmosphere.

In the 19th century, many observers guessed Venus spun in 24 hours until Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli — who lived from 1835 to 1910 — proposed that Venus was tidally locked with the Sun, keeping the same face pointed sunward across a 224.7-day orbit.

However, that illusion held until the 1961 inferior conjunction, when MIT physicist Gordon H. Pettengill — who lived from 1926 to 2021 — bounced radar pulses deeper through the clouds from Millstone Hill alongside NASA's 26-meter Goldstone antenna in California, Jodrell Bank in the United Kingdom, and Yevpatoria in Crimea.

Then on March 8, 1963, Jet Propulsion Laboratory radar astronomers Richard M. Goldstein and Roland L. Carpenter reported in *Science* that Doppler-shifted radar echoes showed Venus rotating opposite to Earth once every 240 days, and on July 28, 1967, MIT and Harvard astrophysicist Irwin I. Shapiro — born in 1929 — reported in *Science* a retrograde rotation period of 243.09 days, plus or minus 0.18, with an equator tilted less than 2 degrees. [CHAPTER CARD: The 116.75-Day Solar Day and Where the Sun Rises in the West]

Why do astronomers say Venus's day is 243 Earth days long while sunrise to sunrise takes 116.75 Earth days?

A sidereal day measures one full 360-degree spin relative to the distant stars, which on Venus takes 243.0226 Earth days — 5,832.6 hours — in a clockwise, retrograde direction, with an obliquity of 177.36 degrees and a slight 2.64-degree tilt that produces no seasons.

Meanwhile, Venus orbits the Sun counterclockwise at 35.02 kilometers per second along a nearly circular orbit with an eccentricity of 0.0068, completing one year in 224.701 Earth days.

Because its clockwise spin and counterclockwise orbit work together to sweep the Sun across the sky, a solar day on Venus lasts 116.75 Earth days — 2,802.0 hours, or 1.92 solar days per Venusian year — with the Sun rising in the west and setting in the east, until the 583.92-day synodic interval between close approaches to Earth at 38.2 million kilometers equals 5.001444 Venusian solar days, fooling early observers into seeing the same face every conjunction. Now what physical force braked Venus and reversed its spin?

[CHAPTER CARD: Crushing 92-Bar Thermal Tides vs Solar Gravity]

If Venus orbits at 0.723 astronomical units where solar gravity tugs strongly on its solid body, why didn't the Sun tidally lock Venus so one side faces the light forever?

As NASA's Mariner 2 radiometer showed in 1962 and the Soviet Venera 4 probe confirmed in 1967, Venus is wrapped in an atmosphere of 96.5 percent carbon dioxide and 3.5 percent nitrogen weighing 4.8 times 10 to the 20th kilograms — 92 times the mass of Earth's atmosphere — exerting a surface pressure of 92 bars at a density of 65 kilograms per cubic meter, or 6.5 percent the density of liquid water, and a temperature of 737 kelvins, or 464 degrees Celsius.

However, because Venus receives 2,601.3 watts per square meter of solar irradiance — 1.911 times Earth's 1,361.0 watts per square meter — sunlight heats the thick dayside atmosphere, driving gas toward the cooler nightside until two atmospheric mass bulges shift ahead of and behind the subsolar line.

Then as Cornell astrophysicist Thomas Gold — who lived from 1920 to 2004 — and his student Steven Soter showed in 1969, the Sun's gravity pulls on those thermal atmospheric bulges in the opposite direction to solid-body rock tides, creating an equilibrium spin of 243 days. [CHAPTER CARD: Four Final Rotation States, 60× Winds, and a 20-Minute Wobble]

You might assume the only way a planet can end up with an axial tilt of 177.36 degrees — spinning backwards compared to Earth — is if a giant protoplanet smashed into it and flipped its spin axis upside down.

Wrong.

While Caltech planetary scientist David J. Stevenson — born in 1948 — and colleagues showed in 2006 that two giant impacts 10 million years apart could reverse Venus's spin and destroy an early moon, French astronomer Jacques Laskar — born in 1955 — and Alexandre C. M. Correia proved in *Nature* on June 14, 2001 that any rocky planet with a dense atmosphere evolves through core-mantle friction and atmospheric tides into one of just four final rotation states — and that Venus could start with normal prograde rotation, slow to a halt, and reverse into retrograde rotation without its spin axis ever flipping upside down.

Even today, that atmosphere grips the planet: then on April 24, 2020, Hokkaido University physicist Takeshi Horinouchi and colleagues reported in *Science* using Japan's Akatsuki orbiter — in orbit from December 7, 2015 to 2024 — that thermal tides near the 70-kilometer cloud tops maintain winds of 100 meters per second, or 360 kilometers per hour, circling Venus in 4 Earth days at 60 times the planet's rotation speed.

And in 2021, UCLA astronomer Jean-Luc Margot — born in 1969 — and colleagues used 15 years of radar speckle tracking from 2006 to 2020 to prove that atmospheric momentum transfer makes Venus's 243.0226-day spin fluctuate by up to 20 minutes while revealing a 3,500-kilometer-radius core deeper inside. [CHAPTER CARD: What Venus's Backwards Clock Teaches Us About Rocky Worlds]

Looking at a planet whose 243-day backwards spin outlasts its 224.7-day year while 360-kilometer-per-hour clouds circle overhead every 4 days, you might think Venus is a lone cosmic freak.

But as University of Toronto astrophysicist Jérémy Leconte and colleagues showed in *Science* on February 6, 2015, atmospheric thermal tides can prevent even thin-atmosphere Earth-mass exoplanets in the habitable zones of red dwarf stars from becoming tidally locked — meaning Venus is our closest natural laboratory for understanding how atmospheres control the spin and climate of rocky worlds across the galaxy.

Across more than four centuries of discovery — from Galileo Galilei's 1610 phases, Mikhail Lomonosov's 1761 transit atmosphere, and Giovanni Schiaparelli's slow-spin prediction, to Gordon H. Pettengill's 1961 radar ranging, Richard M. Goldstein and Roland L. Carpenter's March 8, 1963 *Science* discovery, Irwin I. Shapiro's July 28, 1967 243.09-day measurement, Thomas Gold and Steven Soter's 1969 thermal-tide balance, Jacques Laskar and Alexandre C. M. Correia's June 14, 2001 *Nature* four rotation states, Takeshi Horinouchi's April 24, 2020 *Science* Akatsuki super-rotation study, and Jean-Luc Margot's 2021 20-minute radar wobble and 3,500-kilometer core measurement — science solved the backwards clock of our sister world.

Venus proves that a thick atmosphere and internal friction can brake and reverse an entire planet's spin — and next, we travel beyond the cloud-wrapped inner planets out to the main asteroid belt to ask why NASA launched a spacecraft to a giant metal world called asteroid 16 Psyche.
