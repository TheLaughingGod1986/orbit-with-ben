# Why Does Venus Spin Backwards and Have a Day Longer Than Its Year?

Look from Venus orbit past the golden cloud horizon at Earth's twin — a rocky planet 12,104 kilometers across circling the Sun at 108.21 million kilometers. First observed in 1610 and probed by radar on March 8, 1963, why does Venus take 243 Earth days to rotate once on its axis — longer than its 224.7-day year — and what secret force inside its 92-bar atmosphere makes it spin backwards until the Sun rises in the west and sets in the east? Because solar heating of its carbon dioxide atmosphere creates thermal tides that pull against solar gravity and core-mantle friction, braking Venus into a slow clockwise spin.

Not because Venus is tidally locked facing the Sun forever, but because the mystery of gravitational tides, atmospheric thermal tides, and internal friction locked Earth's neighbor into a 243-day backwards sidereal spin with an axial tilt of 177.36 degrees.

What would you see and feel hovering beneath sulfuric acid clouds that whip around the globe in 4 Earth days — 60 times faster than the ground — or standing on a 464-degree-Celsius surface where sunrise to sunrise takes 116.75 Earth days and day length suddenly wobbles by 20 minutes? Although Venus has 95 percent of Earth's 6,378.1-kilometer radius at 6,051.8 kilometers and 81.5 percent of Earth's mass at 4.8673 times 10 to the 24th kilograms, its 5,832.6-hour rotation is 243.69 times slower than Earth's 23.9345-hour day. Now how did radar pierce those clouds?

[CHAPTER CARD: How Radar Pierced Venus's Clouds and Found a Backwards Spin]

In 1610, Italian astronomer Galileo Galilei — 1564 to 1642 — observed the phases of Venus across its 584-day cycle, and during the 1761 transit Russian polymath Mikhail Lomonosov — 1711 to 1765 — found the first evidence of a Venusian atmosphere.

In the 19th century, observers guessed Venus spun in 24 hours until Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli — 1835 to 1910 — proposed that Venus was tidally locked with the Sun across its 224.7-day orbit.

However, that illusion held until the 1961 conjunction, when MIT physicist Gordon H. Pettengill — 1926 to 2021 — bounced radar pulses deeper through the clouds from Millstone Hill alongside NASA's 26-meter Goldstone antenna in California, Jodrell Bank in the United Kingdom, and Yevpatoria in Crimea.

Then on March 8, 1963, Jet Propulsion Laboratory astronomers Richard M. Goldstein and Roland L. Carpenter reported in *Science* that Doppler echoes revealed a 240-day opposite radar spin — confirmed as retrograde in 1964 while radar refined Venus's solid radius to 6,052 kilometers, 70 kilometers below its clouds. Now why does that spin produce a 116.75-day solar day?

[CHAPTER CARD: The 116.75-Day Solar Day and Where the Sun Rises in the West]

On July 28, 1967, MIT and Harvard astrophysicist Irwin I. Shapiro — born in 1929 — reported in *Science* that radar pinned Venus's retrograde rotation to 243.09 days, plus or minus 0.18, with an equator tilted under 2 degrees. Why is Venus's sidereal day 243 Earth days while sunrise to sunrise takes 116.75 Earth days?

A sidereal day is one 360-degree spin relative to the stars, which takes 243.0226 Earth days — 5,832.6 hours — clockwise, with an obliquity of 177.36 degrees and a 2.64-degree tilt.

Meanwhile, Venus orbits the Sun counterclockwise at 35.02 kilometers per second with 0.0068 eccentricity, completing one year in 224.701 Earth days.

Because clockwise spin and counterclockwise orbit combine, Venus has a 116.75-day solar day — a 116.75-day west-to-east solar day of 2,802.0 hours, or 1.92 solar days per year — where the Sun rises in the west and sets in the east, until the 583.92-day synodic interval at 38.2 million kilometers equals 5.001444 Venusian solar days, presenting the same face every conjunction. Now what force braked Venus and reversed its spin?

[CHAPTER CARD: Crushing 92-Bar Thermal Tides vs Solar Gravity]

If Venus orbits at 0.723 astronomical units where solar gravity tugs strongly on its body, why didn't the Sun tidally lock Venus forever?

As Mariner 2 showed in 1962 and Venera 4 confirmed in 1967, Venus holds an atmosphere of 96.5 percent carbon dioxide and 3.5 percent nitrogen weighing 4.8 times 10 to the 20th kilograms — 92 times Earth's atmospheric mass — exerting 92 bars of pressure at 65 kilograms per cubic meter, or 6.5 percent water's density, and 737 kelvins, or 464 degrees Celsius.

However, because Venus receives 2,601.3 watts per square meter of sunlight — 1.911 times Earth's 1,361.0 watts per square meter — solar heating drives dayside gas toward the nightside until two atmospheric bulges shift ahead of and behind the subsolar line.

Then as Cornell astrophysicist Thomas Gold — 1920 to 2004 — and Steven Soter showed in 1969, solar gravity pulls on those 92-bar thermal tides opposite to solid-body rock tides, locking in a 243-day spin. Now did a giant impact have to flip Venus upside down first?

[CHAPTER CARD: Four Final Rotation States, 60× Winds, and a 20-Minute Wobble]

You might assume the only way a planet gets an axial tilt of 177.36 degrees — spinning backwards compared to Earth — is if a giant impact flipped its spin axis upside down.

Wrong.

While Caltech scientist David J. Stevenson — born in 1948 — and colleagues showed in 2006 that two impacts 10 million years apart could reverse Venus's spin, French astronomer Jacques Laskar — born in 1955 — and Alexandre C. M. Correia proved in *Nature* on June 14, 2001 that any rocky planet with a dense atmosphere evolves through core-mantle friction and atmospheric tides into one of four final rotation states — braking a prograde spin into retrograde rotation without flipping its axis.

Even today, that atmosphere grips the planet: on April 24, 2020, Hokkaido University physicist Takeshi Horinouchi reported in *Science* using Japan's Akatsuki orbiter — in orbit from December 7, 2015 to 2024 — that thermal tides near 70 kilometers altitude drive 60-times-faster cloud super-rotation, with 100-meter-per-second, 360-kilometer-per-hour winds circling Venus in 4 Earth days.

And in 2021, UCLA astronomer Jean-Luc Margot — born in 1969 — used radar tracking from 2006 to 2020 to measure a 20-minute day-length wobble and a 3,500-kilometer-radius core deeper inside. Now what does Venus's spin tell us about worlds beyond our solar system?

[CHAPTER CARD: What Venus's Backwards Clock Teaches Us About Rocky Worlds]

Looking at a planet whose 243-day backwards spin outlasts its 224.7-day year while 360-kilometer-per-hour clouds circle every 4 days, you might think Venus is a lone freak.

But as University of Toronto astrophysicist Jérémy Leconte showed in *Science* on February 6, 2015, thermal tides can prevent even thin-atmosphere exoplanets near red dwarf stars from tidally locking — while on November 7, 2023, DLR physicist Heinz-Wilhelm Hübers reported in *Nature Communications* atomic oxygen at 100 kilometers between Venus's 70-kilometer super-rotating clouds and 120-kilometer upper flow.

From Galileo's 1610 phases, Lomonosov's 1761 transit, and Schiaparelli's slow-spin prediction, to Pettengill's 1961 radar ranging, Goldstein and Carpenter's March 8, 1963 *Science* discovery, Shapiro's July 28, 1967 243.09-day measurement, Gold and Soter's 1969 thermal tides, Laskar and Correia's June 14, 2001 four states, Horinouchi's April 24, 2020 Akatsuki study, and Margot's 2021 20-minute wobble and 3,500-kilometer core — science solved the backwards planetary clock of our sister world.

Venus proves that a thick atmosphere and internal friction can brake and reverse an entire planet's spin — and next, we travel to the main asteroid belt to ask why NASA launched a spacecraft to a giant metal world called asteroid 16 Psyche.
