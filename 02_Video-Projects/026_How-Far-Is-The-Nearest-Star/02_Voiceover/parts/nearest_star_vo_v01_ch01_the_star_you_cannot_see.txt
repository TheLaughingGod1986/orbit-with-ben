The nearest star to the Sun is called Proxima Centauri. It is a red dwarf, a small, cool star, far smaller and dimmer than ours. That is why you will never spot it by eye, even on the darkest night.

It gives out less than one percent of the Sun's light. But small stars burn their fuel slowly. Proxima will still be glowing for trillions of years, long after our own Sun has gone.

But it is not alone. Close beside it in the sky are two much brighter stars, Alpha Centauri A and B, a pair a little further off at about four point three seven light-years. From the southern hemisphere they shine as one of the brightest points in the whole night sky. From Britain, they never rise.

The three are one family. Proxima loops around the bright pair at a vast distance, and one orbit takes it about half a million years. When anyone says "the nearest star", they mean the faint one: Proxima, four point two four light-years away.

A light-year is a distance, not a time. It is how far light travels in a year. So when you look towards Proxima, you are seeing light that left it more than four years ago.
