How do you measure the distance to something you can never visit?

Try this. Hold up your thumb at arm's length. Close one eye, then the other. Your thumb jumps against the background. The closer your thumb, the bigger the jump. Your two eyes are a ruler.

Astronomers use the same trick with the whole of Earth's orbit. Photograph a star in January, and again in July, from the other side of the Sun. A nearby star shifts a tiny amount against the far more distant stars behind it. Measure the shift, and geometry gives you the distance.

The shift is unbelievably small. For centuries nobody could see it at all, and some astronomers took that as proof that Earth must stand still. The real reason was the distance. In 1838 the astronomer Friedrich Bessel measured it for a star called 61 Cygni. The angle he found was like the width of a coin seen from about thirteen kilometres away. It was the first time anyone had measured the distance to a star, and it showed how deep the sky really is.

Today a European spacecraft called Gaia has measured these shifts for more than a billion stars. Proxima's is the largest of all, because it is the nearest. Even so, it is like seeing a coin from about five kilometres away.
