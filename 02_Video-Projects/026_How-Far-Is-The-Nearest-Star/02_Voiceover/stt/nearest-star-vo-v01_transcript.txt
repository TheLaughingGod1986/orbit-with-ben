Look up on a clear night and the stars seem close, scattered just above the rooftops. So how far away is the nearest one? Light, the fastest thing there is, takes more than four years to cross the gap. And here is the strange part. You cannot see that star at all, not without a telescope. In this film, I will show you which star it is, how anyone measured a distance that large, what that gap looks like if you shrink the whole thing down to fit on Earth, and how long it would take to get there. The nearest star to the Sun is called Proxima Centauri. It is a red dwarf, a small, cool star, far smaller and dimmer than ours. That is why you will never spot it by eye, even on the darkest night. It gives out less than one percent of the Sun's light. But small stars burn their fuel slowly. Proxima will still be glowing for trillions of years, long after our own sun has gone. But it is not alone. Close beside it in the sky are two much brighter stars, Alpha Centauri A and B, a pair a little further off at about four point three seven light-years. From the Southern Hemisphere, they shine as one of the brightest points in the whole night sky. From Britain, they never rise. The three are one family. Proxima loops around the bright pair at a vast distance, and one orbit takes it about half a million years. When anyone says the nearest star, they mean the faint one, Proxima, four point two four light-years away. A light-year is a distance, not a time. It is how far light travels in a year. So when you look towards Proxima, you are seeing light that left it more than four years ago. How do you measure the distance to something you can never visit? Try this. Hold up your thumb at arm's length. Close one eye, then the other. Your thumb jumps against the background. The closer your thumb, the bigger the jump. Your two eyes are a ruler. Astronomers use the same trick with the whole of Earth's orbit. Photograph a star in January and again in July from the other side of the Sun. A nearby star shifts a tiny amount against the far more distant stars behind it. Measure the shift, and geometry gives you the distance. The shift is unbelievably small. For centuries, nobody could see it at all, and some astronomers took that as proof that Earth must stand still. The real reason was the distance. In eighteen thirty-eight, the astronomer Friedrich Bessel measured it for a star called 61 Cygni. The angle he found was like the width of a coin seen from about thirteen kilometers away. It was the first time anyone had measured the distance to a star, and it showed how deep the sky really is. Today, a European spacecraft called Gaia has measured these shifts for more than a billion stars. Proxima's is the largest of all because it is the nearest. Even so, it is like seeing a coin from about five kilometers away. We make one of these every week. Subscribing is how the next one finds you. Numbers like four light-years are hard to feel, so let's shrink everything down. Make the Sun the size of a grapefruit and put it on a table. At that scale, Earth is a pinhead about fifteen meters away across a big room. Everything you have ever known, every person, every city sits on that pin. The farthest any human has ever traveled to the Moon is only about four centimeters from it. Now, where is the nearest star? Proxima becomes a cherry, and that cherry is about four thousand kilometers away. Put the grapefruit in London, and the cherry is further away than Cairo. A little beyond it sit the bright pair, Alpha Centauri A and B, one about the size of a big grapefruit, the other more like an orange. Between them, almost nothing. A few specks of dust and an emptiness so complete that it is hard to picture. And our farthest spacecraft? Voyager One, launched in nineteen seventy-seven. After nearly fifty years of flying, at this scale, it has traveled about two and a half kilometers from the grapefruit. It has barely left the town. So how long would the journey take? Walk at a steady pace, never stopping, never sleeping, and you would reach Proxima in about nine hundred million years. Take a car at motorway speed, and it is about forty-six million years, far longer than humans have existed. A jet airliner cuts it to about five million years. Voyager One is the fastest thing we have ever sent out of the Solar System, moving at about seventeen kilometers every second. It is not heading towards Proxima, but at its speed, crossing the same distance would take about seventy-five thousand years. Even light at three hundred thousand kilometers a second needs four years and three months. Put that beside the light you know. Moonlight reaches you in just over a second. Sunlight takes about eight minutes. Light from the nearest star takes more than four years, and that is the shortest trip of its kind there is. So what is waiting at the end of the journey? In 2016, astronomers found a planet around Proxima. Proxima b is at least about as massive as Earth, and it circles its star every 11 days, close enough to sit in the zone where water could be liquid. But do not picture a second Earth yet. Nobody knows what its surface is like or whether it has air at all. Its star is a violent one. Proxima throws out flares, sudden blasts of energy that, for a star its size, are far more violent than our sun's. They may have stripped any atmosphere away. Whether Proxima b could ever be friendly is still open. Could we ever go and look? One plan, called Breakthrough Starshot, imagines probes weighing about as much as a paperclip pushed by giant lasers to a fifth of the speed of light, arriving in about 20 years. Nothing like it has been built yet. It is a plan, and a very hard one. Even a simple hello is slow. A radio message sent from Earth tonight would arrive in early 2031. If someone there answered straight away, the earliest reply would reach us in 2035. So this is the nearest star, a faint red sun you cannot see more than four light-years away with a world we cannot yet know. What if the most important thing about the nearest star is not how close it is but how far? The bigger question sits above you every clear night.
