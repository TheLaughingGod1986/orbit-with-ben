[CHAPTER CARD: Three Earths Wide to One]

In 1610, Italian astronomer Galileo Galilei observed Jupiter's four largest moons through an early telescope, and by the late sixteen hundreds astronomers including Italian-French astronomer Giovanni Domenico Cassini recorded a "permanent spot" on the giant planet.

Formed four point six billion years ago with more than twice the combined mass of all other solar system bodies outside the Sun, Jupiter has a radius of forty-three thousand four hundred and forty point seven miles — sixty-nine thousand nine hundred and eleven kilometres, eleven times wider than Earth, like a basketball beside a grape.

Because Jupiter has no solid ground to slow storms down, its nine-point-nine-hour rotation drives over a dozen prevailing winds reaching three hundred and thirty-five miles per hour — five hundred and thirty-nine kilometres per hour — at the equator.

However in the southern hemisphere, observations in the late eighteen hundreds gauged the Great Red Spot at twenty-five thousand five hundred miles across on its long axis — about forty-one thousand kilometres, wide enough to fit three Earths side by side. [CHAPTER CARD: Losing Five Hundred Eighty Miles Yearly]

Astronomers have followed the downsizing of the Great Red Spot since the nineteen thirties.

When NASA's Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 spacecraft flew past Jupiter in 1979 and 1980, they measured the Great Red Spot at fourteen thousand five hundred miles across — twenty-three thousand three hundred and thirty-five kilometres.

Then after the NASA and ESA Hubble Space Telescope — named for American astronomer Edwin Hubble — launched in 1990, a 1995 Hubble photo showed the spot at thirteen thousand and twenty miles across, and by 2009 it measured eleven thousand one hundred and thirty miles.

Suddenly beginning in 2012, observations revealed that the spot was shrinking by five hundred and eighty miles per year — just under one thousand kilometres annually, changing from an oval to a circle — until planetary scientist Amy Simon at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center announced on May 15, 2014 that April 21, 2014 Hubble images showed the storm at ten thousand two hundred and fifty miles across, just under sixteen thousand five hundred kilometres, as small eddies fed into the vortex. Now how deep beneath Jupiter's clouds do the roots of that storm reach?

[CHAPTER CARD: Three Hundred Miles Below the Clouds]

Wrong. Jupiter's Great Red Spot is not a shallow weather-layer storm confined to the top forty-four miles of clouds.

In Jupiter's skies, three cloud layers — ammonia ice, ammonium hydrosulfide crystals, and water ice and vapour — span about forty-four miles, or seventy-one kilometres, above a transition layer forty miles deeper called the "Jovicline."

However after NASA's Juno spacecraft entered orbit in 2016 and flew over the Great Red Spot on July 11, 2017 and in July 2019 at one hundred and thirty thousand miles per hour, its Microwave Radiometer peered deeper below the water clouds while NASA's Deep Space Network tracked velocity shifts of zero point zero one millimetres per second from over four hundred million miles away.

Published in *Science* on October 28, 2021, those passes proved that the Great Red Spot extends over two hundred miles in microwave data and reaches a gravity depth of about three hundred miles — five hundred kilometres — while surrounding jet streams plunge two thousand miles deep alongside eight Ferrel-like cells per hemisphere, each at least thirty times larger than Earth's. [CHAPTER CARD: The Four-Hundred-MPH Racing Ring]

Although the Great Red Spot has shrunk to about ten thousand miles across — still wide enough for Earth to fit inside — the winds in its outermost lane are speeding up.

Seen from the side, the storm has a tiered wedding-cake structure with high central clouds cascading down to outer layers while sulfur and phosphorus gases paint its crimson bands.

By analysing eleven years of Hubble storm reports from 2009 to 2020 — tracking tens to hundreds of thousands of wind vectors across features as small as one hundred and five miles across, twice the length of Rhode Island, over Jupiter's ten-hour day — researchers at the University of California, Berkeley and NASA Goddard discovered a surprising trend published on September 27, 2021.

While inner winds cruise slowly, the average counterclockwise wind speed in the outer high-speed ring increased by up to eight percent between 2009 and 2020 — changing by less than one point six miles per hour per Earth year to exceed four hundred miles per hour, with abrupt shifts during a nearby 2017 convective storm. Now why did Hubble's time-lapse movie catch the storm jiggling every ninety days?

[CHAPTER CARD: Hubble's Ninety-Day Stress-Ball Wobble]

Between December 2023 and March 2024, when Jupiter ranged from three hundred and ninety-one million to five hundred and twelve million miles from Earth, Amy Simon's team used Hubble over ninety days to track the Great Red Spot across one full oscillation cycle.

Published in *The Planetary Science Journal* and announced on October 9, 2024, the Hubble time-lapse movie revealed that the Great Red Spot is not as stable as it looks: its elliptical shape oscillates on a ninety-day cycle, jiggling like a bowl of gelatin and squeezing in and out like a stress ball at the same time as its longitudinal drift speeds up and slows down.

Then as the storm accelerates and decelerates in longitude, it pushes against the jet streams to its north and south — like a sandwich where the bread bulges out when there is too much filling in the middle, while ultraviolet images show its core grows brightest at its largest size due to less haze absorption. Now does this squeezing and shrinking mean the Great Red Spot is about to vanish?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why the Storm Will Not Vanish]

Beyond Jupiter on Neptune, where strong bounding jet streams are absent, dark storm spots drift in latitude and disappear in a few years, while on Jupiter three smaller ovals merged to form the Little Red Spot, about half the size of its larger cousin.

However on Jupiter, the Great Red Spot has been held at its southern latitude — trapped between eastward and westward jet streams two thousand miles deep — for more than three hundred years.

Having watched the storm shrink since Hubble's Outer Planet Atmospheres Legacy program began ten years earlier, Amy Simon's team explained why the Great Red Spot is still contracting today: right now it is over-filling its southern latitude band relative to the wind field, but once it shrinks inside that band, the winds will hold it in place in a stable, less-elongated shape rather than letting it vanish.
