# Why Knocked-Sideways Uranus Has 21-Year Winters

At the north pole of Uranus, the seventh planet in orbit around our Sun, what would you see when the Sun sinks below the horizon? A single winter night that lasts twenty-one Earth years.

While Earth spins at a twenty-three point four four degree tilt, Uranus is tipped at ninety-seven point seven seven degrees — more than four times Earth's tilt, and nearly at a right angle to its orbit — rolling around the Sun on its side like a ball.

That extreme sideways roll plunges half the planet into twenty-one years of darkness and helps its atmosphere drop to minus two hundred and twenty-four point two degrees Celsius, even colder than farther-out Neptune. How did an entire giant planet get knocked onto its side, and what did our only spacecraft to visit Uranus find there?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Star That Moved]

13 March 1781. British astronomer William Herschel searches the night sky for faint stars through a telescope and spots a pale light that moves.

It was the first planet found with a telescope, and the first new planet discovered since ancient times — though at first, Herschel thought it was a comet or a star.

Two years later, in 1783, astronomers accepted this unknown object as a new planet, in part because of observations by German astronomer Johann Elert Bode.

Herschel tried to name his discovery Georgium Sidus, after the British monarch. Instead, it was named for Uranus, the Greek god of the sky, as suggested by Johann Bode.

With an equatorial diameter of fifty-one thousand one hundred and eighteen kilometres, Uranus is four times wider than Earth. If Earth were a nickel, Uranus would be about as big as a softball. From an average distance of two point nine billion kilometres — about nineteen astronomical units, or nineteen times Earth's distance from the Sun — sunlight takes two hours and forty minutes to reach Uranus, and one orbit takes about eighty-four Earth years.

Yet for nearly two centuries, a deeper secret hid in the dark around it. Why did it take an aeroplane flying at night to reveal that Uranus was tipped onto its side?

[CHAPTER CARD: The World Knocked Sideways]

10 March 1977. Aboard NASA's Kuiper Airborne Observatory and at the Perth Observatory in Australia, scientists watch Uranus pass in front of a distant star, SAO 158687.

Before and after the planet crosses the star, the starlight blinks off and on. Uranus, like Saturn, has rings — standing nearly upright around a sideways world.

Uranus has thirteen faint rings: nine narrow, dark grey inner rings, two more inner rings found in 1986, and two outer rings discovered by Hubble on 22 December 2005 and imaged in colour in 2006 — the inner one reddish and the outer one blue like Saturn's E ring.

Orbiting in that tilted plane are twenty-eight known moons, unique in being named for characters from classic English plays and poems.

Why does Uranus spin at a ninety-seven point seven seven degree tilt — rotating backwards like Venus, with one day taking about seventeen hours, seven hours shorter than Earth's twenty-four-hour day? Scientists think this mystery may come from a collision with an Earth-sized object long ago.

For nearly a quarter of each eighty-four-year Uranian orbit, the Sun shines over one pole, plunging the other half into a twenty-one-year dark winter. What did our only spacecraft to visit Uranus find when it flew into that world?

[CHAPTER CARD: Six Hours at the Ice Giant]

24 January 1986. After travelling more than three billion kilometres in nine years, NASA's Voyager 2 makes the first — and so far the only — visit to Uranus.

Coming within eighty-one thousand five hundred kilometres of the cloud tops, Voyager 2 gathers much of its critical information in just six hours, discovering ten new moons and two new rings.

What gives Uranus its blue-green colour? Its atmosphere is about eighty-two point five per cent hydrogen and fifteen point two per cent helium, with two point three per cent methane gas that absorbs red sunlight and reflects blue-green light back out.

Inside that atmosphere lies a paradox. Uranus's atmosphere drops to a minimum temperature of forty-nine Kelvin — minus two hundred and twenty-four point two degrees Celsius.

Why is that a shock? Because it makes Uranus even colder than Neptune in some places, even though Neptune orbits more than thirty times as far from the Sun as Earth, while Uranus orbits at nineteen times Earth's distance.

In 1986, Voyager 2 saw only a few discrete clouds on a calm globe. Case closed? Is Uranus just a sleepy, frozen world where nothing ever changes?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Corkscrew Magnet]

Wrong. Voyager 2 arrived when one pole stared into the Sun. Twenty-one years later, as Uranus reached its December 2007 equinox — when the Sun passes over its equator and illuminates the whole planet — the giant woke up.

Through Hubble and ground-based telescopes, astronomers saw dynamic clouds and bright storms erupt across Uranus. Wind speeds on Uranus reach up to nine hundred kilometres an hour — five hundred and sixty miles an hour, more than twice as fast as Earth's strongest four-hundred-kilometre-an-hour winds — blowing backwards at the equator, then shifting forward closer to the poles.

Stranger still is the magnetic field Voyager 2 discovered — stronger than Saturn's, yet completely lopsided.

Magnetic fields normally align with a planet's rotation, but Uranus's magnetic axis is tilted nearly sixty degrees from its spin axis, and offset from the planet's centre by one-third of its radius.

Because that lopsided magnet spins sideways every seventeen hours, Uranus's auroras miss the poles, and the magnetosphere tail extending millions of miles behind Uranus twists into a long corkscrew shape. What happens if a spacecraft dives beneath those clouds to land on the ice below?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why You Can't Land on Ice]

That brings us to the biggest misconception about Uranus. People hear it is called an "ice giant" with a minus-two-hundred-and-twenty-four-degree sky, and imagine a spacecraft could land on frozen ground.

As an ice giant, Uranus has no true surface. Beneath the hydrogen and helium clouds, eighty per cent or more of the planet's mass is a hot, dense fluid of water, methane, and ammonia, above a small rocky core.

Near that core, Uranus heats up to nine thousand degrees Fahrenheit — four thousand nine hundred and eighty-two degrees Celsius, more than five thousand degrees hotter than its freezing cloud tops.

While a spacecraft has nowhere to land on Uranus, it could not fly through its atmosphere unscathed either. Extreme pressures and temperatures would destroy a metal spacecraft.

If you cannot land on Uranus, where in this sideways system could you stand on solid ice and look up at those vertical rings?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Ten-Minute Cliff on Miranda]

16 February 1948. At the McDonald Observatory in western Texas, astronomer Gerard Kuiper discovers Miranda in telescopic photos — the last moon of Uranus found before Voyager 2.

At about five hundred kilometres in diameter, Miranda is only one-seventh as large as Earth's Moon, a size unlikely to support much tectonic activity.

Yet when Voyager 2 flew past in January 1986, Miranda looked like Frankenstein's monster, pieced together from parts that did not merge properly. Carving across its surface are giant fault canyons as much as twelve times as deep as Earth's Grand Canyon.

Because of Miranda's low gravity and towering cliffs, a rock dropped off the highest cliff would take ten minutes to reach the foot of the cliff.

On 19 April 2022, the Planetary Science Decadal Survey for 2023 to 2032 outlined a new Uranus mission as a highest priority objective, and in October 2024 Hubble and New Horizons simultaneously observed Uranus from two viewpoints.

Uranus is a sideways giant of twenty-one-year winters, corkscrew magnetism and ten-minute cliffs. Next in our Real Worlds series, we follow New Horizons beyond Uranus to Pluto, where a giant heart of nitrogen ice hides without a single crater.
