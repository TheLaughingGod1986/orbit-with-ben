# Why This Planet Has 21-Year Winters

If you stood at the north pole of Uranus, the seventh planet in orbit around our Sun, would you survive the wait for sunrise after sunset? Not for twelve hours. You would wait in freezing darkness for twenty-one Earth years.

While every other planet spins roughly upright, Uranus is tipped at ninety-seven point seven seven degrees — nearly at a right angle to its orbit — rolling around the Sun on its side like a ball.

So why is a planet closer to the Sun than Neptune even colder than Neptune in some places, why does its magnetic field twist into a giant corkscrew, and what happens if you drop a rock off the cliffs of its strangest moon?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Star That Moved]

13 March 1781. British astronomer William Herschel searches the night sky for faint stars through a telescope and spots a pale light that moves.

It was the first planet found with the aid of a telescope, and the first new planet discovered since ancient times — though at first, Herschel thought it was a comet or a star.

Two years later, in 1783, astronomers accepted this unknown object as a new planet, in part because of observations by German astronomer Johann Elert Bode.

Herschel tried unsuccessfully to name his discovery Georgium Sidus, after King George the Third. Instead, the planet was named for Uranus, the Greek god of the sky, as suggested by Johann Bode.

With an equatorial diameter of fifty-one thousand one hundred and eighteen kilometres, Uranus is four times wider than Earth. If Earth were the size of a nickel, Uranus would be about as big as a softball. From an average distance of two point nine billion kilometres — about nineteen astronomical units from the Sun — sunlight takes two hours and forty minutes to reach Uranus, and one orbit around the Sun takes about eighty-four Earth years.

Yet for nearly two centuries, a deeper secret hid in the dark around it. Why did it take an aeroplane flying at night to reveal that the Uranian system was tipped onto its side?

[CHAPTER CARD: The World Knocked Sideways]

10 March 1977. Aboard NASA's Kuiper Airborne Observatory and at the Perth Observatory in Australia, scientists watch Uranus pass in front of a distant star, SAO 158687.

Before and after the planet crosses the star, the starlight blinks off and on. Uranus, like Saturn, is encircled with rings — standing nearly upright around a sideways world.

Uranus is surrounded by thirteen faint rings in two sets: nine narrow, dark grey inner rings, and two outer rings discovered by Hubble in December 2005 — the inner one reddish and the outer one blue like Saturn's E ring.

Orbiting inside that plane are twenty-eight known moons, unique in being named for characters from William Shakespeare and Alexander Pope.

Why does Uranus spin at a ninety-seven point seven seven degree tilt — rotating in the opposite direction to most planets, like Venus, with one day taking about seventeen hours? Scientists think this mystery may be the result of a collision with an Earth-sized object long ago.

For nearly a quarter of each eighty-four-year Uranian year, the Sun shines directly over each pole, plunging the other half into a twenty-one-year-long, dark winter. What did our only spacecraft to visit Uranus find when it flew into that world?

[CHAPTER CARD: Six Hours at the Ice Giant]

24 January 1986. After travelling more than three billion kilometres in nine years, NASA's Voyager 2 makes the first — and so far the only — visit to Uranus.

Coming within eighty-one thousand five hundred kilometres of the cloud tops, Voyager 2 gathers much of its critical information about the planet, rings and moons in just six hours, discovering ten new moons and two new rings.

What gives Uranus its blue-green colour? Its atmosphere is mostly hydrogen, at about eighty-two point five per cent, and helium, at about fifteen point two per cent, with about two point three per cent methane gas. Methane absorbs the red portion of sunlight and reflects blue-green light back out.

Inside that atmosphere lies a paradox. Uranus's planetary atmosphere drops to a minimum temperature of forty-nine Kelvin — minus two hundred and twenty-four point two degrees Celsius.

Why is that a shock? Because it makes Uranus even colder than Neptune in some places, even though Neptune is more than thirty times as far from the Sun as Earth, while Uranus orbits at about nineteen times Earth's distance.

In 1986, Voyager 2 saw only a few discrete clouds on a calm globe. Case closed? Is Uranus just a sleepy, frozen world where nothing ever changes?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Corkscrew Magnet]

Wrong. Voyager 2 arrived when one pole stared into the Sun. Twenty-one years later, as Uranus reached its December 2007 equinox — when the Sun passes over its equator and illuminates the whole planet — the giant woke up.

Through Hubble and ground-based telescopes, astronomers saw dynamic clouds and rapidly changing bright features erupt across Uranus. Wind speeds on Uranus reach up to nine hundred kilometres an hour — five hundred and sixty miles an hour — blowing backwards against the planet's rotation at the equator, then shifting to blow forward closer to the poles.

Stranger still is the magnetic field Voyager 2 discovered — stronger than Saturn's, yet completely lopsided.

Magnetic fields normally align with a planet's rotation, but Uranus's magnetic axis is tilted nearly sixty degrees from its spin axis, and offset from the planet's centre by one-third of its radius.

Because that lopsided magnet spins sideways every seventeen hours, Uranus's auroras miss the poles, and the magnetosphere tail extending millions of miles behind Uranus twists into a long corkscrew shape. Why is that magnetic field off-centre — and what if a spacecraft tried to land on the ice below?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why You Can't Land on Ice]

That brings us to the biggest misconception about Uranus. People hear it is called an "ice giant" with a minus-two-hundred-and-twenty-four-degree sky, and imagine a spacecraft could land on a solid frozen surface.

Let us test that idea with physics. As an ice giant, Uranus does not have a true surface.

Beneath the hydrogen and helium clouds, most — eighty per cent or more — of the planet's mass is a hot, dense fluid of "icy" materials: water, methane, and ammonia, above a small rocky core.

Near that core, Uranus heats up to nine thousand degrees Fahrenheit — four thousand nine hundred and eighty-two degrees Celsius.

While a spacecraft would have nowhere to land on Uranus, it could not fly through its atmosphere unscathed either. The extreme pressures and temperatures would destroy a metal spacecraft.

If you cannot land on Uranus itself, where in this sideways system could you stand on solid ice and look up at those vertical rings?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Ten-Minute Cliff on Miranda]

16 February 1948. At the McDonald Observatory in western Texas, astronomer Gerard Kuiper discovers Miranda in telescopic photos — the smallest and closest of the five Uranian moons known before Voyager 2.

At about five hundred kilometres in diameter, Miranda is only one-seventh as large as Earth's Moon, a size unlikely to support much tectonic activity.

Yet when Voyager 2 flew past in January 1986, Miranda looked like Frankenstein's monster, pieced together from parts that did not merge properly. Carving across its surface are giant fault canyons as much as twelve times as deep as Earth's Grand Canyon.

Because of Miranda's low gravity and large cliffs, a rock dropped off the edge of the highest cliff would take a full ten minutes to reach the foot of the cliff.

Because Voyager 2 only had six hours at Uranus, on 19 April 2022 the Planetary Science Decadal Survey for 2023 to 2032 outlined a new mission to Uranus as a highest priority objective, and in October 2024 Hubble and New Horizons simultaneously observed Uranus from two viewpoints.

Uranus is a sideways giant of twenty-one-year winters, corkscrew magnetism and ten-minute cliffs. Next, we follow New Horizons past the orbit of Uranus to the frozen edge of the solar system, where Pluto hides a giant heart of nitrogen ice without a single crater.
