Today, by a cosmic coincidence that still feels like a gift, the Moon and the Sun look almost the same size in our sky.

That is why a total solar eclipse can happen — a clean black disc, a sudden wrong night, a corona like a white crown around an absence.

It will not last forever.

As the Moon drifts outward, its apparent size shrinks. Perfect totals become rarer. There will still be alignments. There will still be drama. But eventually the Moon sits too far to cover the Sun completely.

Then many eclipses become rings of fire — annular — a bright hoop where darkness used to win.

Are we living in a special window — an unknown expiry date dressed as forever?

Yes. A narrow one, on the scale of worlds. Early Earth saw a Moon too close for the polite geometry of totality as you know it. Far-future Earth will see a Moon too small to erase the Sun.

You live in the middle — the era of perfect covers.

Would your distant descendants still gasp at totality the way you do?

They might gasp at a ring instead. Same dance of shadows. Different costume. The physics does not care about our nostalgia. It only cares about angles, distances, and gravity’s slow bookkeeping.

Orbit stands in the path of totality and feels the temperature drop — then the future version refuses to go fully dark. Suddenly the black coin fails.

Some wonders have an expiry date written in orbital mechanics.

So what should you take from a Moon that is leaving?

Not panic.

Perspective.
