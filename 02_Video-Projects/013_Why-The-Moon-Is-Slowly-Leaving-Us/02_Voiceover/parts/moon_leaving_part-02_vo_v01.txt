Then the “fixed” Moon becomes a moving mass in a changing orbit — gravity still binding it, distance still growing.

Could you feel the drift if you lived a thousand years? What if patience is the whole mystery?

No. You’d feel tides, seasons, eclipses — not the centimetres. The Moon’s departure is a conspiracy of patience. It does not slam a door. It loosens a bolt so slowly the house forgets the bolt was ever tight.

Until you measure it. Until the laser’s round-trip stretches. Until the centimetres stack into kilometres, then into a different sky.

So if the change is real… what did the Earth–Moon system look like when that gap was smaller?

Wind the clock back.

The Moon was closer. Much closer. Models of the early Earth–Moon system put the young Moon on a tighter leash after the giant impact that likely built it from debris — near enough that the lunar disc would have dominated the sky like a looming continent of stone.

Then tides stop being polite coastal rhythms. They become planetary muscles flexing the oceans. Shorelines would have been rewritten on a schedule that would terrify a modern harbour master.

Earth’s day was shorter then. The planet spun faster. A “day” could have been a handful of hours — a strobe of light and dark instead of the long breath you call morning and night.

Would you recognise that world as home? The chemistry of the air, maybe. The ocean’s salt, maybe. The sky? No. The Moon would feel like a neighbour leaning over the fence — not a distant silver coin.

So the Moon wasn’t always “out there.”

It was near enough to boss the oceans — and near enough to help teach Earth the length of daylight.

But what changed?

Why did near become far?
