What if the Moon is already leaving you? Look up tonight. That white coin feels permanent, pinned to the dark like a signal that never moves. But it isn't. Not by myth, not by disaster film. By centimeters every year, measured by lasers bouncing off mirrors humans left in the dust. How fast is it going? Why can't Earth's gravity keep it? And what does that quiet escape mean for the tides under your feet, for the length of your day, and for the last perfect eclipse your grandchildren might never see? Then Orbit looks up with you, and the sky stops feeling fixed. The Moon is leaving us. Why pretend the sky is a finished painting? Apollo crews and robotic landers left laser reflectors on the lunar surface, arrays of corner mirrors that send light straight back where it came from. Observatories on Earth fire photon pulses at those mirrors and time the round trip. Year after year, the trip takes a little longer. The gap grows by about three-point-eight centimeters a year, roughly a fingernail's width. Quiet enough that no calendar on your wall notices. Patient enough that geology does. If you've only ever known this sky, that sounds like nothing. But nothing kept up for a hundred million years remakes the architecture of a world
