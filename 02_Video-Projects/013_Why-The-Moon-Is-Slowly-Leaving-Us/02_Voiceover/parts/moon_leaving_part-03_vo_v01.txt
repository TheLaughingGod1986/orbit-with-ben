Here is the engine — and it is still running under your feet.

Earth’s gravity raises tides on the Moon. The Moon’s gravity raises tides on Earth — mostly in the oceans, and a little in the solid crust itself.

Those tidal bulges do not sit perfectly under the Moon. Earth spins faster than the Moon completes a month, so friction drags the oceanic bulge a little ahead of the Moon’s position.

Then that leading bulge pulls forward on the Moon.

Forward pull means the Moon gains orbital energy.

Gaining orbital energy means climbing to a wider orbit — centimetre by centimetre, year by year.

Meanwhile Earth loses a little spin. Your day lengthens. Not enough for a Thursday to feel wrong — enough that over deep time, hours are added to the clock of a single rotation.

Is this a fight between two worlds — or a secret ledger written in water?

It is a negotiation written in water and rock. Friction spends Earth’s rotation like a currency. The Moon banks that energy as distance. The ledger balances. Nobody gets a refund.

However neat the diagram looks, the process is messy: coasts, basins, and solid-Earth tides all contribute. The spectrum of Earth’s responses is not a single pure note — but the net effect is clear.

Could it reverse tomorrow?

Not while Earth still has oceans and still spins faster than the Moon orbits. As long as that gear turns this way, the Moon keeps cashing cheques drawn on the length of your day.

Fossils even whisper corroboration: ancient corals and shells record more days per year when Earth spun faster — a diary of a quicker world written in calcium before anyone invented clocks. Suddenly deep time has receipts.

So if the Moon keeps leaving… what do we lose?

Not just romance.

Geometry.
