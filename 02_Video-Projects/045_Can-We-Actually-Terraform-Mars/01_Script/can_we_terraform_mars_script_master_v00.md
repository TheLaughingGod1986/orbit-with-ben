# Can We Actually Terraform Mars, and Why Does Even a Robot Need Help?

Orbiting one hundred and forty-two million miles from the Sun, as you cross the rusty horizon of a freezing desert where surface air pressure is just zero point six percent of Earth's, can we actually terraform Mars, and why does even a robot need help to survive there?

Formed four point five billion years ago with a radius of two thousand one hundred and six miles — about half the width of Earth, yet with nearly the same surface area as Earth's dry land — Mars is the only planet we know of inhabited entirely by robots, where a 2018 NASA study proved that vaporising the polar ice caps would only double atmospheric pressure to one point two percent of Earth's.

Why did NASA's MAVEN orbiter find the solar wind stripping away one hundred grams of Martian atmosphere every second, what paradox did scientists uncover when they tallied every carbon reservoir buried across the Red Planet, and what happens next after Perseverance's microwave-sized MOXIE experiment generated one hundred and twenty-two grams of breathable oxygen directly from thin Martian air?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Planet Inhabited Entirely by Robots]

On July 20, 1976, NASA's nine-hundred-and-seventy-eight-kilogram Viking 1 lander touched down at Chryse Planitia, recording temperatures from minus one hundred and twenty-three degrees Fahrenheit before dawn to minus twenty-seven degrees in the afternoon, until the site was renamed the Thomas Mutch Memorial Station in January 1982 for Viking imaging team leader Thomas A. Mutch.

Then on July 4, 1997, NASA's Mars Pathfinder inflated seventeen-foot airbags three hundred and thirty feet above the ground and bounced fifteen times as high as fifty feet — becoming the Carl Sagan Memorial Station for American astronomer Carl Sagan and deploying the twenty-three-pound, one-foot-tall Sojourner rover named after American civil-rights crusader Sojourner Truth.

Beyond Earth at one point five astronomical units from the Sun, where sunlight takes thirteen minutes to arrive and solar irradiance drops to five hundred and eighty-six point two watts per square metre, Mars rotates every twenty-four point six hours in a six-hundred-and-sixty-nine-point-six-sol year with a twenty-five-degree axial tilt and thirty-eight percent of Earth's gravity.

However, Mars's atmosphere averages just six point three six millibars — ninety-five point one percent carbon dioxide and zero point one six percent oxygen — where temperatures swing from seventy degrees Fahrenheit down to minus two hundred and twenty-five degrees, so liquid water quickly evaporates or freezes. Now if Mars is so thin and freezing today, how did floods carve its ancient valleys?

[CHAPTER CARD: Where the Ancient Martian Air Went]

About three point five billion years ago, catastrophic floods carved the Ares Vallis floodplain with a volume of water the size of North America's Great Lakes in about two weeks, across a world holding Valles Marineris — two thousand four hundred miles long and five point eight miles deep — and Olympus Mons, rising twenty-five miles high across a base as large as Arizona.

However, Mars has no global magnetic field today, only magnetized crust in its southern hemisphere left from four billion years ago.

Until NASA's MAVEN orbiter arrived, the fate of Mars's ancient air remained a mystery — then on November 5, 2015, results highlighted by astronaut and NASA science associate administrator John Grunsfeld revealed that the one-million-mile-per-hour solar wind strips away Martian gas at about one hundred grams, roughly a quarter of a pound, every second, with seventy-five percent escaping down the tail and twenty-five percent from the polar plume.

Worse still, without a magnetic shield or thick air, Curiosity's radiation detector measured zero point six four millisieverts per day on the surface — about three months of Earth's two-point-four-millisievert annual natural background radiation in a single Martian day. Now could we restore that lost atmosphere simply by melting Mars's polar ice caps?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why Vaporising the Poles Is Not Enough]

Wrong. Vaporising Mars's polar ice caps would not give the Red Planet a warm, Earth-like sky.

For decades, writers and scientists proposed releasing carbon dioxide trapped in the Martian surface to thicken the atmosphere and warm the planet enough for liquid water to stay stable on the ground.

However, because Mars orbits farther from the Sun than Earth, researchers estimate that a carbon dioxide pressure similar to Earth's total atmospheric pressure is needed to warm the surface enough for stable liquid water, and water vapour cannot provide significant warming by itself because temperatures are too low for water to persist as vapour without carbon dioxide warming the planet first.

Then in July 2018, a NASA-sponsored study combining twenty years of data from Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, Mars Odyssey, and MAVEN revealed the catch: vaporising Mars's polar ice caps by spreading dust or using explosives would only double Martian pressure from zero point six percent of Earth's to one point two percent. What if we heated the Martian soil or mined deeper into its rocks instead?

[CHAPTER CARD: Strip-Mining a Planet a Hundred Yards Deep]

When the 2018 NASA team tallied every other carbon source across Mars, the shortfall remained just as stark.

Carbon dioxide attached to dust particles in Martian soil could provide up to four percent of the needed pressure, but releasing it would require strip-mining the entire planet to a depth of around one hundred yards and heating the soil.

Beyond the soil, carbon locked in shallow mineral deposits would yield less than five percent of the required pressure, and carbon dioxide trapped in water-ice clathrates would also contribute less than five percent, while recovering unknown deep crustal carbonates requires heating rock above three hundred degrees Celsius, or five hundred and seventy-two degrees Fahrenheit.

Worse, importing volatiles by redirecting comets and asteroids would take many thousands of impacts, and even if atmospheric loss to space stopped now, volcanic outgassing is so low that it would take about ten million years just to double Mars's current atmosphere. However, if present-day technology cannot terraform the whole planet, how can robots make breathable air on Mars right now?

[CHAPTER CARD: A Microwave-Sized Box Making Oxygen]

Rather than hauling all their oxygen from Earth or waiting ten million years for volcanoes, future astronauts can live off the land through robotic in-situ resource utilization.

Lowered into the chassis of NASA's Perseverance rover in 2019, a microwave-oven-sized device built by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology called MOXIE — the Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment — pumped in thin Martian air and electrochemically separated one oxygen atom from each molecule of carbon dioxide, while Perseverance's SuperCam microphone recorded MOXIE's air compressor on May 27, 2021, the ninety-sixth sol of the mission.

Then across sixteen runs through a full Martian year, ending with nine point eight grams on August 7, 2023 and highlighted by NASA deputy administrator Pam Melroy, MOXIE generated a total of one hundred and twenty-two grams of molecular oxygen — about what a small dog breathes in ten hours.

At its peak, MOXIE produced twelve grams of oxygen per hour — twice NASA's original goal — at ninety-eight percent purity or better. Now how do engineers turn that microwave-sized breakthrough into a human outpost?

[CHAPTER CARD: Twin Orbiters and the Real Mars Roadmap]

The next step beyond MOXIE is not a planet-wide atmosphere overnight, but a full-scale robotic system that generates, liquefies, and stores industrial quantities of oxygen — both for astronauts to breathe inside shielded habitats and for rocket propellant to launch them home against Mars's five-point-zero-three-kilometre-per-second escape velocity.

Meanwhile, as Perseverance and Curiosity explore two sides of Mars about two thousand three hundred miles apart — with Perseverance collecting sealed rock cores such as its twenty-fifth sample at Sapphire Canyon — NASA is also mapping the solar wind that stripped Mars's ancient air.

Launched on November 13, 2025, NASA's twin refrigerator-sized ESCAPADE orbiters — Escape and Plasma Acceleration and Dynamics Explorers — arrive at Mars in September 2027 and begin their science mission the following spring, studying Mars's real-time response to space weather to help protect future explorers.

Next, we travel outward beyond Jupiter to Saturn's icy moon Enceladus — a three-hundred-and-ten-mile world as wide as Arizona that shoots geysers of ocean water and hydrothermal hydrogen eight hundred miles per hour into space.
