# Can We Actually Terraform Mars, and Why Does Even a Robot Need Help?

Orbiting one hundred and forty-two million miles from the Sun, as you cross the rusty horizon of a cold, dry desert where atmospheric pressure is around zero point six percent of Earth's, can we actually terraform Mars, and why does even a robot need help there?

Formed about four point five billion years ago with a radius of two thousand one hundred and six miles — about half the diameter of Earth, yet with nearly the same surface area as Earth's dry land — Mars is the only planet we know of inhabited entirely by robots, where a 2018 NASA-sponsored study showed that vaporising the polar ice caps would only double Martian pressure to one point two percent of Earth's.

Why did NASA's MAVEN spacecraft find the solar wind stripping away about one hundred grams of gas every second, what paradox did researchers uncover when they analysed every carbon reservoir across the planet, and what happens next after Perseverance's microwave-oven-sized MOXIE device generated one hundred and twenty-two grams of oxygen from thin Martian air?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Planet Inhabited Entirely by Robots]

On July 20, 1976, NASA's nine hundred and seventy-eight kilogram Viking 1 lander touched down on the western slopes of Chryse Planitia, recording minus one hundred and twenty-three degrees Fahrenheit before dawn to minus twenty-seven degrees in the afternoon, until in January 1982 it was renamed the Thomas Mutch Memorial Station for Viking imaging team leader Thomas A. Mutch.

Then on July 4, 1997, Mars Pathfinder inflated seventeen-foot airbags about three hundred and thirty feet above Mars and bounced about fifteen times as high as fifty feet — named the Carl Sagan Memorial Station for astronomer Carl Sagan and deploying the twenty-three-pound, one-foot-tall Sojourner rover named after civil-rights crusader Sojourner Truth.

Beyond Earth at one point five astronomical units from the Sun, where sunlight takes thirteen minutes to arrive and solar irradiance is five hundred and eighty-six point two watts per square metre, Mars rotates every twenty-four point six hours in a six hundred and eighty-seven Earth-day year with a twenty-five-degree tilt and thirty-eight percent of Earth's gravity.

However, Mars's surface pressure averages just six point three six millibars — ninety-five point one percent carbon dioxide and zero point one six percent oxygen — and temperatures range from seventy degrees Fahrenheit down to minus two hundred and twenty-five degrees, so surface liquid water quickly evaporates or freezes. Now if Mars is so cold and dry today, how did floods carve its ancient channels?

[CHAPTER CARD: Where the Ancient Martian Air Went]

About three point five billion years ago, Mars experienced huge floods — cutting the Ares Vallis floodplain with a volume of water the size of North America's Great Lakes in about two weeks, across a world with Valles Marineris extending two thousand four hundred miles long and five point eight miles deep beside Olympus Mons, standing more than twenty-five miles tall across an Arizona-sized base.

However, Mars has no global magnetic field today, only magnetized southern-hemisphere crust from four billion years ago.

Until MAVEN arrived, the speed of Mars's atmospheric loss remained uncertain — then on November 5, 2015, results highlighted by astronaut and NASA science associate administrator John Grunsfeld revealed that the one-million-mile-per-hour solar wind strips away gas at about one hundred grams, roughly a quarter of a pound, every second, with almost seventy-five percent coming from the tail and nearly twenty-five percent from the polar plume.

Worse still, under that thin atmosphere, Curiosity's Radiation Assessment Detector measured zero point six four millisieverts per day on the surface — about three months of Earth's two point four millisievert annual natural background exposure in a single Martian day. Now could we restore that lost atmosphere by vaporising Mars's polar ice caps?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why Vaporising the Poles Is Not Enough]

Wrong. Vaporising Mars's polar ice caps would not give the Red Planet a warm, thick atmosphere.

For decades, writers and scientists proposed releasing carbon dioxide trapped in the Martian surface to thicken the atmosphere and warm the planet.

However, because Mars is farther from the Sun, researchers estimate that a carbon dioxide pressure similar to Earth's total atmospheric pressure is needed for stable liquid water, and water cannot provide significant warming by itself because temperatures are too low for water vapour to persist without prior warming by carbon dioxide.

Then in July 2018, a NASA-sponsored study combining about twenty years of observations from Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, Mars Odyssey, and MAVEN revealed the catch: vaporising the polar ice caps by spreading dust or using explosives would only double Martian pressure to one point two percent of Earth's. What if we heated Martian soil or mined its minerals instead?

[CHAPTER CARD: Strip-Mining a Planet a Hundred Yards Deep]

When the 2018 team analysed every other carbon source on Mars, the shortfall remained just as stark.

Carbon dioxide attached to dust particles in Martian soil could provide up to four percent of the needed pressure, but releasing it would require strip-mining the entire planet to a depth of around one hundred yards.

Beyond the soil, carbon locked in shallow mineral deposits would yield less than five percent of the required pressure, and carbon dioxide trapped in water-ice clathrates would contribute less than five percent, while recovering deeper crustal carbon-bearing minerals requires temperatures above three hundred degrees Celsius.

Worse, importing volatiles by redirecting comets and asteroids to hit Mars would require many thousands, and even if atmospheric loss stopped, current geologic outgassing is so low that it would take about ten million years just to double Mars's atmosphere. However, if present-day technology cannot terraform Mars, how can robots extract breathable oxygen right now?

[CHAPTER CARD: A Microwave-Sized Box Making Oxygen]

Rather than hauling all their oxygen from Earth or waiting ten million years for volcanoes, astronauts can extract oxygen directly from the Martian atmosphere.

Lowered into Perseverance's chassis in 2019, a microwave-oven-sized device created at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology called MOXIE — the Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment — pumped in thin Martian air and separated one oxygen atom from each carbon dioxide molecule, while Perseverance's SuperCam microphone recorded MOXIE's air compressor on May 27, 2021, the ninety-sixth day of the mission.

Then across sixteen runs, ending with nine point eight grams on August 7, 2023 and highlighted by NASA deputy administrator Pam Melroy, MOXIE generated one hundred and twenty-two grams of oxygen — about what a small dog breathes in ten hours.

At its most efficient, MOXIE produced twelve grams of oxygen an hour — twice NASA's original goal — at ninety-eight percent purity or better. Now how do engineers turn that microwave-oven-sized device into a human outpost?

[CHAPTER CARD: Twin Orbiters and the Real Mars Roadmap]

Beyond MOXIE, the next step is a full-scale system that includes an oxygen generator like MOXIE and a way to liquefy and store that oxygen — supplying breathable air and rocket propellant against Mars's five point zero three kilometre per second escape velocity.

Meanwhile, as Perseverance and Curiosity explore different areas of Mars about two thousand three hundred miles apart — with Perseverance collecting its twenty-fifth sample, Sapphire Canyon — NASA is also studying Mars's real-time response to the solar wind.

Launched on November 13, 2025, NASA's twin refrigerator-sized ESCAPADE orbiters — Escape and Plasma Acceleration and Dynamics Explorers — arrive at Mars in September 2027 and begin their science mission the following spring to study Mars's climate history.

Next, we travel outward beyond Jupiter to Saturn's icy moon Enceladus — a three-hundred-and-ten-mile world as wide as Arizona that shoots geysers of icy water particles and gas eight hundred miles per hour into space.
