# Why Does Saturn's Moon Enceladus Shoot Geysers Into Space?

Orbiting one hundred and forty-eight thousand miles from Saturn within the densest part of the planet's outermost E-ring, as you cross a frozen horizon where surface temperatures drop to minus three hundred and thirty degrees Fahrenheit, why does Saturn's moon Enceladus shoot geysers into space?

Only about three hundred and ten miles across — about four percent the size of Earth and about as wide as Arizona — Enceladus has the whitest, most reflective surface in the solar system, yet in 2005 NASA's Cassini spacecraft discovered icy water particles and gas gushing at approximately eight hundred miles per hour from warm fractures near its south pole.

Why did measurements of the moon's slight orbital wobble prove that its icy crust floats over a global subsurface ocean, what secret did Cassini uncover when it dived thirty miles above the surface to sniff hydrothermal hydrogen and sodium phosphates a hundred times richer than Earth's oceans, and what happens next after the James Webb Space Telescope mapped a water-vapour plume spanning more than six thousand miles?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Whitest World in the Solar System]

On August 28, 1789, British astronomer William Herschel spotted Enceladus orbiting Saturn, and in his 1847 Cape of Good Hope observations his son John Herschel named the moon after the giant Enceladus of Greek mythology because Saturn — Cronus in Greek myth — was the leader of the Titans.

Then in the 1980s, NASA's Voyager spacecraft revealed that although Enceladus is only about three hundred and ten miles across — five hundred kilometres, with a mass of one point zero eight times ten to the twentieth kilograms and a mean density of one thousand six hundred and ten kilograms per cubic metre — its icy surface is bright white all over, holding a visual geometric albedo of one point zero as the most reflective body in the solar system.

Because Enceladus reflects so much sunlight a billion miles farther from the Sun than Earth, its surface temperature drops to about minus three hundred and thirty degrees Fahrenheit, or minus two hundred and one degrees Celsius.

However, while parts of Enceladus show craters up to twenty-two miles across, its south polar region is almost entirely free of impact craters and littered with house-sized ice boulders. Now if Enceladus is so small and frigid, what keeps its south pole warm enough to erupt?

[CHAPTER CARD: Dione's Gravity and the Tiger Stripes]

Beyond Saturn's main rings at one hundred and forty-eight thousand miles — two hundred and thirty-eight thousand kilometres, or three point nine five Saturnian radii — Enceladus orbits between Mimas and Tethys, keeping the same face tidally locked toward Saturn every thirty-two point nine hours within the densest part of Saturn's E-ring.

However, Enceladus is also trapped in an orbital resonance with a larger moon, Dione — discovered on March 21, 1684 by Italian-French astronomer Giovanni Domenico Cassini and orbiting Saturn every two point seven days at two hundred and thirty-four thousand five hundred miles — so Enceladus orbits Saturn twice every time Dione orbits once.

Because Dione's gravity stretches Enceladus's orbit into an ellipse, tidal heating warms the moon's interior until, in 2005, NASA's Cassini spacecraft discovered icy water particles and gas gushing continuously from warm south-polar fractures called "tiger stripes" at approximately eight hundred miles per hour, or four hundred metres per second.

Inside that plume, organic materials were about twenty times denser than scientists expected, while most of the spray falls like snow back to the surface and a small fraction escapes to build Saturn's E-ring. Now does that spray come from a small local pocket of water, or a global ocean?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Wobble That Proved an Ocean]

Wrong. Enceladus's outer ice shell is not frozen solid to its rocky core, and its south-polar geysers are not fed by a mere local pond.

Early analysis of Cassini data had suggested only a lens-shaped body of water underlying the moon's south polar region.

Then Cassini scientists analysed more than seven years of spacecraft images taken since mid-2004, mapping surface craters across hundreds of pictures to measure changes in Enceladus's rotation with extreme precision.

In September 2015, a team led by Cassini imaging scientist Peter Thomas at Cornell University alongside Matthew Tiscareno and Cassini imaging team lead Carolyn Porco at the Space Science Institute announced that Enceladus's orbital wobble — or libration — is far larger than it would be if the surface and core were rigidly connected, proving that a global liquid-water ocean lies deeper beneath an ice shell averaging twelve to sixteen miles thick globally and thinning to half a mile to three miles at the south pole. Now what is happening on the dark seafloor at the bottom of that global ocean?

[CHAPTER CARD: Diving Thirty Miles Above the Plume]

On October 28, 2015, NASA's Cassini spacecraft executed its deepest and last dive through the Enceladus plume, passing thirty miles — forty-nine kilometres — above the moon's surface.

Using its Ion and Neutral Mass Spectrometer — originally designed to sample the upper atmosphere of Titan — Cassini sniffed the spray until, in April 2017, lead author Hunter Waite alongside Cassini project scientist Linda Spilker and NASA science associate administrator Thomas Zurbuchen announced that nearly ninety-eight percent of the gas in the plume is water vapour, about one percent is molecular hydrogen, and the rest is a mixture including carbon dioxide, methane, and ammonia.

Meanwhile, in Saturn's E-ring, Cassini's Cosmic Dust Analyzer detected peculiar nanograins of silica, which can only be generated where liquid water and rock interact at temperatures above about two hundred degrees Fahrenheit — ninety degrees Celsius.

Together, those silica nanograins and hydrogen gas prove that water circulates deeper through Enceladus's seafloor and pours out of chimney-like hydrothermal vents, providing chemical energy for methanogenesis where microbes could combine hydrogen with dissolved carbon dioxide. However, did Enceladus's ocean hold the rarest chemical building block required for life?

[CHAPTER CARD: Phosphorus a Hundred Times Earth's Oceans]

Life as we know it requires six primary chemical elements — carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur — but phosphorus, the least abundant of those bio-essential elements, had never been detected in an ocean beyond Earth.

Then planetary scientist Frank Postberg and colleagues surveyed three hundred and forty-five high-salinity Type 3 ice grains recorded in Saturn's E-ring by Cassini's Cosmic Dust Analyzer, where average salt concentrations reach zero point five to two point zero percent by weight.

On June 14, 2023, the team reported in *Nature* that nine of those ice grains showed prominent mass spectral peaks at one hundred and twenty-five, one hundred and sixty-five, and one hundred and eighty-seven atomic mass units — the unmistakable signature of sodium phosphates.

Backed by hydrothermal laboratory experiments at one hundred and fifty degrees Celsius and thirty megapascals for up to one thousand hours, the discovery proved that Enceladus's alkaline, carbonate-rich ocean dissolves calcium phosphate minerals from its rocky core to reach orthophosphate concentrations at least one hundred times higher than in Earth's oceans. Now how far across the Saturnian system does Enceladus's water spray actually reach?

[CHAPTER CARD: Webb's Six-Thousand-Mile Water Plume]

Beyond Cassini's close flybys, researchers led by Geronimo Villanueva and Stefanie Milam at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center alongside Heidi Hammel pointed the James Webb Space Telescope's Near-Infrared Spectrograph at Enceladus from Lagrange Point 2 one million miles from Earth and published their findings in *Nature Astronomy* on May 17, 2023.

Suddenly, Webb revealed a water-vapour plume jetting from Enceladus's southern pole spanning more than six thousand miles — more than twenty times the size of the three-hundred-and-thirteen-mile moon itself, and nearly the distance from Los Angeles, California to Buenos Aires, Argentina.

Even across that expanse, water vapour gushes at about seventy-nine gallons per second — filling an Olympic-sized swimming pool in just a couple of hours, compared with more than two weeks using a garden hose on Earth — while roughly thirty percent stays within a donut-shaped torus co-located with Saturn's E-ring and seventy percent escapes to supply water across the Saturnian system, explaining why operators plunged Cassini into Saturn in 2017 after thirteen years in orbit so Enceladus would remain pristine.
