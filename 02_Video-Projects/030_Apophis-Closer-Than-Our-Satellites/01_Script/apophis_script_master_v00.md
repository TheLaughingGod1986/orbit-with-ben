# The Asteroid That Will Fly Closer Than Our Satellites

On Friday the thirteenth of April, 2029, an asteroid as tall as the Eiffel Tower will pass closer to Earth than the satellites that bring us our television.

It will miss. We know that now, for certain. So why, twenty years ago, was it the most dangerous asteroid anyone had ever found?

Its name is Apophis. In this film I will show you how it went from a threat to a gift, how close it will really come, what Earth's gravity will do to it, and how you can watch it from your own garden. So how did the scariest rock in the sky become the one everyone wants to see?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Most Dangerous Rock]

In June 2004, three astronomers at Kitt Peak in Arizona spotted a faint moving dot. Roy Tucker, David Tholen and Fabrizio Bernardi saw it for two nights, then lost it.

In December it was found again, from Australia. And as the orbit was worked out, the chance that it would hit Earth in 2029 went up, and up. By Christmas it reached about one in thirty-seven.

That put it at level four on the Torino scale, the scale astronomers use to rate impact threats. Level four means a one per cent chance or more of a collision that could devastate a whole region. No asteroid had ever scored that high before. None has since.

They gave it a name to match. Apophis, the serpent of chaos who fought the Sun god in Egyptian myth. Two of its finders also liked a villain of that name on a science fiction television show.

But a scare like this is how astronomy works. A few nights of data leave a wide cone of possible paths. Earth sat inside the cone. The question was whether more data would shrink it away from us.

[CHAPTER CARD: How We Know It Misses]

The answer came from the past. Astronomers searched old photographs and found Apophis in images taken in March 2004, months before anyone had noticed it.

Those extra points stretched the orbit across a much longer arc. Within days the 2029 impact was ruled out. Smaller chances for 2036 and 2068 lingered for years.

Then, in March 2021, the giant radar dish at Goldstone in California bounced radio waves off the asteroid as it passed. Radar is like shouting into a canyon and timing the echo. The echo tells you how far away the rock is and how fast it is moving, far more precisely than any photograph. The echoes pinned its position to within a few hundred metres.

The result was clear. Apophis will not hit Earth in 2029, or in 2036, or in 2068. Not for at least a hundred years.

So the danger is gone. What is left is something much rarer: a close look at a big asteroid, delivered to our door.

[CHAPTER CARD: Closer Than Our Satellites]

Now picture the evening of the thirteenth of April, 2029.

High above the equator, a ring of satellites circles at about thirty-six thousand kilometres. They carry our weather pictures, our television and our phone calls.

Apophis will pass inside that ring. At its closest it will be about thirty-two thousand kilometres from the ground. That is roughly a tenth of the way to the Moon.

From Britain, Europe and Africa it will look like a star, steady and white, sliding across the sky. You will not need a telescope. It will be about as bright as the faintest star in the Plough, and because it is so close, you will see it move against the stars. In a few hours it will cross a stretch of sky as wide as many outstretched hands.

An asteroid this big comes this close only about once in a thousand years. Billions of people will have the chance to see it.

[CHAPTER CARD: What Earth Does to Apophis]

So that is the story. A near miss, a good show, case closed?

Not quite. Because in this story the danger runs the other way. It is Earth that will shake Apophis.

Our planet's gravity will tug on it as it passes. Its orbit will change. Today Apophis goes round the Sun in a little less than a year. After the flyby, it will take a little more.

The pull may also change the way it spins. And some scientists think the strain could set off small landslides on its surface, shaking loose dust and boulders, like a quake on a world with almost no gravity.

Why does that matter? Asteroids are leftovers from when the planets formed. Their surfaces are coated in dust, baked by sunlight for millions of years. If the flyby shakes that coat loose, we get to see fresh rock underneath, the inside of a building block of planets.

Nobody has ever watched that happen. In 2029, we will.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Spacecraft Waiting for It]

We will not just be watching from the ground.

NASA's OSIRIS-REx spacecraft brought back pieces of another asteroid, Bennu, in 2023. Now it has a new name, OSIRIS-APEX, and a new target. It is on its way to Apophis.

It will arrive just after the flyby and stay for about eighteen months. It will map the surface, see what Earth's pull has changed, and fire its thrusters close to the ground to stir up the dust and look underneath.

Asteroids can surprise us. When OSIRIS-REx touched Bennu, the ground behaved almost like a pit of plastic balls, and the spacecraft sank in far more easily than anyone expected. Apophis may hold surprises of its own.

Europe is planning a smaller spacecraft too, to ride alongside Apophis through the flyby itself.

[CHAPTER CARD: Could We Stop One?]

Here is the scariest idea in this film. One day, a rock like Apophis will be heading for us. Could we do anything?

In 2013, a meteor only about twenty metres across exploded over Chelyabinsk in Russia. The shock wave broke windows across the city, and around fifteen hundred people were hurt, mostly by flying glass. Apophis is more than fifteen times wider.

But in 2022 we tried something new. NASA flew a spacecraft called DART straight into a small asteroid moon named Dimorphos, on purpose.

It worked. The impact shortened Dimorphos's orbit by thirty-two minutes. For the first time, humans had changed the path of a world in space.

With enough warning, we can nudge a rock aside. And warning is what finding them early gives us. That is why astronomers keep watching the sky. And the watch is getting sharper. The Vera Rubin Observatory in Chile has begun mapping the whole southern sky, again and again. NASA's NEO Surveyor, a space telescope built only to hunt asteroids near Earth, is due to launch in 2027.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Night We All Look Up]

Twenty years ago, Apophis was the asteroid everyone feared.

In 2029 it will be the asteroid everyone sees. A rock older than the dinosaurs, sliding past the satellites, while families stand outside and point.

What if the most dangerous thing about an asteroid is not hitting us, but never seeing it coming?

We see this one. We saw it twenty-five years early. And it will come back, again and again, and each time we will know exactly where it is. And on that Friday night in April, all you have to do is look up.
