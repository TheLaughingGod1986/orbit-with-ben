# 030 Apophis — script v01

On Friday the thirteenth of April 2029, an asteroid as tall as the Eiffel Tower will pass inside the ring of our satellites. Will it hit us?

No. It will miss. We know that now, for certain.

So why, twenty years ago, was it the biggest danger anyone had ever found in the sky? And why are astronomers now counting the days until it arrives?

Its name is Apophis. So how did the scariest rock in the sky become the one everyone wants to see?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Most Dangerous Rock]

June 2004. Three astronomers at Kitt Peak in Arizona spot a faint moving dot. Roy Tucker, David Tholen and Fabrizio Bernardi follow it for two nights. Then they lose it. For six months, nobody sees it again.

In December it is found again, from Australia. And as the orbit is worked out, the chance that it will hit Earth in 2029 goes up, and up. By Christmas it reaches about one in thirty-seven. That is roughly the chance of rolling a double six with a pair of dice.

That put it at level four on the Torino scale, the scale astronomers use to rate impact threats. Level four means a one per cent chance or more of a collision that could devastate a whole region. No asteroid had ever scored that high before. None has since.

They gave it a name to match. Apophis, the serpent of chaos who fought the Sun god in Egyptian myth.

But a scare like this is how astronomy works. A few nights of data leave a wide cone of possible paths, and Earth sat inside the cone. So the question was simple. Would more data shrink that cone away from us, or onto us?

[CHAPTER CARD: How We Know It Misses]

The answer came from the past. Astronomers searched old photographs and found Apophis in images taken in March 2004, months before anyone had noticed it.

Those extra points stretched the orbit across a much longer arc. Within days the 2029 impact was ruled out. Smaller chances for 2036 and 2068 lingered for years.

Then, in March 2021, the giant radar dish at Goldstone in California bounced radio waves off the asteroid as it passed. Radar is like shouting into a canyon and timing the echo. The echo tells you how far away the rock is and how fast it is moving, far more precisely than any photograph.

The result was clear. Apophis will not hit Earth in 2029, or in 2036, or in 2068. Not for at least a hundred years.

So the danger is gone. What is left is something much rarer: a close look at a big asteroid, delivered to our door. But how close will it really come?

[CHAPTER CARD: Closer Than Our Satellites]

Now picture the evening of the thirteenth of April, 2029.

High above the equator, a ring of satellites circles at about thirty-six thousand kilometres. They carry our weather pictures, our television and our phone calls.

Apophis will pass inside that ring. At its closest it will be about thirty-two thousand kilometres from the ground. That is roughly a tenth of the way to the Moon.

From Britain, Europe and Africa you will see it as a star, steady and white, sliding across the sky. You will not need a telescope. It will be about as bright as the faintest star in the Plough, and because it is so close, you will see it move against the stars. It will be racing past at about seven kilometres a second, fast enough to cross Britain in about two minutes.

Why is that so special? An asteroid this big comes this close only about once in a thousand years. Billions of people will have the chance to see it. It will be the closest pass of an asteroid this big that anyone has ever known about.

[CHAPTER CARD: What Earth Does to Apophis]

So that is the story. A near miss, a good show. Case closed?

Not quite. Because in this story the danger runs the other way. It is Earth that will shake Apophis.

As it passes, our planet's gravity will pull it, twist it, stretch it and squeeze it. Its orbit will change. Today Apophis goes round the Sun in a little less than a year. After the flyby, it will take a little more.

The twist will probably change the way it spins. And the stretching may set off small landslides on its steepest slopes, like a quake on a world with almost no gravity.

Why does that matter? Asteroids are leftovers from when the planets formed. Their surfaces are coated in dust, baked by sunlight for millions of years. If the flyby shakes their dusty coat loose, we get to see fresh rock underneath, the inside of a building block of planets. Nobody has ever watched that happen. In 2029, we will.

And this time we will not only watch from the ground. NASA's OSIRIS-REx spacecraft brought back pieces of another asteroid, Bennu, in 2023. Renamed OSIRIS-APEX, it is now on its way to Apophis. It will arrive just after the flyby and stay for about eighteen months, mapping what Earth's pull has changed. Near the end, it will fire its thrusters close to the ground, to blow away the dust and look underneath.

Asteroids can surprise us. When OSIRIS-REx touched Bennu, the ground behaved almost like a pit of plastic balls, and the spacecraft sank in far more easily than anyone expected. What is hiding under the surface of Apophis is still a mystery.

But Apophis leaves us with a harder question. What if the next one does not miss?

[CHAPTER CARD: Could We Stop One?]

In 2013, a meteor only about twenty metres across exploded over Chelyabinsk in Russia. The shock wave broke windows across the city, and around fifteen hundred people were hurt, mostly by flying glass. Apophis is more than fifteen times wider. And Chelyabinsk came with no warning at all. It flew in out of the glare of the Sun, and nobody saw it coming.

So could we do anything? In 2022, we tried. NASA flew a spacecraft called DART straight into a small asteroid moon named Dimorphos, on purpose.

It worked. The impact shortened Dimorphos's orbit by thirty-two minutes. For the first time, humans had changed the path of a world in space.

With enough warning, we can nudge a rock aside. And warning is what finding them early gives us. That is why the watch is getting sharper. The Vera Rubin Observatory in Chile has begun mapping the whole southern sky, again and again. And NASA's NEO Surveyor, a space telescope built only to hunt asteroids near Earth, is due to launch in 2027.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Night We All Look Up]

Twenty years ago, Apophis was the asteroid everyone feared.

In 2029 it will be the asteroid everyone sees. A rock older than the dinosaurs, sliding past the satellites, while families stand outside and point.

What if the most dangerous thing about an asteroid is not hitting us, but never seeing it coming?

We see this one. We found it twenty-five years early. And it will come back, again and again. Each time, we will know exactly where it is. So on that Friday night in April, all you have to do is look up.
