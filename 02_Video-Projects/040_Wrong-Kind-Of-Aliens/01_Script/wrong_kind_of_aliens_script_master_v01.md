[CHAPTER CARD: Two Thousand Five Hundred Metres Into the Dark]

February 1977. Four hundred miles off the South American coast aboard the research vessel Knorr, marine geologist Robert Ballard of the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution stared at photographs taken eight thousand feet — two thousand five hundred metres — below the surface along the Galápagos Rift by a towed camera platform called ANGUS.

Ballard and thirty marine geologists, geochemists, and geophysicists had found the world's first known active hydrothermal vent. But there were no biologists aboard, because nobody expected what came next: life was thriving in the abyss.

Where cold seawater seeps deeper into cracks in the seafloor, magma-heated subsurface rocks heat it up to seven hundred and fifty degrees Fahrenheit — four hundred degrees Celsius — before mineral-laden fluids percolate back up through vent openings.

With no sunlight and no plants, foot-long clams, human-sized tube worms, and eight hundred vent animal species across the global ocean survive on chemosynthesis — microbes converting carbon dioxide in the ocean into organic compounds. What if those dark vents exist inside an icy moon orbiting Saturn?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Five-Hundred-Kilometre Moon That Sprays Its Ocean Into Space]

28 August 1789. British astronomer William Herschel spotted Enceladus orbiting Saturn at two hundred and thirty-eight thousand kilometres, completing one orbit every thirty-two point nine hours.

Only five hundred kilometres — three hundred and ten miles — across, about as wide as Arizona, Enceladus has the whitest, most reflective surface in the solar system, with a surface temperature of minus two hundred and one degrees Celsius.

But in 2005, NASA's Cassini spacecraft discovered icy water particles and gas gushing at eight hundred miles per hour — four hundred metres per second — from south-polar fractures where the ice shell thins from twenty to twenty-five kilometres down to one to five kilometres, with organic materials twenty times denser than expected.

Then on 28 October 2015, during Cassini's deepest dive through the plume, its spectrometer detected nearly ninety-eight percent water vapour, one percent hydrogen from chimney-like vents, carbon dioxide, methane, ammonia, and silica nanograins generated where water and rock interact above ninety degrees Celsius. [CHAPTER CARD: The Nuclear Octocopter Bound for Selk Crater]

Because Titan's atmosphere is ninety-five percent nitrogen and five percent methane with a notable lack of oxygen — as on Earth billions of years ago — its dense, calm atmosphere and low gravity make flying an ideal way to travel.

Launching no earlier than July 2028 and arriving in late 2034, NASA's Dragonfly mission will send an eight-hundred-and-seventy-five-kilogram, car-sized nuclear octocopter carried by eight sets of one-point-three-five-metre rotors across Titan during a three-point-three-year mission.

Making one flight every one to two Titan days — where each Titan day lasts sixteen Earth days — Dragonfly will operate during eight-Earth-day daytimes, then recharge during the Titan night.

Flying across organic dunes, Dragonfly will explore the eighty-kilometre-wide Selk Crater — where liquid water and organics mixed in a primordial soup — while fifty-five to eighty kilometres deeper below the ice, Titan hides an underground ocean of liquid water mixed with salts and ammonia. [CHAPTER CARD: Bringing Silicon to Life]

That brings us to a major paradox in our search for alien life: since carbon and silicon both form bonds to four atoms simultaneously and silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust, why do we assume biology can never weave silicon into living molecules?

No living organism on Earth is known to put silicon-carbon bonds together. But in 1993, chemical engineer Frances Arnold at the California Institute of Technology conducted the first directed evolution of enzymes — work that won the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Then in research published on 24 November 2016, Arnold's team took a heme protein called cytochrome c from an Icelandic hot-spring bacterium, Rhodothermus marinus, and mutated its DNA across three rounds of directed evolution.

Now that evolved enzyme selectively makes silicon-carbon bonds in water at room temperature fifteen times more efficiently than the best catalyst invented by chemists — proving that living cells can rapidly adapt to bring silicon to life.

Whether alien life is a chemosynthetic microbe in a dark ocean, prebiotic chemistry on Titan, or a cell using new elements, the cosmos has more ways to come alive than we imagined.
