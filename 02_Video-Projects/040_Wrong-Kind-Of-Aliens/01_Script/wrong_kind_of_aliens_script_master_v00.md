[CHAPTER CARD: Two Thousand Five Hundred Metres Into the Dark]

February 1977. Four hundred miles off the South American coast aboard the research vessel Knorr, marine geologist Robert Ballard of the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution stared at photographs taken eight thousand feet — two thousand five hundred metres — below the surface along the Galápagos Rift by a towed camera platform called ANGUS.

Ballard and thirty marine geologists, geochemists, and geophysicists had discovered the world's first active hydrothermal vent. However, there were no biologists aboard, because nobody expected what came next: life was thriving in the abyss.

Where cold seawater seeps deeper into seafloor cracks, subsurface magma heats it up to seven hundred and fifty degrees Fahrenheit — four hundred degrees Celsius — before mineral-rich fluids percolate back up through vent openings.

With no sunlight and no plants, foot-long clams, human-sized tube worms, and eight hundred vent animal species across the global ocean survive on chemosynthesis — microbes turning carbon dioxide and vent chemicals into food. What if those dark vents exist inside an icy moon orbiting Saturn?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Five-Hundred-Kilometre Moon That Sprays Its Ocean Into Space]

28 August 1789. British astronomer William Herschel spotted Enceladus orbiting Saturn at two hundred and thirty-eight thousand kilometres, completing one orbit every thirty-two point nine hours.

Only five hundred kilometres — three hundred and ten miles — across, about as wide as Arizona, Enceladus has the whitest, most reflective surface in the solar system, chilling its crust to minus two hundred and one degrees Celsius.

But in 2005, NASA's Cassini spacecraft discovered icy water particles and gas gushing at eight hundred miles per hour — four hundred metres per second — from south-polar fractures where the ice shell thins from twenty to twenty-five kilometres down to one to five kilometres, with organic materials twenty times denser than expected.

Then on 28 October 2015, during Cassini's deepest dive through the plume, its spectrometer found nearly ninety-eight percent water vapour, one percent hydrogen, carbon dioxide, methane, ammonia, and silica nanograins formed where water and rock interact above ninety degrees Celsius. [CHAPTER CARD: The Nuclear Octocopter Bound for Selk Crater]

Because Titan's atmosphere is ninety-five percent nitrogen and five percent methane with a notable lack of oxygen, those hydrocarbon seas never catch fire — and with air four times denser and one-seventh of Earth's gravity, staying aloft is about twenty-eight times easier by our own rough sum.

Launching no earlier than July 2028 and arriving in late 2034, NASA's Dragonfly mission will send a car-sized, nuclear-powered octocopter carried by eight sets of one-point-three-five-metre rotors across Titan during a three-point-three-year mission.

Making one flight every one to two Titan days — where each Titan day lasts sixteen Earth days — Dragonfly will work during eight-Earth-day daytimes, then recharge through the Titan night.

Flying across organic dunes, Dragonfly will explore Selk Crater — where liquid water once mixed with organics in a primordial soup — while fifty-five to eighty kilometres deeper below the ice, Titan hides a second ocean of liquid water mixed with salts and ammonia. [CHAPTER CARD: Bringing Silicon to Life]

That brings us to a major paradox in our search for alien life: since carbon and silicon both form bonds to four atoms simultaneously and silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust, why do we assume biology can never weave silicon into living molecules?

No living organism on Earth was known to assemble carbon-silicon bonds naturally. However, in 1993, chemical engineer Frances Arnold at the California Institute of Technology conducted the first directed evolution of enzymes — work that won the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Then in research published on 24 November 2016, Arnold's team took a heme protein called cytochrome c from an Icelandic hot-spring bacterium, Rhodothermus marinus, and mutated its DNA across three rounds of directed evolution.

Now that evolved enzyme forged carbon-silicon bonds in water at room temperature fifteen times more efficiently than the best synthetic catalyst invented by chemists — proving that living cells can rapidly adapt to bring silicon to life.

Whether alien life is a chemosynthetic microbe in a dark ocean, prebiotic chemistry on Titan, or a cell using new elements, the cosmos has more ways to come alive than we imagined.
