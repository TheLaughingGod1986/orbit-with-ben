# Why Do Mars and the Other Planets Sound So Strange?

High in orbit above the Red Planet, before NASA landed two microphones on Mars, what secret signal or sound would you hear on the Martian surface or beneath the thick orange-brown atmosphere of Saturn's moon Titan?

Equipped with two microphones on the Red Planet, NASA's Perseverance rover recorded nearly five hours of Martian wind gusts, crunching wheels, and over twenty-five thousand laser shots zapping rock seven metres away — solving a mystery of planetary acoustics: while sound on Earth travels at three hundred and forty-three metres per second, on Mars low-pitched sounds travel at two hundred and forty metres per second while higher-pitched sounds move at two hundred and fifty metres per second.

Why does Mars split sound into two speeds that falter at eight metres, what if you heard the one-minute descent recording from Titan on 14 January 2005, and how did an X-ray telescope detect a black hole humming a B-flat fifty-seven octaves below middle C?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Silent Planet That Spoke]

1 December 2018. Before NASA landed a working microphone on Mars, the InSight lander sat on Elysium Planitia as ten to fifteen mile per hour winds — sixteen to twenty-four kilometres per hour — blew across the lander.

InSight's seismometer and air pressure sensor picked up the vibrations, but the seismometer readings were nearly all bass, so engineers pitched them up two octaves and sped up the air pressure readings one hundred times to make the wind audible.

Then Perseverance arrived with two microphones — an Entry, Descent, and Landing microphone and the thirty-gram SuperCam microphone on its mast, sampling air twenty thousand times per second for three point five minutes while weather sensors sampled one to two times per second.

Across nearly five hours of audio during the first two hundred and sixteen Martian days, Perseverance heard sand grains, whistling wind, howling dust devils, and then on 30 April 2021 the Ingenuity helicopter's rotors spinning at two thousand five hundred revolutions per minute — humming an eighty-four hertz tone from eighty metres away. [CHAPTER CARD: The Planet With Two Speeds of Sound]

Here is the acoustic paradox that stunned Perseverance's team: between wind gusts, Mars was so quiet that researchers thought the microphone was broken.

To test how sound moves through the Martian atmosphere, scientists fired SuperCam's rock-vaporizing laser — zapping rock targets as small as a full stop from seven metres, or twenty feet, away across twenty-five thousand laser shots.

When the laser strikes rock, it vaporizes into hot plasma, creating a shockwave and a high-pitched pop above two kilohertz. By timing those pops alongside Ingenuity's eighty-four hertz hum, the team uncovered a secret of Mars's thin, cold carbon dioxide atmosphere.

On Earth, sound travels at three hundred and forty-three metres per second — seven hundred and sixty-seven miles per hour — and drops off after sixty-five metres, or two hundred and thirteen feet.

But on Mars, sound falters at eight metres, or twenty-six feet, and splits in two: low-pitched sounds travel at two hundred and forty metres per second, while higher-pitched sounds move at two hundred and fifty metres per second. What if a probe descended into a thick outer-planet moon instead?

[CHAPTER CARD: One Hundred and Forty-Seven Minutes Into Titan]

14 January 2005. In the outer solar system, the European Space Agency's three-hundred-and-thirty-five-kilogram Huygens probe — named after Dutch physicist Christiaan Huygens — plunged into the thick orange-brown atmosphere of Saturn's moon Titan.

During its one-hundred-and-forty-seven-minute — two-and-a-half-hour — descent through super-rotating winds and haze, a microphone on the Huygens probe recorded the sounds as it descended to Titan's surface.

Researchers then combined those sounds into a one-minute laboratory reconstruction of what a traveller aboard Huygens would have heard plunging toward Titan's soft, sandy riverbed.

After touching down on 14 January 2005 — the first probe to land in the outer solar system — Huygens transmitted from Titan's surface for another seventy-two minutes until Cassini disappeared over the horizon.

Together, Cassini and Huygens revealed a hidden ocean beneath Titan's surface, hydrocarbon dunes one hundred metres high covering ten million square kilometres, and liquid methane seas covering one point six million square kilometres. The pioneer who taught us to hear them was University of Iowa physicist Donald Gurnett. In September 1957, the year Sputnik 1 launched, seventeen-year-old Gurnett arrived from an Iowa farm and then walked into the office of physicist James Van Allen — who discovered Earth's radiation environment — after Explorer 1 launched on 31 January 1958, listing his qualifications in three words: *"Radio control electronics."*

Across sixty years and forty-one space missions, Gurnett's plasma-wave instrument on IMP-6, launched on 13 March 1971, revealed that Earth emits radio waves at one billion watts.

As principal investigator on Voyager 1 and 2 — launched on 5 September 1977 and 20 August 1977 — Gurnett obtained the first radio and plasma observations of Jupiter and the first detection of lightning on the planet. [CHAPTER CARD: The Deepest Note in the Universe]

That brings us to a widespread misconception about sound in space: we assume that beyond a planet's atmosphere, real acoustic sound waves cannot travel.

Let us test that paradox inside the Perseus galaxy cluster — the brightest cluster in X-rays, two hundred and fifty million light-years from Earth — where galaxies sit inside hot cluster gas.

In 2002, astronomer Andrew Fabian of the Institute of Astronomy in Cambridge led a fifty-three-hour observation with NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory — announced on 9 September 2003 — discovering ripples in the gas that are sound waves travelling hundreds of thousands of light-years from the central supermassive black hole.

Generating those cavities takes the combined energy of one hundred million supernovae, keeping the gas warm. With ten million years between waves, the black hole has hummed a steady B-flat note for two point five billion years — fifty-seven octaves below middle C, over a million billion times deeper than human hearing, compared with seven octaves on a piano.

From Mars's two-speed winds to a B-flat ringing across Perseus, the cosmos is alive with sound.
