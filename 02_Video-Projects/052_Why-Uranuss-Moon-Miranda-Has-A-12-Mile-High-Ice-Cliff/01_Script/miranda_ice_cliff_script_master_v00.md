Why does Uranus's smallest major moon Miranda have an ice cliff 12 miles high — a fault wall up to 12 times deeper than Earth's Grand Canyon?

Look across the horizon of Miranda in the images NASA's Voyager 2 captured on January 24, 1986, and you see the sunlit scarp of Verona Rupes: a sheer drop of fractured water ice estimated at 20 kilometers, or 12 miles, deep on a tiny world in orbit around Uranus only 293 miles across, where a rock dropped off the highest cliff takes a full 10 minutes to fall to the bottom.

Not because Miranda was shattered into rubble and glued back together, as scientists first suspected in 1986, but because orbital resonances with neighbouring moons once flexed Miranda's interior like a rubber ball, melting a subsurface ocean at least 62 miles deep beneath a thin icy crust that cracked and dropped along gigantic fault blocks.

What would you see and feel if you stepped off that rim 1.8 billion miles from the Sun? In Miranda's weak gravity, you would fall for about 12 minutes before reaching the canyon floor at 200 kilometers per hour — the speed of a racecar, slow enough that you could survive with airbag protection.

On February 16, 1948, Dutch-American astronomer Gerard Kuiper discovered Miranda on telescopic photos at the McDonald Observatory in western Texas, and named it for Prospero's daughter in William Shakespeare's play The Tempest — the last moon of Uranus found before Voyager 2 arrived 38 years later. Why would a moon only one-seventh the width of Earth's Moon hold enough heat to tear its own surface apart?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Flyby at the Edge of the Sunlight]

On January 24, 1986, NASA's Voyager 2 swept through the Uranian system 1.8 billion miles — 2.9 billion kilometers — from the Sun, where sunlight is so faint and the icy moons are so darkened by radiation that none reflect more than about one-third of the sunlight striking them.

Of the five major moons known before Voyager 2 arrived — Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, and Oberon — Miranda is both the smallest and the closest to Uranus, orbiting right across the target zone Voyager 2 needed to thread on its way to Neptune.

While Voyager 2 photographed Uranus's largest moon, Titania — a world more than 1,000 miles across — from 229,000 miles away at an 8-mile resolution, the spacecraft skimmed just 22,500 miles — 36,250 kilometers — above Miranda.

Because exposures in that dim twilight lasted long enough to blur an ordinary picture, engineers at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and the U.S. Geological Survey used new navigation techniques to steady Voyager 2's narrow-angle camera, resolving ridges and valleys on Miranda just 2,160 feet — 660 meters — across.

When those sharp frames appeared on screens at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, why did mission scientists immediately compare Miranda's southern hemisphere to Frankenstein's monster?

[CHAPTER CARD: Three Coronae on a Frankenstein Moon]

Before Voyager 2 arrived in January 1986, the textbook expectation for Miranda was simple: a moon just 293 miles across — made of roughly equal amounts of water ice and silicate rock — is only one-seventh as large across as Earth's Moon, far too small to keep the internal heat needed for major tectonic activity.

Wrong.

Instead of a quiet, battered sphere, Voyager 2 revealed a geologic mystery: two starkly different landscapes stitched together across Miranda's southern hemisphere like Frankenstein's monster, pieced together from parts that never quite merged properly.

Beside ancient, rolling plains pockmarked by impact craters from 3 to 20 miles — 5 to 30 kilometers — wide sat three giant, lightly cratered collections of ridges and valleys called coronae: Arden, Elsinore, and Inverness, with Inverness framing a bright V-shaped chevron, separated from the older terrain by sharp boundaries like mismatched patches on a moth-eaten coat.

To explain those three unique coronae on a moon 293 miles wide, scientists first proposed that a colossal collision had smashed Miranda apart and the pieces had haphazardly reassembled, or that large rocky or metallic meteorite strikes had melted the subsurface until slushy water rose and refroze. But if Miranda did not have to be blown apart, how did its giant fault cliffs actually form?

[CHAPTER CARD: Slickensides and the Tilted Orbit]

Look closely at Voyager 2's highest-resolution fault image, and you see how Miranda's surface broke apart through global stretching and faulting rather than a random pile of rubble.

Below and right of centre in frame PIA00044, a single fault scarp drops 3 miles — 5 kilometers — straight down, already higher than the walls of Earth's Grand Canyon, while beyond it on the moon's limb Verona Rupes plunges an estimated 12 miles, or 20 kilometers, into shadow.

Etched down the face of that 3-mile inner cliff are parallel grooves called slickensides — scrape marks gouged into the ice as giant fault blocks rubbed against one another while the valley floor dropped deeper and offset the surrounding ridges.

What supplied the force to slide blocks of solid water ice miles high was orbital physics: unlike Uranus's other four major moons, Miranda follows a slightly inclined orbit, and in the past it was locked into an orbital resonance with neighbouring moons, where exact integer ratios of their orbits repeatedly flexed and heated Miranda like a rubber ball.

When planetary scientists in 2024 built a computer model to reverse-engineer those exact fault scarps and coronae from the tidal stresses inside Miranda, what secret did they find hiding beneath the ice?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Sixty-Two-Mile Ocean Beneath the Ice]

In a study published on October 16, 2024 in the Planetary Science Journal and announced on October 28, 2024 by the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, researchers from the University of North Dakota, Johns Hopkins APL, and the Planetary Science Institute reverse-engineered Miranda's surface by testing computer models of its interior against the cracks and coronae mapped by Voyager 2.

The interior structure that matched Miranda's observed fault patterns required a vast liquid water ocean beneath its surface some 100 to 500 million years ago — an ocean at least 62 miles, or 100 kilometers, deep, sealed beneath an icy crust no more than 19 miles, or 30 kilometers, thick.

Because Miranda has a radius of just 146 miles — 235 kilometers — that 62-mile-deep ocean would have filled almost half of the moon's body, turning what everyone assumed was a frozen snowball into an ocean world.

However, when the moons' resonance later desynchronized and Miranda cooled, its ocean did not leave behind the global expansion cracks that would appear if all that water had frozen solid — suggesting that 76 years after Kuiper's 1948 discovery, Miranda may still hold a thin ocean beneath its ice now. Does that mean Miranda is already a proven ocean world teeming with life — or is there a catch in our 1986 data?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Unseen Half of Miranda]

You might wonder from the headlines whether Miranda is already a confirmed, geyser-spouting ocean world ready for life, especially after a 2023 Johns Hopkins APL study reanalyzed Voyager 2 particle and magnetic data and found Miranda and Ariel may be releasing material into space, and 2024 James Webb Space Telescope observations pointed to carbon-dioxide ice replenished from inside Ariel.

Here is why planetary scientists themselves say the answer remains unknown until we return: every image we have of Miranda's coronae and Verona Rupes comes from one brief flyby on January 24, 1986, when Uranus's extreme tilt pointed only the moon's southern hemisphere toward the Sun.

We have seen only 1 of Miranda's 2 hemispheres — leaving the northern half of the 293-mile moon unmapped in darkness — and because computer models and 1986 images cannot directly detect liquid water underground, proving whether an ocean survives beneath Verona Rupes requires sending a new spacecraft to orbit Uranus.

Until a new orbiter returns to the icy darkness 1.8 billion miles from the Sun, another NASA mission has already dived in the opposite direction straight into the solar atmosphere — and next, we ask why NASA's Parker Solar Probe flew inside the Sun's corona without melting.
