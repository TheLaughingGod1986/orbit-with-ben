## Title

Is the Sun Getting Brighter?

One promise. Yes/no about a familiar thing. No series suffix. 29 characters.

## Core question

The Sun looks finished. Is it actually getting brighter, and why would it brighten while it runs out of fuel?

## Experiential angle

The viewer is already in the light. The open is the Sun filling the frame, a prominence already lifting. The science is the core turning hydrogen into helium, so the star brightens as it runs down. Then a dimmer young Sun over a wet Earth, then a modestly brighter Sun about a billion years on. Orbit appears in two inquisitive beats only. The world does the science.

## Why someone would click

Same title shape as *Is the Moon Leaving Us?* The surprise is that the star gets brighter while the fuel runs down. Signed-out search on 25 Sep 2026 did not put a million-view film on this exact question. See the topic pick.

## First 30 seconds

- **By 5 seconds:** “The Sun is getting brighter. Why does it brighten while it runs down?”
- **By 15 seconds:** the light on your face, a prominence already lifting, and the danger named as a slow climb that is already true.
- **By 30 seconds:** not the spots, not a human century of warming. The ride is why the light rises, what the sky was like under a fainter Sun, and what happens as it keeps climbing.

## Story arc

Hook → the climb you cannot see → brighter while it runs down → when the light was less → ten percent more → the Sun you already have → a bigger question, then a handoff. No goodbye.

## Chapter teach-points

1. The Climb You Cannot See — 1% per 110 million years, not the 0.1% sunspot cycle, not recent warming.
2. Brighter While It Runs Down — hydrogen to helium, the core heats, the star brightens.
3. When the Light Was Less — a fainter young Sun, and liquid water anyway.
4. Ten Percent More — about a billion years, about 10% brighter, a moist greenhouse. Not the red giant.
5. The Sun You Already Have — you are in the middle of the climb.

## Visual identity

One Sun. White-gold disc, a prominence in motion, then a dimmer young disc, then a slightly larger brighter disc. Distinct from the Moon film’s night sky and from any death-of-the-Sun picture. No red giant.
