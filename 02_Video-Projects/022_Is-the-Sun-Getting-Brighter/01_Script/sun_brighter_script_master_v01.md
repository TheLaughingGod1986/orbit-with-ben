# Is the Sun Getting Brighter?

The Sun is getting brighter. Why does it brighten while it runs down?

You are looking at the light on your face, and a prominence is already lifting off the limb. The danger is not that ribbon. The danger is a slow climb that is already true.

It is not the spots. It is not the warming of a human century. In this film I will show you why the light rises, what the sky was like under a fainter Sun, and what happens as it keeps climbing.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Climb You Cannot See]

The motion is real, and it is the wrong clock.

A prominence is plasma held in the Sun's magnetic field. It can hang, fall back, or break away. In a day it is over. Your eyes are built for that. They are not built for the change in the title.

So is the Sun getting brighter? Yes. Not by a ribbon you could watch from the garden. By a slow rise in the light of the whole star. About one percent brighter in every hundred and ten million years, as it uses its hydrogen. Schröder and Connon Smith published that pace in 2008, in a model of the Sun's long life.

What if the spots were the whole story? They are not. Across a cycle of about eleven years, the total light wobbles by about a tenth of one percent. Spots darken a patch. Brighter faculae make up the rest, and a little more. The wobble rises, falls, and comes back. It is not a climb.

The warming measured on Earth is a different ledger, written in the air over decades. This film does not say the Sun has brightened enough, in your lifetime, to be that warming. Across a human life the one-percent climb is far smaller than the sunspot wobble. You will not see it, and you will not feel it.

Now the strange question. Why would a star brighten while its fuel is running down?

[VISUAL MUST: The ribbon breaks away or falls back. The disc remains, still restless at the limb. In the edit, a slow upward line sits beside a small eleven-year wobble. The Sun stays the subject. No Orbit. No baked-in captions.]
[TEACH: Secular brightening is about 1% every 110 million years (Schröder & Connon Smith, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, 2008). The sunspot cycle shifts total brightness by about 0.1% and then reverses. Those clocks are not the same, and neither clock is the warming of the last century.]

[CHAPTER CARD: Brighter While It Runs Down]

The fuel is hydrogen. The ash is helium. The change sits in the core, where the disc you see cannot show it.

Deep inside, the Sun is fusing hydrogen into helium. The net of the proton-proton chain is simple enough to say aloud. Four hydrogen nuclei become one helium nucleus. A little mass becomes energy, and that energy is the light. The radiation that leaves the surface takes eight minutes to reach you. The energy was made long before that.

Here is the turn. Helium is not a second sort of hydrogen. Each helium nucleus locks up more mass in fewer particles. As the core fills with ash, fewer particles remain to hold the star up against gravity. Pressure would fall unless something else rose.

So the core contracts a little. It heats. Hotter hydrogen fuses faster. Faster fusion releases more energy, and the star brightens. The Sun brightens because it is running down. The waste in the middle leaves the fire no choice but to burn harder.

That is the first payoff. The tank is not dimming as it empties. About one percent more light in every hundred and ten million years, for as long as this remains the star you know. The spectrum drifts only a little hotter. What changes is the amount of light, not a new colour on a walk.

What would you see across a million years? Almost nothing, then a Sun a little more severe. Why that happens is no longer a mystery, and it is not unknown. It is mass, gravity, and ash.

[TEACH: Core hydrogen fusion raises the mean mass per particle. The core contracts and heats, the fusion rate rises, and luminosity rises. The Sun gets brighter as it uses its fuel.]

But the Sun over your street is not the Sun that a young Earth knew.

[CHAPTER CARD: When the Light Was Less]

Wind the clock back. When Earth was young, this same star was fainter. Solar models put the early Sun near seventy percent of today's light, roughly thirty percent dimmer than the disc in your sky. The one-percent rule is today's pace. It is not a flat rate you can run backwards across the whole life of the star. The young Sun was the dimmer one.

Then the paradox arrives. A star that faint should have left the early Earth frozen. The rocks say otherwise. There was liquid water. There was an ocean. How does a dimmer Sun sit over a wet world?

The usual repair is the air. More greenhouse gas, carbon dioxide and early methane, held heat the weaker sunlight did not supply. The faint young Sun is a paradox with an answer, not a hole in the model. The sunlight was less. The atmosphere did more. How warm that early air was is still partly unknown.

What would you have seen from that shore? A Sun that gave less heat, a sky that may not have been this blue, and a day that was not yet yours. The light was enough for water once the air had taken its share. It was not the daylight you are standing in.

Until the two Suns sit side by side, the daylight you trust feels finished. It is the middle of a climb.

[CHAPTER CARD: Ten Percent More]

Now run today's pace forward.

One percent in a hundred and ten million years. Ten percent takes about eleven of those steps, a bit over a billion years. Schröder and Connon Smith follow the rise and place a harder limit for Earth near a billion years from now, when the Sun is on the order of ten percent brighter. More light warms the surface. Warmer air holds more water. Water vapour is itself a greenhouse gas. Why the oceans would feel it is that water in the air. Past the limit, they can begin to lose water to space. The name for that threshold is a moist greenhouse. It is not next century. It is the far side of the same climb.

Would you survive it? Not a shoreline that still needs an ocean. Life under this star has a very long while yet. The number matters because of its direction. The Sun does not sit still and then fail all at once. It leans brighter the whole way.

The disc swells only slowly through this stretch. A little larger. The thing that changes the shore is the light. The great red swelling comes later, and it belongs to another film. This one stops at the brightening. The blue can last a very long time. It cannot last for ever under a star that only turns one way.

However bright that future disc is, you are not standing under it.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Sun You Already Have]

You are standing in the middle of the climb.

The fainter Sun is behind you. The ten-percent Sun is further off than any civilisation you can picture. Overhead is the star that learned to be this bright, and it is still learning. About one percent every hundred and ten million years. A tenth of one percent up and down with the spots. A prominence when the cameras want motion. Three clocks. Only one of them answers the title.

The Sun is getting brighter because the helium in its core leaves the fire no choice. Earth has lived the dimmer half. The oceans are still here because the climb has not gone too far. That is timing, and it is also just the physics, running in ordinary daylight.

What if the real question was never whether the Sun is changing, but whether you arrived while the light is still gentle enough for a blue sky?

The bigger question sits in that blue. How long can it last, under a star that brightens while it burns?
