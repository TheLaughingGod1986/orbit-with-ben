# What Happens When Earth's Magnetic Field Flips?

Right now, the invisible magnetic shield around our planet is weakening, and the north magnetic pole is racing across the Arctic toward Siberia. One day, north and south will swap places completely. Will that leave us unprotected?

No. Life on Earth has survived hundreds of magnetic flips, and our air will not blow away into space.

So why is the shield dropping, why do satellites already have to shut down when they fly through a weak spot over the South Atlantic, and what will the night sky look like when the poles trade places?

In this film I will show you how a French physicist found lava frozen backwards, why the last brief flip forty-one thousand years ago left its mark inside ancient trees, and why the real change happens in our sky, not to the ground beneath our feet. So how do we even know Earth's compass can turn upside down?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Backwards Lava]

August 1905, central France. A physicist named Bernard Brunhes climbs down into a road cutting near the village of Pontfarein, carrying a hammer and a compass.

He is looking at a layer of ancient lava that poured over beds of clay millions of years ago, baking the mud underneath into natural red brick. When lava or brick cools down, tiny grains of iron oxide inside it lock into line with Earth's magnetic field, like microscopic compass needles freezing in place as the rock hardens.

Brunhes takes those samples back to his laboratory. He expects them to point north, just like the compass on his desk. Instead, every single grain in the lava and the baked clay points south.

At first, people wonder if the rock played a trick on itself. Perhaps some strange chemistry made the iron grains flip on their own as they cooled.

Twenty-three years later, in 1929, a Japanese geophysicist named Motonori Matuyama tests rocks all across Japan and Manchuria. He lines them up by age. Every young rock points the way our compasses point today. Every rock older than about seven hundred and eighty thousand years points the opposite way. The rocks were not broken. The whole planet had turned its magnetic field upside down.

If the poles flipped then, what generates a magnet the size of a world, and why does it ever lose its way?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Ocean of Liquid Iron]

Three thousand kilometres beneath your shoes lies the engine that runs every compass on Earth.

Down there, the outer core is not solid rock. It is a churning ocean of liquid iron and nickel, hotter than the surface of the Sun, swirling around a solid iron heart. Because Earth spins, and because heat rises from the centre, that liquid metal twists into giant spiralling currents, like storm clouds inside the planet.

Moving metal carries electric currents, and electric currents make a magnetic field. Scientists call this a geodynamo. Most of the time, Earth's rotation keeps those iron currents lined up with the spin axis, so we get one steady north pole and one steady south pole.

However, a liquid ocean is messy. Eddies and whirlpools form in the molten iron, fighting against the main flow. When enough of those backwards eddies grow strong at the same time, the main field weakens, wobbles, and eventually tips all the way over.

Looking back through the ocean floor, where new volcanic rock records every flip like a barcode on a supermarket packet, geologists found that Earth reverses its poles every few hundred thousand years on average. Over the last eighty-three million years, it has flipped one hundred and eighty-three times.

And since scientists began measuring the field directly in the 1830s, its overall strength has dropped by about nine per cent. That sounds like the start of a countdown. So does a dropping shield mean the next flip has already begun?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Dent Over South America]

If you want to see where Earth's magnetic shield is thinnest today, you do not look at the North Pole. You look high above Brazil and the South Atlantic Ocean.

Since 2013, a trio of European spacecraft called Swarm has been mapping our magnetic field from orbit in fine detail. They are watching a vast weak spot known as the South Atlantic Anomaly.

Above most of the planet, the magnetic shield holds the inner belt of trapped space radiation safely out at a thousand kilometres or more above the ground. Over the South Atlantic, because a patch of magnetic flux deep on the surface of the liquid core has already flipped backwards, the shield sags inward. Charged particles dip down to just two hundred kilometres above the ocean, lower than the orbit of the International Space Station.

For you and me on the ground, nothing feels different. You could sail a boat across the South Atlantic all afternoon and never notice a thing.

Up in low Earth orbit, it is another story. When the Hubble Space Telescope flies through that dent, up to ten times a day, it cannot take pictures; its delicate cameras have to be switched off so stray particles do not blind the sensors. Laptop computers aboard the Space Station have crashed when flying through the same zone, and in 2016 Japan's Hitomi space observatory suffered glitches as it crossed the anomaly.

Since 1970, that weak spot has grown wider and split into two lobes. A weaker global field, a racing pole, a growing dent over the Atlantic. Case closed? Are we overdue for a total flip right now?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Tree That Remembered]

Wrong. We are not overdue, and today's drop is probably just a normal breath in a restless planet.

When geologists drilled deep sediment cores and measured how strong the magnetic field was over the past hundred thousand years, they found that today's field is still stronger than average. A nine per cent drop from a high peak still leaves us with plenty of shield.

Even more surprising, the field does not always wait seven hundred and eighty thousand years to act up. Sometimes it tries to flip, drops nearly to zero, and then snaps back to where it started. Scientists call that a geomagnetic excursion.

The most famous one happened about forty-one thousand years ago, named the Laschamp event after lava flows in France where it was first spotted. How do we know what happened year by year? Because in the swamps of northern New Zealand, giant kauri trees were growing right through it, and their preserved trunks were dug out of the mud intact.

By measuring carbon-fourteen in the tree rings, researchers watched the magnetic shield collapse in slow motion. As the field dropped to about six per cent of its current strength, more cosmic rays hit the upper air, making a spike of radiocarbon that every tree ring recorded like a diary.

The collapse took a few hundred years, and the weak phase lasted around a thousand years before the field rebuilt itself. Modern humans were already living in Europe and Australia, painting caves and looking up at the night sky. So what did that sky actually look like, and why did they survive?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why Our Air Stays Put]

Here is the scariest idea people repeat about a magnetic flip. Without a strong magnetic field, wouldn't the solar wind strip away Earth's atmosphere and bake the surface, turning our world into a second Mars?

Let us test that idea with numbers.

First, a magnetic field never vanishes completely during a flip. When the main north-south magnet weakens, smaller backup poles still remain, leaving roughly ten to twenty per cent of the shield in place.

Second, and far more important, we have a backup shield that never switches off: the air itself. The blanket of nitrogen and oxygen above your head weighs as much as a layer of water ten metres deep covering the whole planet. Even if the magnetic field dropped to zero tomorrow, high-energy particles from space would slam into gas molecules high in the stratosphere and stop dead long before reaching the ground.

What about Mars? Mars lost most of its air over billions of years largely because it is small, with only about a third of Earth's surface gravity. A reversal on Earth takes only a few thousand years, far too brief for the solar wind to steal a heavy planet's atmosphere. In fact, when scientists compared millions of years of fossil records against the barcode of past reversals, they found no link between magnetic flips and mass extinctions. Life on the ground carries on.

The real trouble in a modern flip would be for our technology: power grids during solar storms, and satellites flying through wider radiation zones that engineers would need to harden with extra shielding.

So if a flip does not wipe out life or blow away the air, what would you actually see if you stood outside in the middle of one?

[CHAPTER CARD: Eight Poles in the Sky]

Computer models of Earth's liquid core show that while the field is turning over, it does not simply swing one north pole down to Antarctica like a bar magnet on a pivot.

Instead, for a few thousand years, the field breaks into a patchwork. Earth grows four, six, or even eight magnetic poles at once. You might have a north pole over Europe, a south pole in the Pacific, and another north pole near the equator.

A pocket compass would point toward whichever local pole happened to be nearest. Songbirds, sea turtles and whales, which sense magnetic lines to navigate across oceans, would have to lean on the Sun, the stars and the coastline as their grandmothers' magnetic map slowly redrew itself over generations.

And every time a solar wind gust brushed against those scattered poles, the aurora would not stay locked in the Arctic and Antarctic circles.

Curtains of green and crimson light would funnel down toward the middle of the planet, shimmering above London, Cairo, Mumbai and Sydney on ordinary clear nights. For a hundred generations of humans, the northern lights would become the lights of the whole world, until the liquid iron deep underfoot settled into its new rhythm and locked the compass south.

Our shield is not a stone wall that cracks and falls. It is a living ocean of metal three thousand kilometres down, breathing, shifting and painting the sky. Next, we leave Earth's shield behind and land on Saturn's giant moon Titan, where the air is so thick you could strap on wings and fly.
