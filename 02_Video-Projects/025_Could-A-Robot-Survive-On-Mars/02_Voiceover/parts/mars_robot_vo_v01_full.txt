A robot built to last ninety days on Mars kept going for more than fourteen years. Why did it survive so long, and what finally stopped it?

Mars does not crush machines. It does not melt them. It wears them down, slowly, in ways you already know from home: the cold that kills a phone battery, the dust on a windscreen, a tyre that finally splits.

So could a robot survive on Mars? Could Orbit? In this film I will show you what Mars does to the machines we send, how some of them lasted far longer than anyone planned, and what it really takes to stay alive there.

At first glance, Mars looks like a desert you could drive across. Red rock, rolling dunes, a hazy sky. It is a trick of the eye.

The air is so thin that the pressure at the ground is less than one percent of Earth's. The average temperature is about minus sixty degrees. At the first Viking landing site, nights fell to nearly minus ninety. And because the air is thin and Mars has no global magnetic shield, radiation reaches the ground. Curiosity measured it. In one day on Mars, the dose is roughly what you would get in three months on Earth.

A person would need a pressure suit just to stand there. But a robot does not breathe, and it does not get sick. So the thin air and the radiation are not what usually ends a robot's life on Mars.

The killers are quieter. Cold. Dust. Wear. Of the robots that landed safely and went to work, most were stopped by one of those three.

And whatever goes wrong, nobody can rush to help. A radio signal takes from about three minutes to more than twenty to cross between the planets, each way. Nobody can steer a rover live, like a toy car. Every robot on Mars has to look after itself between messages from home.

Start with the cold, because you have felt what it does.

Take your phone out on a freezing day and watch the battery drop. Electronics and batteries hate deep cold. On Mars, every night is colder than any winter night in a British garden. So a rover spends part of its power just keeping its own insides warm, like a person burning energy to shiver.

That only works while power keeps coming in. In 2009 the rover Spirit drove into soft sand at a place the team called Troy, and its wheels sank. It could not climb out. It could not tilt its solar panels towards the low winter Sun. As the days shortened, its power fell. On the twenty-second of March 2010, Spirit sent its last message.

Spirit did not break apart. It got stuck, and winter did the rest. Its mission had been planned for ninety days. It worked for more than six years.

The cold can be even more direct. In 2008 the Phoenix lander touched down near the north pole of Mars to dig for ice. When winter came, it went silent. Two years later, a spacecraft in orbit photographed it again. Its solar panels looked broken, most likely bent by the weight of frost and ice that had built up over the winter.

Spirit had a twin on the far side of Mars. It lasted much longer, and the reason was the wind.

Mars is a dusty world. Fine red dust settles on everything, including solar panels. A panel under dust is like a windscreen nobody wipes: less and less light gets through. A rover that runs on sunlight slowly starves.

Opportunity should have starved within months. Instead, again and again, Martian wind and passing dust devils swept its panels clean. The team called them cleaning events. Each one gave the rover back its power, and it kept driving. Ninety days became years. Years became more than fourteen.

Then, in 2018, a storm grew until it wrapped the whole planet. The sky over Opportunity grew so dark that almost no sunlight reached its panels. On the tenth of June 2018, Opportunity sent its last signal. Engineers on Earth sent more than a thousand commands over the next eight months, asking it to wake up. It never answered.

What finally stopped the most successful rover of its time was not cold or rock. It was the sky turning off.

Why does dust matter so much? Because on Mars there is no rain to wash anything clean. The lander InSight spent four years listening for marsquakes, and felt more than a thousand of them. But dust kept building on its panels. In December 2022, it went quiet the same way: not broken, just out of light.

Even a robot that never runs out of power wears out.

Curiosity runs on a nuclear power source, a small supply of plutonium that gives off heat day and night, dust or no dust. When the 2018 storm darkened Opportunity's sky, Curiosity kept working in another part of the planet.

But its wheels had a different problem. They are thin aluminium, made light enough to fly to Mars. In 2013, the team noticed holes. Sharp rocks were punching through, like a tyre on broken glass. Since then the drivers have planned routes around the sharpest ground, and they photograph the wheels to watch the damage grow.

Why not just build the wheels tougher? Because every kilogram has to be launched off Earth, carried for months, slowed from thousands of kilometres an hour and set down gently on another world. On Mars, strength always has to be weighed against getting there at all.

And then there is the little helicopter. Ingenuity was built for up to five test flights over thirty days, in air one percent as thick as ours. It flew seventy-two times over almost three years. In January 2024, after its last landing, images showed damage to its rotor blades. It could not fly again. It is still standing on Mars.

So, could a robot survive on Mars?

Yes. Not forever, and not alone. The robots that last are the ones with a steady supply of power, heaters to fight the night, and a team on Earth who drive carefully around every sharp rock. Sometimes they also need luck, in the shape of a gust of wind at the right moment.

That is what Mars teaches. Survival there is not one heroic moment. It is a thousand small decisions, made from another planet, every single day.

What would Orbit need? Power that does not depend on a clear sky. A way to stay warm through the night. A shell tough enough for sharp ground. And someone at home, watching.

What if the real story of every Mars robot is not how it died, but how long people kept it going?

The bigger question sits in the tracks they leave. Every robot that stops on Mars stays exactly where it stopped, and the dust slowly covers it.
