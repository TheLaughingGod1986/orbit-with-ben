# Why Did NASA Bring Back a Sample of Asteroid Bennu?

Orbiting one hundred and five million miles from the Sun where a spinning-top asteroid of carbon-rich rubble approaches within one hundred and eighty-six thousand miles of Earth every six years, as you cross near-Earth space with NASA's OSIRIS-REx spacecraft, why did NASA bring back a sample of asteroid Bennu?

When OSIRIS-REx touched down inside Nightingale crater on October 20, 2020 and returned one hundred and twenty-one point six grams of pristine asteroid dust to the Utah desert on September 24, 2023, scientists protected fragile carbon-rich grains that would otherwise burn up or suffer alteration in Earth's atmosphere.

Now why did a boulder-strewn asteroid act like a pit of plastic balls on contact, what secret did chemists uncover when Bennu's dust revealed water-soluble phosphates, eleven evaporite salts, fourteen amino acids, all five nucleobases, ribose and glucose sugars, and flexible space gum, and what happens next to our search for how life began?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Half-Kilometre Time Capsule Driven by Sunlight]

On September 11, 1999, astronomers in New Mexico with the Lincoln Laboratory Near Earth Research survey spotted asteroid 1999 RQ36 within zero point zero five astronomical units of Earth — twenty times the Moon's distance — before a nine-year-old named it Bennu in 2013.

At its equator, Bennu spans one-third of a mile — slightly wider than the height of the Empire State Building while Mercury exceeds three thousand miles across — yet its composition formed within ten million years of our solar system's birth over four point five billion years ago.

Between seven hundred million and two billion years ago, a collision in the Main Asteroid Belt between Mars and Jupiter shattered a carbon-rich parent asteroid sixty to one hundred and thirty miles across, the size of Connecticut, scattering pieces including Bennu.

As Bennu spins every four point three hours at a one-hundred-and-seventy-five-degree tilt, daytime highs of two hundred and forty degrees Fahrenheit and night lows of minus one hundred degrees Fahrenheit create the thermal push described by engineer Ivan Yarkovsky, nudging Bennu into a one-point-two-year orbit tilted five degrees that passes within one hundred and eighty-six thousand miles of Earth every six years. [CHAPTER CARD: The Boulder Field That Spits Rocks into Space]

In 2007 and 2008, NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope measured the temperature, brightness, and low thermal inertia of Bennu, convincing astronomers its surface heated and cooled rapidly like a sandy beach.

Then NASA launched OSIRIS-REx on September 8, 2016, flew past Earth at ten thousand seven hundred and ten miles on September 22, 2017, glimpsed Bennu from one point four million miles in August 2018, arrived on December 3, 2018, and entered orbit on December 31, 2018 around the smallest object ever orbited.

Instead of smooth beaches, you see a rugged world littered with boulders up to seventy-two feet across, while cameras observed Bennu ejecting rocky particles on more than three hundred occasions.

After mapping Bennu for a year, the team announced on December 12, 2019 that OSIRIS-REx would target a northern crater named Nightingale just four hundred and sixty feet wide where freshly exposed regolith waited between boulders. Now why did contact shock the mission team?

[CHAPTER CARD: Stepping into a Pit of Plastic Balls]

Wrong. Despite seventy-two-foot boulders, Bennu's surface does not push back like solid rock.

When OSIRIS-REx touched down inside Nightingale crater on October 20, 2020 and injected nitrogen gas one second after contact, its sampler arm sank almost half a metre beneath the surface before thrusters halted its descent at nine seconds, kicking up six tons of loose rock and leaving a crater twenty-six feet wide.

In twin studies on July 7, 2022 in *Science* and *Science Advances*, principal investigator Dante Lauretta and the team reported that the spacecraft would have sunk into Bennu without firing thrusters, feeling no more resistance than squeezing a French press coffee plunger — like stepping into a pit of plastic balls.

Why did Spitzer's telescope read those boulders like beach sand? Because when researchers at the University of Arizona and NASA's Johnson Space Center ran X-ray computed tomography scans on Bennu stones, published in *Nature Communications* on March 17, 2026, they found crack networks that shed heat rapidly. [CHAPTER CARD: One Hundred and Twenty-One Point Six Grams in Houston]

Two days after touchdown on October 20, 2020, images confirmed OSIRIS-REx had exceeded its two-ounce — sixty-gram — requirement, and on October 28, 2020, engineers closed the sample capsule.

After a final flyover within two point one miles of Bennu on April 7, 2021, OSIRIS-REx fired its engines for seven minutes on May 10, 2021, thrusting away at six hundred miles per hour for a two-point-five-year cruise before releasing its capsule over Utah on September 24, 2023 and flying on as OSIRIS-APEX to Apophis.

Inside cleanrooms at NASA's Johnson Space Center, curators collected two point four eight ounces — seventy point three grams — of rocks and dust from outside the sampler head by October 20, 2023, beating the sixty-gram goal before opening the head.

After removing two fasteners blocking the remaining sample on January 11, 2024, NASA announced on February 15, 2024 a total Bennu sample of four point two nine ounces — one hundred and twenty-one point six grams — more than double the mission requirement. [CHAPTER CARD: Phosphates, Eleven Brine Salts, and RNA Sugars]

Published on June 26, 2024 in *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*, early analysis showed Bennu is dominated by serpentine clay — mirroring mid-ocean ridges where mantle rock encounters water — and encrusted with water-soluble magnesium-sodium phosphate of unprecedented purity and grain size.

Then on January 29, 2025, twin papers in *Nature* and *Nature Astronomy* identified eleven evaporite minerals — from calcite, halite, and sylvite to trona — left as salty water evaporated over thousands of years, alongside ammonia, formaldehyde, fourteen of the twenty amino acids life uses to make proteins, and all five nucleobases of DNA and RNA.

On December 2, 2025 in *Nature Geoscience*, scientists at Tohoku University announced that Bennu's dust holds the five-carbon sugar ribose and, for the first time in an extraterrestrial sample, six-carbon glucose — a common energy source for life.

Because Bennu contains ribose, phosphates, and all five nucleobases but no deoxyribose, all components to form RNA are present in Bennu, supporting the "RNA world" hypothesis for how life began. Now what older material was hiding inside those grains?

[CHAPTER CARD: Space Gum, Supernova Stardust, and the Left-Handed Mystery]

In two *Nature Astronomy* papers on December 2, 2025, researchers at NASA's Ames Research Center and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory reinforced a thirty-micrometre Bennu particle with platinum, welded on a tungsten needle, and shaved it until it was a thousand times thinner than a human hair.

Under X-ray spectroscopy, they found a flexible polymer rich in nitrogen and oxygen that dimpled like soft plastic — an ancient "space gum" formed from carbamate before Bennu's parent asteroid warmed enough for liquid water, alongside presolar grains holding six times the amount of supernova dust of any other studied astromaterial.

However when astrobiologists measured the handedness of Bennu's amino acids, they uncovered a deeper mystery: while life on Earth almost exclusively produces left-handed amino acids, Bennu holds an equal mixture of left-handed and right-handed mirror images, meaning science still cannot explain why life turned left.

With at least seventy percent of the sample preserved at NASA's Johnson Space Center for future generations, this half-kilometre asteroid proved the building blocks of biology were widespread across the solar system — and next, we travel to Saturn to ask why its moon Iapetus is half black as coal and half bright as snow.
