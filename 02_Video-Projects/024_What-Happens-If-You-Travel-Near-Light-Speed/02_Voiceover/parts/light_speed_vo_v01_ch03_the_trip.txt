You and your twin say goodbye. You board a ship that reaches ninety-nine percent of light speed, fly to a star ten light-years away, turn around and come straight back.

On Earth, your twin waits a little over twenty years. On board, about three years pass for you. You step off the ship three years older. Your twin is twenty years older. The trip did not damage you. It simply spent less of your time.

From inside the ship, nothing feels slow. Your coffee cools at the normal rate. So what would you see out of the window? The stars ahead crowd together and shift towards blue. The stars behind thin out and fade to red. And something else changes: the distance. Ahead of you, the gap to that star shrinks. Ten light-years of space becomes about one and a half. That is how you cross it in so little of your own time without ever passing the speed of light.

But wait. From your seat, it was Earth that was moving. Why isn't your twin the younger one?

Because the two trips were not the same. Your twin never changed course. You fired your engines, stopped, turned around and changed direction. In that turn you switched from one frame of motion to another, and your twin never did. The paradox breaks there. The one who turns around comes home younger. The clocks on the airliners settled the same question with real hardware.
