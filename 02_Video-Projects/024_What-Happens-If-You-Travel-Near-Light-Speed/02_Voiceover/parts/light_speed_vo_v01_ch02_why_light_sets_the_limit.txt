Here is the rule underneath it all. Light always passes you at the same speed, however fast you are moving.

Imagine a clock made of light: a flash bouncing between two mirrors, one tick for every trip. Now put that clock on a fast ship and watch it go past. From outside, the flash is not going straight up and down any more. It is travelling on a long slanted path, because the mirrors moved while it was in flight. The light cannot speed up to cover the extra distance. So every tick takes longer. The moving clock runs slow, and so does everything moving with it: your heartbeat, your thoughts, your ageing.

At ten percent of light speed, the slowing is about half a percent. At ninety percent, your time runs at less than half the pace of home. At ninety-nine percent, seven to one. At ninety-nine point nine nine percent, seventy to one.

You do not need a spaceship to see it. Cosmic rays hit the top of the atmosphere and make particles called muons. A muon lives only about two millionths of a second. Even at nearly light speed, that is enough to cross only about six hundred and sixty metres. Yet muons reach the ground in great numbers. In 1963 physicists counted them on a mountain and at sea level, and the numbers only made sense if the muons' clocks were running slow. Several are passing through you as you watch this.
