# Sources — What Happens If You Travel Near the Speed of Light?

Spoken claims for the description and script draft. Not narration.

| Claim in the film | Source |
|---|---|
| As velocity approaches light speed, moving clocks run slower by the Lorentz factor $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ | Einstein, A. (1905). "Zur Elektrodynamik bewegter Körper" [*On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies*]. *Annalen der Physik*, 17(10), 891–921. §4: "Physical Meaning of the Equations Obtained in Respect to Moving Rigid Bodies and Moving Clocks." English text: [Wikisource / Dover Translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Electrodynamics_of_Moving_Bodies). Taylor, E. F. & Wheeler, J. A. (1992). *Spacetime Physics: Introduction to Special Relativity* (2nd ed.), W. H. Freeman, Ch. 3, pp. 60–64. |
| At 90% the speed of light, time passes at less than half pace; at 99%, one traveler year is seven Earth years | Relativistic proper time formula $\Delta \tau = \Delta t / \gamma$. At $v = 0.90c$, $\gamma \approx 2.294$ (traveler clock runs at $\approx 43.6\%$ Earth rate). At $v = 0.990c$, $\gamma \approx 7.089$ (traveler clock runs at $\approx 14.1\%$ Earth rate, or $1 \text{ year shipboard} \approx 7.09 \text{ Earth years}$). Taylor & Wheeler (1992), *Spacetime Physics*, Ch. 3. |
| Muons created 15 km high reach the surface only because relativistic motion lengthens their lifespan | Frisch, D. H. & Smith, J. H. (1963). "Measurement of the Relativistic Time Dilation Using $\mu$-Mesons." *American Journal of Physics*, 31(5), 342–355. DOI [10.1119/1.1969508](https://doi.org/10.1119/1.1969508). Mount Washington experiment ($1,910\text{ m}$ elevation to sea level). Proper muon mean lifetime $\tau_0 \approx 2.2 \ \mu\text{s}$ allows non-relativistic travel of only $\approx 660\text{ m}$. At $v \approx 0.994c$ ($\gamma \approx 8.8$), observed sea-level flux ($412 \pm 9\text{ hr}^{-1}$) matched relativistic prediction; classical prediction was $<30\text{ hr}^{-1}$. Precursor: Rossi, B. & Hall, D. B. (1941). *Physical Review*, 59(3), 223–228. DOI [10.1103/PhysRev.59.223](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.59.223). |
| Several muons pass through your body every second (sea-level flux about 1 per cm² per minute) | Particle Data Group: Workman, R. L. et al. (2022). "Review of Particle Physics." *PTEP* 2022, 083C01. DOI [10.1093/ptep/ptac097](https://doi.org/10.1093/ptep/ptac097). Cosmic Rays chapter ([PDF](https://pdg.lbl.gov/2022/reviews/rpp2022-rev-cosmic-rays.pdf)): "The integral intensity of vertical muons above 1 GeV/$c$ at sea level is $\approx 70\,\mathrm{m}^{-2}\mathrm{s}^{-1}\mathrm{sr}^{-1}$… Experimentalists are familiar with this number in the form $I \approx 1\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ for horizontal detectors." |
| Commercial airliners carried cesium atomic clocks around the world in 1971, confirming time dilation | Hafele, J. C. & Keating, R. E. (1972). "Around-the-World Atomic Clocks: Predicted Relativistic Time Gains" and "Observed Relativistic Time Gains." *Science*, 177(4044), 166–170. DOI predicted [10.1126/science.177.4044.166](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.177.4044.166); observed [10.1126/science.177.4044.168](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.177.4044.168). Quote (observed paper): "These results provide an unambiguous empirical resolution of the famous clock 'paradox' with macroscopic clocks." **Eastward:** predicted net $-40 \pm 23\,\mathrm{ns}$ (kinematic $-184 \pm 18$, gravitational $+144 \pm 14$); observed $-59 \pm 10\,\mathrm{ns}$. **Westward:** predicted net $+275 \pm 21\,\mathrm{ns}$; observed $+273 \pm 7\,\mathrm{ns}$. Film says only that clocks were "off by billionths of a second, by the amounts the theory predicted" — do not equate predicted with observed eastward. Summary table: [HyperPhysics](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Relativ/airtim.html). |
| GPS satellite clocks tick faster by ~38 microseconds a day, requiring relativistic corrections | Ashby, Neil (2003). "Relativity in the Global Positioning System." *Living Reviews in Relativity*, 6(1), 1. DOI [10.12942/lrr-2003-1](https://doi.org/10.12942/lrr-2003-1). Special relativity (orbital speed $3.87\text{ km/s}$) slows clocks by $\approx 7.2 \ \mu\text{s/day}$; general relativity (weaker gravitational field) speeds clocks by $\approx 45.9 \ \mu\text{s/day}$. Net drift is $+38.7 \ \mu\text{s/day}$. Uncorrected, positional errors would accumulate at roughly $11.6\text{ km/day}$ ($c \times 38.7 \ \mu\text{s}$). Ashby, N. (2002). "Relativity and the Global Positioning System." *Physics Today*, 55(5), 41–47. DOI [10.1063/1.1485583](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.1485583). |
| The twin paradox is resolved because the traveling twin breaks symmetry by accelerating to turn around | Taylor, E. F. & Wheeler, J. A. (1992). *Spacetime Physics* (2nd ed.), Ch. 4 "Trip to Canopus," §4.9 Relativity of Simultaneity. LibreTexts mirror: [4.9 Relativity of Simultaneity](https://phys.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Relativity/Spacetime_Physics_(Taylor_and_Wheeler)/04%3A_Trip_to_Canopus/4.09%3A_Relativity_of_Simultaneity). Verbatim: "This 'jump' appears on no single clock but is the result of the traveler changing frames at Canopus… And notice that the traveler is unique in the experience of changing frames; only the traveler suffers the terrible jolt of reversing direction of motion. In contrast, the Earth observer stays relaxed and comfortable in the same frame during the astronaut's entire trip. Therefore there is no symmetry between rocket traveler and Earth dweller, so no genuine contradiction in their differing time lapses, and the story of the twins is not a paradox." *(Earlier short paraphrase "The traveler changes reference frames; the stay-at-home does not…" was not verbatim — replaced.)* Einstein, A. (1918). "Dialog über Einwände gegen die Relativitätstheorie." *Die Naturwissenschaften*, 6(48), 697–702. DOI [10.1007/BF01494950](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01494950). |
| Accelerating a 1,000-kilogram probe to 0.9c requires over $1.16 \times 10^{20}$ joules (~28 gigatons of TNT) | Relativistic kinetic energy $E_k = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$. For $v = 0.90c$, $\gamma \approx 2.29415$, giving $(\gamma - 1) \approx 1.29415$. For $m = 1,000\text{ kg}$: $E_k \approx 1.29415 \times 1000 \times (2.99792 \times 10^8)^2 \approx 1.163 \times 10^{20}\text{ J}$ ($\approx 27.8\text{ gigatons TNT}$). Millis, M. G. (2004). "Prospects for Breakthrough Propulsion from Physics." NASA/TM-2004-213082. [NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20040171114). Energy Institute (2024), *Statistical Review of World Energy*: annual world primary energy use is $\sim 6.2 \times 10^{20}\text{ J}$. |
| Pushing from 0.9c to 0.99c quadruples the kinetic energy to over $5.47 \times 10^{20}$ joules | For $v = 0.990c$, $\gamma \approx 7.0888$, giving $(\gamma - 1) \approx 6.0888$. For $m = 1,000\text{ kg}$: $E_k \approx 6.0888 \times 1000 \times c^2 \approx 5.472 \times 10^{20}\text{ J}$ ($\approx 130.8\text{ gigatons TNT}$), over 4.7 times the energy needed for $0.9c$ and roughly 88% of humanity's total annual energy production for a single 1-tonne payload. Forward, R. L. (1984). "Roundtrip Interstellar Travel Using Laser-Pushed Lightsails." *Journal of Spacecraft and Rockets*, 21(2), 187–195. DOI [10.2514/3.25633](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.25633). |
| At 0.99c, a microgram of dust carries about $5 \times 10^{8}$ J (about 130 kg of TNT), "the energy of a bomb" | Exact KE: $E_k = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$ with $\gamma \approx 7.089$, $m = 10^{-9}\text{ kg}$ → $E_k \approx 5.47 \times 10^{8}\text{ J} \approx 131\text{ kg TNT}$ ($4.184 \times 10^{6}\text{ J/kg}$). Dust-impact physics (Starshot-scale, not this exact TNT line): Hoang, T. et al. (2017). "The Interaction of Relativistic Spacecraft with the Interstellar Medium." *ApJ*, 837, 5. DOI [10.3847/1538-4357/aa5da6](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/aa5da6) / [arXiv:1608.05284](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.05284). **Note:** Hoang et al. model cratering/erosion at ~0.2$c$; the "130 kg TNT / bomb" figure is the KE calculation above, not a Hoang quote. |
| The fastest thing we have built (Parker Solar Probe) peaks near 192 km/s, about 0.064% of $c$ | NASA / APL: perihelion 24 Dec 2024 peak speed $\approx 192\text{ km/s}$ ($\approx 430{,}000\text{ mph}$). $192 / 299{,}792.458 \approx 0.000640 = 0.064\%$ of $c$. https://science.nasa.gov/mission/parker-solar-probe/ · https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/heliophysics/nasas-parker-solar-probe-makes-history-with-closest-pass-to-sun/ |
| The stars ahead crowd together and shift blue; the stars behind thin and redden | Relativistic aberration + Doppler. McKinley, J. M. & Doherty, P. (1979). "In search of the 'starbow': The appearance of the starfield from a relativistic spaceship." *Am. J. Phys.*, 47(4), 309–316. DOI [10.1119/1.11826](https://doi.org/10.1119/1.11826). Taylor & Wheeler (1992), *Spacetime Physics*, headlight / aberration exercises. |
| Macroscopic relativistic human flight remains beyond foreseeable technology (**open engineering limit**) | Lubin, P. (2016). "A Roadmap to Interstellar Flight." *Journal of the British Interplanetary Society*, 69, 40–72. arXiv [1604.01356](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.01356). Breakthrough Starshot targets $\sim 0.2c$ only for gram-scale beamed-light sails. For human-scale craft at $\ge 0.9c$, propellant mass ratios (relativistic rocket equation), gigawatt-to-terawatt beamed arrays, and relativistic interstellar dust shielding are fundamentally unresolved (**speculative / unbuilt engineering**). |
| Time dilation makes the distant universe reachable within a single human lifetime (**wonder framing**); 1$g$ with a midpoint flip crosses the Milky Way in "a little over twenty years" of crew time | Relativistic rocket with continuous $1\text{-g}$ proper acceleration and midpoint flip: $\tau = 2(c/g)\operatorname{arcosh}(1 + gd/2c^2)$. For $d = 100{,}000\text{ ly}$ and $g = 9.80665\text{ m/s}^2$: $\tau \approx 22.4\text{ years}$ shipboard (computed $22.37\text{ yr}$), while $\sim 100{,}000$ years elapse on Earth. Film wording: "a little over twenty years." Replaces earlier loose ~24 yr figure. Classic derivation: [The Relativistic Rocket (UCR/Baez FAQ)](https://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/physics/Relativity/SR/Rocket/rocket.html). Taylor & Wheeler (1992), *Spacetime Physics*, Ch. 4. |
