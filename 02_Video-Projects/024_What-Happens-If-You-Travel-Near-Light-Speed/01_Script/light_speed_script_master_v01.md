# What Happens If You Travel Near the Speed of Light?

You leave Earth at ninety-nine percent of the speed of light. Why does home come back older than you?

You are gone for one year by your own watch. You land, and seven years have passed at home. Nobody cheated. Your watch is fine. Time itself ran at a different pace for you. The danger was never the speed. It was your own clock.

That is not science fiction. Your phone already depends on it every day. In this film I will show you why a moving clock runs slow, what a trip near light speed would do to you and the people you leave behind, and why you can never quite reach the limit.

[CHAPTER CARD: Your Clock Is Already Slow]

Start with the satellites over your head.

Every GPS satellite carries an atomic clock, and every one of them drifts. Because they move fast, their clocks run slow by about seven millionths of a second each day. Because they sit higher up, in weaker gravity, they run fast by about forty-six. The total is about thirty-eight millionths of a second a day, gained.

That sounds like nothing. But your position comes from timing light signals. Leave that drift alone, and the map in your pocket would wander by around ten kilometres every day. The system is built to correct it. Relativity is not a theory sitting on a shelf. It is in your phone.

In 1971 two physicists bought airline seats, and flew atomic clocks right around the world, once heading east and once heading west. When the clocks came home, they no longer agreed with the clocks that stayed behind. They were off by billionths of a second, by the amounts the theory predicted.

So the question is not whether moving clocks run slow. They do. The question is why.

[CHAPTER CARD: Why Light Sets the Limit]

Here is the rule underneath it all. Light always passes you at the same speed, however fast you are moving.

Imagine a clock made of light: a flash bouncing between two mirrors, one tick for every trip. Now put that clock on a fast ship and watch it go past. From outside, the flash is not going straight up and down any more. It is travelling on a long slanted path, because the mirrors moved while it was in flight. The light cannot speed up to cover the extra distance. So every tick takes longer. The moving clock runs slow, and so does everything moving with it: your heartbeat, your thoughts, your ageing.

At ten percent of light speed, the slowing is about half a percent. At ninety percent, your time runs at less than half the pace of home. At ninety-nine percent, seven to one. At ninety-nine point nine nine percent, seventy to one.

You do not need a spaceship to see it. Cosmic rays hit the top of the atmosphere and make particles called muons. A muon lives only about two millionths of a second. Even at nearly light speed, that is enough to cross only about six hundred and sixty metres. Yet muons reach the ground in great numbers. In 1963 physicists counted them on a mountain and at sea level, and the numbers only made sense if the muons' clocks were running slow. Several are passing through you as you watch this.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Trip]

You and your twin say goodbye. You board a ship that reaches ninety-nine percent of light speed, fly to a star ten light-years away, turn around and come straight back.

On Earth, your twin waits a little over twenty years. On board, about three years pass for you. You step off the ship three years older. Your twin is twenty years older. The trip did not damage you. It simply spent less of your time.

From inside the ship, nothing feels slow. Your coffee cools at the normal rate. So what would you see out of the window? The stars ahead crowd together and shift towards blue. The stars behind thin out and fade to red. And something else changes: the distance. Ahead of you, the gap to that star shrinks. Ten light-years of space becomes about one and a half. That is how you cross it in so little of your own time without ever passing the speed of light.

But wait. From your seat, it was Earth that was moving. Why isn't your twin the younger one?

Because the two trips were not the same. Your twin never changed course. You fired your engines, stopped, turned around and changed direction. In that turn you switched from one frame of motion to another, and your twin never did. The paradox breaks there. The one who turns around comes home younger. The clocks on the airliners settled the same question with real hardware.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Price of Every Nine]

So why haven't we done it? Because going fast costs more than it seems.

Take a single tonne, about the mass of a small car. To push it to ninety percent of light speed takes around a fifth of all the energy humanity uses in a year. To push the same tonne from ninety to ninety-nine percent takes nearly five times as much, close to a whole year of the world's energy, for one small car.

Each extra nine costs more than the last. Closer to the limit, the energy needed grows without end. That is why nothing with mass ever reaches light speed. It is not a wall you hit. It is a hill that keeps getting steeper.

And at those speeds, space itself turns hard. A speck of interstellar dust would hit your ship with the energy of a bomb.

For scale, the fastest thing we have ever built is the probe that skims past the Sun. It reaches about six hundredths of one percent of light speed. Plans exist to push tiny chips, lighter than a paperclip, to a fifth of light speed with lasers. Nothing human-sized is close.

[CHAPTER CARD: A One-Way Ticket to the Future]

Here is the strange gift inside all this.

A ship that could keep accelerating at one Earth gravity, then turn and slow down for the second half, could cross the whole Milky Way in a little over twenty years of the crew's time. A human lifetime is enough to reach almost anywhere.

But on Earth, a hundred thousand years would pass. Everyone you knew, and everyone who remembered them, would be gone long before you arrived. You could not come back to the home you left. Only to a home that had kept going without you.

That is the familiar danger hidden in the title. It is not the speed. It is your own clock, drifting away from everyone else's.

You already live inside this. Your phone depends on it every day. A muon carries it through your body as you sit here. The light from the screen in front of you is travelling at the one speed every clock agrees on.

What if the speed limit of the universe is not a wall at all, but a trade: the faster you go, the more of everyone else's future you spend?

The bigger question is where you would choose to land, if you knew home would not be waiting.
