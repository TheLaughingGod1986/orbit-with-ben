# Every Planet Explained in 10 Minutes (Mercury to Neptune)

On Mercury, the closest planet in orbit around the Sun, why does water ice survive four-hundred-and-thirty-degree heat? At fifty-eight million kilometres out, sunlight shines seven times brighter than on Earth, yet with almost no air you would feel night plunge to minus one hundred and eighty degrees Celsius.

In 1965, Italian mathematician Giuseppe Colombo solved its spin mystery: Mercury turns every fifty-nine Earth days across two eighty-eight-day orbits, so a solar day lasts one hundred and seventy-six Earth days — over two Mercury years.

At a radius of two thousand four hundred and forty kilometres — a blueberry beside an Earth nickel — Mercury is the second densest planet because an iron core fills eighty-five per cent of its radius. Tilted two degrees, its polar craters stay dark, and from March 2011 to April 2015 MESSENGER verified water ice inside one-hundred-and-twelve-kilometre Prokofiev crater. Why is Venus hotter?

[CHAPTER CARD: Venus — The Backwards Furnace]

At one hundred and eight million kilometres out orbits Venus, ninety-five per cent as wide as Earth. What would you see dropping beneath its sulfuric-acid clouds?

You reach the hottest surface in our Solar System, baked to four hundred and sixty-four degrees Celsius under a ninety-six point five per cent carbon dioxide atmosphere. Surface pressure crushes at ninety-two bars — like nine hundred and thirty metres underwater on Earth.

However, Venus spins backwards every two hundred and forty-three Earth days while orbiting in two hundred and twenty-five days, so the Sun rises in the west. In 1978, Pioneer Venus found deuterium at one hundred times Earth's ratio, the scar of a lost ocean. Then on 1 March 1982, Venera 13 survived one hundred and twenty-seven minutes — four times its thirty-two-minute design — before Parker Solar Probe saw the night surface glow red-hot in February 2021. [CHAPTER CARD: Earth — The Shielded Ocean World]

At one hundred and fifty million kilometres — one astronomical unit — orbits Earth, the largest rocky planet at a radius of six thousand three hundred and seventy-eight kilometres. What secret shields our oceans?

Tilted twenty-three point four degrees, Earth spins every twenty-four hours at one thousand six hundred and seventy kilometres per hour — four times Earth's strongest wind gust — bulging the equator forty-three kilometres wider than pole to pole.

Deeper down, a three-thousand-kilometre liquid-iron core drives a magnetic shield that flipped one hundred and eighty-three times in eighty-three million years, with the last flip seven hundred and eighty thousand years ago — twice as long as Homo sapiens have walked Earth. Since German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss measured it in the 1830s, field strength dipped nine per cent, yet our air shields us like thirteen feet of concrete. Why did Mars freeze?

[CHAPTER CARD: Mars — The Frozen Robot Desert]

At two hundred and twenty-eight million kilometres — one point five astronomical units — orbits Mars, at a radius of three thousand three hundred and ninety kilometres, half Earth's size. Why did its river valleys freeze into a robot desert?

Because smaller Mars cooled early and lost its magnetic shield, solar wind stripped its air to six point three six millibars — under one per cent of Earth's pressure — at minus fifty-nine degrees Celsius. Driving in Gale Crater since August 2012, Curiosity measured zero point six four millisieverts of radiation daily — three months of Earth's yearly dose each day.

With no moving plates, lava built Olympus Mons twenty-one point three kilometres high — over twice Mount Everest — across an Arizona-sized base. After a dust storm silenced Opportunity on 10 June 2018 at five thousand one hundred and eleven sols — fifty-six times its ninety-sol design — Ingenuity flew seventy-two times until January 2024. What looms beyond the asteroid belt?

[CHAPTER CARD: Jupiter — The Ocean of Metallic Hydrogen]

Beyond the asteroid belt at seven hundred and seventy-eight million kilometres — five point two astronomical units — looms Jupiter. Holding three hundred and eighteen Earth masses, twice all other planets combined, it spans a radius of sixty-nine thousand nine hundred and eleven kilometres — eleven times wider than Earth, a basketball beside a grape.

However, Jupiter has the shortest day of any planet, spinning in nine point nine hours on a twelve-Earth-year orbit. Since 1610, when Italian astronomer Galileo Galilei spotted its four largest moons, observers tracked its Great Red Spot — a storm wider than Earth that shrank from forty-one thousand kilometres in the late 1800s to sixteen thousand five hundred in 2014.

What if you tried landing there? Case closed? Wrong — there is no solid surface. Beneath a seventy-one-kilometre cloud deck, on 7 December 1995 NASA's Galileo probe parachuted one hundred and fifty-three kilometres down for fifty-eight minutes until twenty-two point seven atmospheres crushed it above a metallic hydrogen ocean mapped by Juno since 2016. [CHAPTER CARD: Saturn — Disappearing Rings and the Hexagon Storm]

At one point four billion kilometres — nine point five astronomical units — orbits Saturn. Spanning one hundred and twenty thousand five hundred kilometres — nine times wider than Earth, a volleyball beside a nickel — Saturn spins in ten point seven hours at six hundred and eighty-seven kilograms per cubic metre, the only planet less dense than water.

Its main rings span two hundred and eighty-two thousand kilometres, yet stand ten metres thick. In six dives before September 2017, Cassini weighed the rings at zero point four one Mimas masses, showing they formed ten million to one hundred million years ago.

Then at Saturn's north pole spins a six-sided jet stream noticed by Voyager in the early 1980s: thirty thousand kilometres wide — wider than two Earths — it races at three hundred and twenty-two kilometres per hour around a two-thousand-kilometre hurricane eye imaged by Cassini on 27 November 2012 with three-hundred-and-thirty-mile-per-hour clouds, turning from blue to gold by 2016. [CHAPTER CARD: Uranus — Knocked Sideways Into 21-Year Winters]

At two point nine billion kilometres — nineteen astronomical units — orbits Uranus. Found on 13 March 1781 by British astronomer William Herschel as the first telescope planet, Uranus spans fifty-one thousand one hundred and eighteen kilometres — four times wider than Earth, a softball beside a nickel — on an eighty-four-Earth-year orbit.

Why are its seasons so extreme? While Earth tilts twenty-three point four degrees, Uranus tips ninety-seven point seven seven degrees — four times Earth's tilt — rolling on its side and spinning backwards every seventeen hours, plunging each pole into a twenty-one-year winter.

When Voyager 2 made the only visit on 24 January 1986, it recorded the coldest planetary atmosphere at minus two hundred and twenty-four point two degrees Celsius, colder than Neptune. Worse, its magnetic axis tilts sixty degrees above an eighty-per-cent hot fluid interior reaching four thousand nine hundred and eighty-two degrees Celsius near the core. Why are Neptune's winds faster?

[CHAPTER CARD: Neptune — Supersonic Winds at the Edge]

Now, at four point five billion kilometres — thirty astronomical units, where noon is nine hundred times dimmer than on Earth — orbits Neptune. Spanning forty-nine thousand five hundred and twenty-eight kilometres — four times wider than Earth, a baseball beside a nickel — Neptune spins in sixteen hours on a one-hundred-and-sixty-five-Earth-year orbit, completing its first orbit since discovery in 2011.

Because Uranus drifted off course, French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier calculated an unseen planet's position for German astronomer Johann Gottfried Galle, who found Neptune on 23 September 1846 on his first night of searching — the first planet found by maths. On 10 October 1846, British astronomer William Lassell spotted Triton.

When Voyager 2 flew past on 25 August 1989, it found our windiest world: methane clouds whipped past two thousand kilometres per hour — three times Jupiter's winds and nine times Earth's four-hundred-kilometre-per-hour gusts — while backwards Triton fires nitrogen geysers eight kilometres high. Next, New Horizons flies past Neptune to Pluto, where a thousand-kilometre nitrogen-ice heart beats with zero craters.
