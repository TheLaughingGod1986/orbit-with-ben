# Every Planet Explained in 10 Minutes (Mercury to Neptune)

On Mercury, the closest planet in orbit around the Sun, why does water ice survive four-hundred-and-thirty-degree heat? At fifty-eight million kilometres out, sunlight shines up to seven times brighter than on Earth, yet without an atmosphere to retain heat you would feel night plunge to minus one hundred and eighty degrees Celsius.

In 1965, Italian mathematician Giuseppe Colombo solved its spin mystery: Mercury rotates on its axis three times — once every fifty-nine Earth days — for every two eighty-eight-day orbits, so one solar day lasts one hundred and seventy-six Earth days, just over two Mercury years.

With a radius of two thousand four hundred and forty kilometres — a blueberry beside an Earth nickel — Mercury is the second densest planet because a metallic core fills about eighty-five per cent of its radius. Tilted just two degrees, deep polar craters stay in permanent shadow, where MESSENGER verified water ice from March 2011 to April 2015 inside Prokofiev crater. Why is Venus hotter?

[CHAPTER CARD: Venus — The Backwards Furnace]

At one hundred and eight million kilometres out orbits Venus, nearly Earth's twin in size. What would you see dropping beneath its sulfuric-acid clouds?

You reach the hottest planetary surface in our Solar System: a runaway greenhouse under a ninety-six point five per cent carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat and bakes the rock to four hundred and sixty-four degrees Celsius. Surface air crushes at ninety-two bars — like nine hundred and thirty metres underwater on Earth.

However, Venus spins backwards every two hundred and forty-three Earth days while orbiting in two hundred and twenty-five days, so the Sun rises in the west. In 1978, Pioneer Venus found deuterium at one hundred times Earth's ratio, the scar of a lost ocean after lighter hydrogen escaped to space. On 1 March 1982, Soviet lander Venera 13 survived one hundred and twenty-seven minutes — nearly four times its thirty-two-minute design. Why did Earth keep its oceans?

[CHAPTER CARD: Earth — The Shielded Ocean World]

At one hundred and fifty million kilometres — one astronomical unit — orbits Earth, the largest rocky planet. What secret shields our oceans?

Tilted twenty-three point four degrees, Earth spins every twenty-four hours at about one thousand six hundred and seventy kilometres per hour — more than four times Earth's strongest wind gust — bulging the equator forty-three kilometres wider than pole to pole.

Some three thousand kilometres beneath our feet, below the rocky mantle, begins Earth's swirling liquid-iron outer core, generating a magnetic shield that deflects the solar wind and flipped one hundred and eighty-three times in eighty-three million years. Since German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss measured magnetic intensity in the 1830s, field strength dropped about nine per cent, yet our atmosphere shields us from radiation like thirteen feet of concrete. [CHAPTER CARD: Mars — The Frozen Robot Desert]

At two hundred and twenty-eight million kilometres — one point five astronomical units — orbits Mars, about half Earth's width. Why did its river valleys freeze into a robot desert?

Because smaller Mars cooled early and lost its global magnetic field about four billion years ago, solar wind stripped its air to six point three six millibars — under one per cent of Earth's pressure — at minus fifty-nine degrees Celsius. With no moving tectonic plates to slide crust over hotspots, lava built Olympus Mons twenty-one point three kilometres high — over twice Mount Everest — across an Arizona-sized base.

After a global dust storm silenced solar-powered Opportunity on 10 June 2018 — exceeding its ninety-day design life by sixty times — NASA's Ingenuity helicopter completed seventy-two flights in that thin air through 18 January 2024. What looms beyond the asteroid belt?

[CHAPTER CARD: Jupiter — The Ocean of Metallic Hydrogen]

Beyond the asteroid belt at seven hundred and seventy-eight million kilometres — five point two astronomical units — looms Jupiter. Holding nearly three hundred and eighteen Earth masses, more than twice all other Solar System bodies combined, it spans eleven times wider than Earth — a basketball beside a grape.

However, Jupiter has the shortest day of any planet, spinning in nine point nine hours on a twelve-Earth-year orbit. In 1610, Italian astronomer Galileo Galilei spotted its four largest moons. Meanwhile, in Jupiter's southern sky swirls the Great Red Spot — a storm wider than Earth measured since the late 1800s as it shrank from about forty-one thousand kilometres to under sixteen thousand five hundred in 2014.

What if you tried landing there? Case closed? Wrong — there is no solid surface. Beneath a seventy-one-kilometre cloud deck, on 7 December 1995 NASA's Galileo probe parachuted one hundred and fifty-three kilometres down for fifty-eight minutes until twenty-two point seven atmospheres crushed it above a deep ocean where pressure squeezes hydrogen into a conducting liquid metal. [CHAPTER CARD: Saturn — Disappearing Rings and the Hexagon Storm]

At one point four billion kilometres — nine point five astronomical units — orbits Saturn. Nine times wider than Earth — a volleyball beside a nickel — Saturn spins in ten point seven hours at six hundred and eighty-seven kilograms per cubic metre, the only planet less dense than water.

Its main rings span two hundred and eighty-two thousand kilometres, yet stand about ten metres thick. Why are they vanishing? During 2017 dives between the innermost D ring and cloud tops, Cassini measured ring material pouring into Saturn at four thousand eight hundred to forty-five thousand kilograms per second, while six gravity crossings weighed the rings at zero point four one Mimas masses — indicating they may have formed ten million to one hundred million years ago.

Then at Saturn's north pole spins a six-sided jet stream noticed by Voyager in the early 1980s: thirty thousand kilometres wide — wider than two Earths — its three-hundred-and-twenty-two-kilometre-per-hour winds circle a central hurricane eye as seasonal haze turned from blue to gold between 2012 and 2016. [CHAPTER CARD: Uranus — Knocked Sideways Into 21-Year Winters]

At two point nine billion kilometres — about nineteen astronomical units — orbits Uranus. Found on 13 March 1781 by British astronomer William Herschel as the first telescope planet, Uranus is four times wider than Earth — a softball beside a nickel — on an eighty-four-Earth-year orbit.

Why are its seasons so extreme? While Earth tilts twenty-three point four degrees, Uranus tips ninety-seven point seven seven degrees — more than four times Earth's tilt — possibly from an ancient collision, rolling sideways and spinning backwards every seventeen hours to plunge each pole into a twenty-one-year winter.

When Voyager 2 made the only visit on 24 January 1986, it recorded the coldest planetary atmosphere at minus two hundred and twenty-four point two degrees Celsius, colder than Neptune. Worse, its magnetic axis tilts nearly sixty degrees above an eighty-per-cent hot fluid interior reaching four thousand nine hundred and eighty-two degrees Celsius near the core. [CHAPTER CARD: Neptune — Supersonic Winds at the Edge]

Now, at four point five billion kilometres — thirty astronomical units, where noon is nine hundred times dimmer than on Earth — orbits Neptune. About four times wider than Earth — a baseball beside a nickel — Neptune spins in about sixteen hours on a one-hundred-and-sixty-five-Earth-year orbit, completing its first full orbit since discovery in 2011.

Because Uranus drifted off course, French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier calculated an unseen planet's position for German astronomer Johann Gottfried Galle, who found Neptune on 23 September 1846 on his first night of searching — the first planet found by maths. On 10 October 1846, British astronomer William Lassell spotted Triton.

When Voyager 2 made the only flyby on 25 August 1989, it found our windiest world: methane clouds whipped past two thousand kilometres per hour — over five times faster than Earth's strongest four-hundred-kilometre-per-hour winds — while backwards-orbiting Triton fires icy geysers over eight kilometres high. Beyond Neptune's orbit in the Kuiper Belt, New Horizons flew past Pluto in July 2015, revealing a thousand-kilometre nitrogen-ice heart with no craters.
