[CHAPTER CARD: The Breadcrumbs We Left Behind]

20 July 1969. American astronaut Neil Armstrong and his Apollo 11 crew spent twenty-two hours on the Moon, leaving their Lunar Module descent stage, instruments, and bootprints in airless dust where micrometeorites grind down surfaces over millions of years.

Then, on 2 March 1972, NASA launched Pioneer 10 to escape the solar system, reaching fifty-one thousand six hundred and eighty-two kilometres per hour, crossing the four-hundred-and-thirty-five-million-kilometre asteroid belt, passing Jupiter on 4 December 1973 at one hundred and twenty-six thousand kilometres per hour, and crossing Neptune's orbit on 13 June 1983.

However, Pioneer 10 carried only a flat aluminium plaque created with astronomer Carl Sagan and radio astronomer Frank Drake, showing a man, a woman, our solar system, and fourteen pulsars.

Pioneer 10 stayed the farthest human-made object until 17 February 1998, and sent its last signal on 23 January 2003 from twelve point two three billion kilometres away — taking eleven hours and twenty minutes to arrive — before coasting deeper toward the red star Aldebaran in two million years. [CHAPTER CARD: The Twelve-Inch Bottle in the Cosmic Ocean]

1977. Before Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 launched, NASA asked Carl Sagan of Cornell University to chair a committee — with technical director Frank Drake and creative director Ann Druyan — to build a message for extraterrestrials.

Drake proposed a twelve-inch, thirty-centimetre gold-plated copper phonograph record spinning at sixteen and two-thirds revolutions per minute — roughly half the speed of a vinyl album — so its grooves could hold far more data.

Inside those grooves, they encoded one hundred and fifteen analogue images, natural sounds of surf, wind, thunder, birds, and whales, printed messages from United States President Jimmy Carter and the United Nations, spoken greetings in fifty-five languages — from Akkadian in Sumer six thousand years ago to modern Wu — plus ninety minutes of music.

Blank discs from Pyral in France were cut in Boulder, Colorado and gold-plated in Gardena, California on 23 August 1977, hand-etched *"To the makers of music – all worlds, all times"* and sealed in an aluminium jacket with a cartridge and needle. [CHAPTER CARD: The Four-Billion-Year Atomic Clock]

Here is the paradox Frank Drake and Carl Sagan had to solve on the Golden Record's cover: how do you write a timing manual for an alien astronomer who has never heard of human seconds?

They used the most abundant atom in the cosmos: an engraving shows a hydrogen atom in its two lowest states, whose transition period is zero point seven zero billionths of a second.

Using that atomic tick, binary numbers around the stylus drawing specify that one rotation takes three point six seconds, played from the outside in, and that one side lasts about an hour.

Then binary numbers and waveforms show how to reconstruct the images: each vertical line lasts about eight milliseconds, and five hundred and twelve interlaced vertical lines form a complete picture — verified by a calibration circle.

And electroplated onto a two-centimetre circle on the cover is ultra-pure uranium-238 at zero point zero zero zero two six microcuries. Because half of uranium-238 decays in four point five one billion years — roughly the age of the Earth — any finder can measure that radioactive clock alongside the fourteen-pulsar map to learn when Voyager launched. [CHAPTER CARD: Thirty-Four Minutes Before Going Blind]

1972. Caltech physicist Edward Stone became Voyager project scientist — serving for fifty years until 2022 — guiding the twin probes along a planetary alignment that occurs once every one hundred and seventy-six years.

Launched on 5 September 1977 — sixteen days after Voyager 2 on 20 August 1977 — Voyager 1 overtook its twin on 15 December 1977, flew past Jupiter on 5 March 1979 and Saturn on 12 November 1980, and helped explore all four giant planets and forty-eight moons.

However, since 1981 Carl Sagan had urged mission managers to turn Voyager 1's cameras backward from beyond Neptune to photograph Earth.

On 14 February 1990, from six billion kilometres — three point seven billion miles — from the Sun, Voyager 1 snapped sixty frames for the first Family Portrait of our solar system, capturing Earth at 04:48 GMT as the Pale Blue Dot: about one pixel wide in a sunbeam, just thirty-four minutes before powering off its cameras forever at 05:22 GMT.

By 25 August 2012, at one hundred and twenty-two astronomical units — eighteen billion kilometres — from the Sun, solar particles vanished and cosmic rays surged, but the magnetic field barely shifted and its plasma sensor had failed in 1980. Case closed? Was Voyager 1 still trapped inside the Sun's bubble?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Solar Tsunami That Rang Interstellar Space]

Wrong. Even though Voyager 1's plasma sensor had failed in 1980, Edward Stone and his team had a backup instrument to probe the unknown medium beyond: the Plasma Wave Subsystem and its two antennas stretched like a giant V.

After crossing the termination shock in December 2004 at ninety-four astronomical units — where the solar wind slows — Voyager 1 spent nearly eight years crossing the outer solar bubble.

Then, suddenly, a solar eruption from March 2012 reached Voyager 1 in April 2013, ringing the ionized gas around the spacecraft like a bell.

From those oscillations, scientists found that the surrounding plasma was more than forty times denser than inside the outer solar bubble — proving Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause into interstellar space on 25 August 2012 at one hundred and twenty-two astronomical units, followed by Voyager 2 on 5 November 2018.

Remarkably, Voyager 1 achieved humanity's first interstellar crossing using 1970s onboard computers with about sixty-eight kilobytes of memory running at two hundred and fifty kilohertz — executing eight thousand instructions per second, versus over fourteen billion on a smartphone. [CHAPTER CARD: One Light-Day and Forty Thousand Years]

That brings us to the biggest misconception about humanity's farthest artefact: that interstellar debris will smash Voyager 1 once its power fades.

Let us test that paradox with physics. Voyager 1's radioisotope thermoelectric generators lose four watts each year, so its last instruments pass beyond NASA's Deep Space Network range around 2036.

However, interstellar space is so empty that after cruising at sixty-one thousand kilometres per hour — three point five astronomical units per year — for forty-nine years, two months, and eleven days since 5 September 1977, Voyager 1 reaches one light-day from Earth on 18 November 2026: twenty-five billion nine hundred and two million kilometres, where a radio signal takes twenty-four hours one way.

Aimed thirty-five degrees north of the ecliptic toward Ophiuchus, Voyager 1 will glide unharmed until the year 40,272 AD — forty thousand years after launch — when it passes within one point seven light-years of star AC+79 3888, also known as Gliese 445, in Ursa Minor.

Voyager 1 and its Golden Record showed that while human footprints near Earth fade, a thirty-centimetre gold-plated copper disc launched in 1977 is carrying fifty-five languages, ninety minutes of music, and a four-and-a-half-billion-year atomic clock into interstellar space one light-day from home.
