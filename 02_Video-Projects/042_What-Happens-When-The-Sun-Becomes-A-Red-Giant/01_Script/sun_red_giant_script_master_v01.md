# What Happens When the Sun Becomes a Red Giant?

High in orbit one hundred and fifty million kilometres from the photosphere of our four-point-six-billion-year-old Sun, as you follow the infrared data recorded when NASA's NEOWISE spacecraft caught an aging star swallowing a planet, why will running out of core hydrogen fuel make our star swell more than one hundred times larger — and could any planet survive?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Twenty-Seven-Million-Degree Engine]

Four point six billion years ago. Forming in the solar nebula, our Sun pulled in ninety-nine point eight percent of our solar system's mass across one point four million kilometres — eight hundred and sixty-five thousand miles — taking one point three million Earths to fill its volume and more than three hundred and thirty thousand Earths to match its mass.

Deeper inside its one hundred and thirty-eight thousand kilometre core, where hydrogen fuses into helium, temperatures top fifteen million degrees Celsius — twenty-seven million degrees Fahrenheit — at one hundred and fifty grams per cubic centimetre, eight times the density of gold or thirteen times lead.

Then radiation takes one hundred and seventy thousand years to cross the radiative zone until reaching the five thousand five hundred degree Celsius photosphere — hot enough to boil carbon like diamonds — where on 1 September 1859 British astronomer Richard Carrington observed the flare behind the strongest geomagnetic storm on record.

However, when a main-sequence star under eight solar masses runs out of core hydrogen, it collapses because fusion was the only force fighting gravity. Why does a collapsing core make the outer layers expand?

[CHAPTER CARD: Caught Swallowing a World]

Squeezing the core increases its temperature and pressure until helium fuses into carbon, while hydrogen fusion moves into the outer layers — expanding them more than one hundred times larger into a red giant that appears more orange than red.

On 3 May 2023, a study in Nature documented the first observation of an aging star swallowing a planet — ZTF SLRN-2020, discovered with ground-based observatories and NASA's NEOWISE spacecraft, launched as WISE in 2009 under principal investigator Edward Wright to image three-quarters of a billion objects before becoming NEOWISE in 2013.

But in the NEOWISE data, the star brightened in infrared light almost a year before ZTF spotted the flash, as a Jupiter-sized planet orbiting closer than Mercury pulled hot gas that cooled into dust like water vapour becoming snow.

Suddenly atmospheric drag sent the planet below the star's visible surface like a meteor burning up in Earth's atmosphere, making the star a few hundred times brighter — before our Sun absorbs Mercury, Venus, and possibly Earth. What happens after the red giant phase?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Glowing Shells of a Dying Star]

After lasting more than one hundred thousand years, a red giant becomes unstable and pulsates — periodically expanding and ejecting its atmosphere until its outer layers blow away into an expanding cloud of dust and gas called a planetary nebula.

In 1779, French astronomer Antoine Darquier de Pellepoix discovered one of those shells — Messier 57, the Ring Nebula — sitting about two thousand light-years away in Lyra at magnitude eight point eight.

However, when NASA's Hubble Space Telescope resolved Messier 57, it revealed a secret shape: deep-blue helium gas in the centre forms a football structure piercing a red doughnut of nitrogen and sulfur, while dark knots along the inner rim look like bicycle spokes around a white dot.

Then NASA's James Webb Space Telescope observed the Southern Ring Nebula, NGC 3132, revealing a dust-cloaked white dwarf that ejected at least eight layers of gas and dust over thousands of years. What is that white dot left behind in the centre?

[CHAPTER CARD: A Teaspoon Heavier Than a Pickup Truck]

After a red giant sheds its atmosphere — a stage our Sun will reach about eight to ten billion years from now — only the dense stellar core remains: a white dwarf.

Although a white dwarf is Earth-sized, it remains hundreds of thousands of times more massive, so dense that a teaspoon of its material weighs more than a pickup truck. Because atoms inside it no longer fuse, it produces no new heat, cooling over billions of years.

In 1917, astronomer Adriaan van Maanen discovered a faint white dwarf in Pisces, and that same year Walter Sydney Adams captured its spectrum — showing calcium and heavy elements, meaning the white dwarf had gobbled up rocky material.

Now astronomers call these polluted white dwarfs — dead stars where NASA's Spitzer telescope confirmed about forty dusty disks, and where Kepler in 2015 discovered a minor planet transiting as surviving exoplanets fling rocky bodies inward to be pulverized by gravity. [CHAPTER CARD: The Planet Seven Times Larger Than Its Star]

Wrong. For years, astronomers debated two theories: had a giant planet close to a white dwarf survived inside the dying star, or stayed on a safe wide orbit and migrated inward later?

In 2020, scientists using NASA's TESS and Spitzer telescopes discovered exoplanet WD 1856 b — a gas giant between four and eleven times Jupiter's mass orbiting an Earth-sized white dwarf eighty light-years away every thirty-four hours at under three million kilometres, fifty times closer than Earth orbits the Sun.

But because the planet is the size of Jupiter while its white dwarf is the size of Earth, the planet is seven times larger than its star and blocks over half the star's light each transit.

When NASA's James Webb Space Telescope published its 1 July 2026 Nature study, it detected methane and clouds — the first atmosphere seen on a planet transiting a dead star — and measured a temperature of one hundred and twenty-six degrees Celsius, two hundred and sixty degrees Fahrenheit, proving heating happened three to five point five billion years after white-dwarf formation as it migrated inward from a safe wide orbit. [CHAPTER CARD: The Ten-Billion-Year Clock]

That brings our solar system's paradox into focus: moving around the Milky Way at seven hundred and twenty thousand kilometres per hour — four hundred and fifty thousand miles per hour — our Sun takes two hundred and thirty million years per galactic trip, and sits just under halfway through its life.

In about five billion years, when core hydrogen runs out, the Sun will swell more than one hundred times larger into a red giant — absorbing Mercury, Venus, and possibly Earth, just as NEOWISE saw at ZTF SLRN-2020.

Then, after pulsating for over one hundred thousand years and ejecting glowing shells like the Ring Nebula, our Sun will become an Earth-sized white dwarf about eight to ten billion years from now — while outer gas giants survive on wide orbits around that remnant, just as Webb proved at WD 1856 b.

Now look up at the northern constellation Boötes, where the red giant Arcturus shines visible to the unaided eye.
