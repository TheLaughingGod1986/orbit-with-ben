# Why Pluto's Frozen Heart Has Zero Craters

If you stood on the bright, heart-shaped glacier of Pluto — in distant orbit around our Sun — what would you see across its horizon?

On 13 and 14 July 2015, NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew within twelve thousand five hundred kilometres of Pluto — averaging thirty-nine times farther from the Sun than Earth — and photographed that bright heart. While impact craters as large as two hundred and sixty kilometres across scar the rest of Pluto, the western half of the heart — a frozen nitrogen glacier called Sputnik Planitia, about one thousand kilometres wide — shows zero craters.

Why is a glacier larger than Texas and Oklahoma combined completely unmarked by ancient impacts at an average of minus two hundred and thirty-two degrees Celsius, what if you watched a giant moon that never sets, and what secret is rising through cracks in that frozen heart?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Girl Who Named a World]

18 February 1930. At the Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, American astronomer Clyde Tombaugh discovers a faint moving world beyond Neptune — for seventy-six years considered our solar system's ninth planet.

Less than a month later, on 14 March 1930 in Oxford, England, an eleven-year-old schoolgirl named Venetia Burney is eating breakfast with her mother and grandfather when her grandfather reads aloud that scientists have discovered a ninth planet.

Venetia suggests naming the frozen world Pluto, after the Roman god of the underworld. Her grandfather forwards the name to the Lowell Observatory, and it is selected.

How small was this unknown world Tombaugh found? With an equatorial diameter of about two thousand three hundred and seventy-seven kilometres, Pluto is only about one-fifth the width of Earth, about two-thirds the diameter of Earth's Moon, and roughly half the width of the United States.

From an average distance of five point nine billion kilometres — thirty-nine astronomical units from the Sun — sunlight takes five point five hours to reach Pluto. If you stood on Pluto's surface at noon, the Sun would be one nine-hundredth the brightness it is on Earth, or about three hundred times as bright as our full Moon — matching the light of twilight near sunset on Earth.

For decades, Pluto's true nature remained a mystery. Why did a secret hiding just nineteen thousand kilometres away turn Pluto into a double world?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Moon That Never Sets]

June 1978. At the United States Naval Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona — only about six miles from where Pluto was discovered at Lowell Observatory — astronomers trying to refine Pluto's orbit notice that photographic plates of Pluto look strangely elongated.

The bulge cycles back and forth every six point three nine days — matching Pluto's rotation period. They have discovered Charon, the largest of Pluto's five known moons, alongside four smaller moons — Nix, Hydra, Kerberos, and Styx — each less than one hundred and sixty kilometres wide.

At one thousand two hundred and fourteen kilometres across, Charon is about half the size of Pluto itself, making it the largest known satellite relative to its parent body in our solar system, often called a double dwarf planet.

Charon orbits Pluto at a distance of just nineteen thousand six hundred and forty kilometres — twelve thousand two hundred miles. For comparison, our Moon is twenty times farther away from Earth.

One day on Pluto — spun on an axis tilted fifty-seven degrees so it rotates backwards from east to west like Venus and Uranus — takes about one hundred and fifty-three hours, or six point four Earth days, the exact time Charon takes to complete one orbit.

Because both worlds are locked in mutual tidal locking, the same side of Charon always faces Pluto, and Charon neither rises nor sets — it hovers forever over the exact same spot in Pluto's sky. Why did astronomers reclassify Pluto in the very year a spacecraft finally launched to solve its mystery?

[CHAPTER CARD: Nine Years to the Kuiper Belt]

19 January 2006. NASA's four-hundred-and-seventy-eight-kilogram New Horizons spacecraft launches from Cape Canaveral atop an Atlas V rocket, firing its upper stages to reach fifty-eight thousand five hundred and thirty-six kilometres an hour — thirty-six thousand four hundred miles an hour — the highest launch velocity ever attained by a human-made object relative to Earth.

Later that same year, in 2006, after astronomers discovered similar icy worlds in the Kuiper Belt — a disc-like zone beyond Neptune extending from about thirty to fifty astronomical units from the Sun — the International Astronomical Union reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet because other objects might cross its orbital path.

Pluto's oval-shaped, two-hundred-and-forty-eight-year orbit carries it as far as forty-nine point three astronomical units from the Sun and as close as thirty astronomical units. In fact, from 1979 to 1999, Pluto was actually closer to the Sun than Neptune.

On 28 February 2007, New Horizons flew past Jupiter for a gravity assist that increased its speed by about fourteen thousand kilometres an hour, shortening its voyage to Pluto by three years.

At last, at 11:49 Universal Time on 14 July 2015 — after more than nine years in flight — New Horizons raced past Pluto, gathering six point two five gigabytes of data that took over fifteen months, until 25 October 2016, to transmit across four point five light-hours at just one to two kilobits per second.

Before the encounter, many expected a dead, crater-saturated relic from four point five billion years ago. Case closed? Was Pluto just a frozen fossil where nothing ever changes?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Glacier That Breathes]

Wrong. When the pictures came down from New Horizons, scientists were astonished by the paradox of how active Pluto's surface is today.

Dominating the planet is the vast heart-shaped nitrogen glacier named Sputnik Planitia, after Sputnik 1, Earth's first artificial satellite. At about one thousand kilometres wide — six hundred miles across — it is larger than the states of Texas and Oklahoma combined, and the largest known glacier in the solar system.

While craters up to two hundred and sixty kilometres in diameter dot other parts of Pluto, the frozen nitrogen plains of Sputnik Planitia show no craters at all.

Instead, the glacier is patterned with city-sized convection cells — blobs of solid nitrogen circulating slowly up and down, continually renewing the surface.

Bordering those plains are rugged mountains two to three kilometres high — six thousand five hundred to nine thousand eight hundred feet tall — and long troughs and valleys stretching up to six hundred kilometres.

When Pluto travels closer to the Sun, its surface ices sublimate directly from solid to gas, rising into a thin nitrogen atmosphere that Pluto's low gravity — about six per cent of Earth's — stretches high above the surface. Why can towering mountains stand beside a flowing glacier on a world between minus two hundred and twenty-six and minus two hundred and forty degrees Celsius?

[CHAPTER CARD: Where Water Is Bedrock]

That brings us to the biggest misconception about Pluto. On Earth, we think of water ice as a soft glacier that flows and melts, and rock as the solid ground beneath it.

Let us test that idea with physics at the edge of the solar system. At minus two hundred and twenty-six to minus two hundred and forty degrees Celsius, water is essentially rock-like. Pluto's two-to-three-kilometre mountains are big blocks of solid water ice, coated with frozen gases like methane, standing firm while softer nitrogen ice flows around their feet.

Even more remarkable, in August 2026, a new analysis of New Horizons imagery led by principal investigator Alan Stern found dark features along the northern edge of Sputnik Planitia — the first evidence of recently flowing liquid on Pluto's surface.

Because Pluto's cold temperatures and thin thirteen-microbar atmosphere make liquid nitrogen rain physically impossible, the study concluded that liquid nitrogen is forming by basal melting underneath the glacier and rising to the surface through cracks.

And beneath its icy mantle and rocky core, Pluto's warmer interior shows hints that it could even hold a deep internal water-ice ocean today. What happened when New Horizons looked back at Pluto — and where does Pluto have a captured twin?

[CHAPTER CARD: Beyond the Frozen Heart]

Looking back from two hundred thousand kilometres away on 14 July 2015, New Horizons captured a farewell portrait of Pluto's crescent surrounded by glowing blue atmospheric haze.

From there, the spacecraft sailed deeper into the Kuiper Belt. On 1 January 2019, four billion miles — six point four billion kilometres — from Earth, New Horizons flew within three thousand five hundred kilometres of Arrokoth, a thirty-five-kilometre-long twin-lobed world and the most distant object ever explored up close.

And on 1 October 2024, speeding away from the Sun at about three hundred million miles per year, New Horizons passed sixty times Earth's distance from the Sun — twice as far out as Pluto was during its 2015 flyby.

Pluto revealed that a world one-fifth the width of Earth can hold water-ice mountains, a giant moon that never sets, and a thousand-kilometre heart of nitrogen ice without a single crater.
