# What Would Happen If You Landed on Titan?

If you stepped out of a lander onto Saturn's largest moon, Titan, would you survive? Your body would not explode, and your blood would not boil.

In fact, while spacecraft in orbit around Saturn fly through a vacuum, Titan wraps itself in an orange nitrogen sky that presses on you about one and a half times harder than the air in your room right now. That pressure would not hurt you at all. What would kill you is the cold, and the fact that there is almost no oxygen to breathe.

So why are the pebbles under your boots made of frozen water, why does its rain fall in slow motion, and why is this mysterious world so good for flying that NASA is sending a drone there?

In this film I will take you down through the golden haze that hid Titan's surface for three and a half centuries, ride aboard the European probe that parachuted onto an alien riverbed in 2005, and show you what you would see beside its dark hydrocarbon seas. Why did it take so long to discover that a moon could hold onto a sky of its own?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Moon With a Sky]

25 March 1655. A young Dutch astronomer named Christiaan Huygens points one of the telescopes he built himself toward Saturn, hoping to study its rings, and spots a large moon circling the ringed planet about every sixteen days.

For nearly three hundred years, nobody could tell whether this distant moon had any air at all, even though it is bigger than our own Moon and wider than the planet Mercury.

Then, in 1944, the Dutch-born astronomer Gerard Kuiper splits the faint light reflected off Titan into a spectrum, and finds the fingerprint of methane gas. For the first time, astronomers know that a moon has an atmosphere of its own.

When NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft flies past Titan in November 1980, scientists hope the cameras will reveal continents, craters or oceans. Instead, every picture comes back as a featureless orange world. A haze, made when sunlight breaks apart nitrogen and methane, hides the ground completely.

Why does Titan still have methane in its sky, when sunlight keeps breaking it apart and should have used it up long ago — and what was hiding beneath the clouds?

[CHAPTER CARD: Where Water Is Rock]

Fourteen January 2005. After a seven-year journey aboard the Cassini spacecraft, a saucer-shaped European probe named Huygens plunges into Titan's upper haze and opens its parachutes.

For two hours and twenty-seven minutes, the probe drifts down through murky orange clouds. As it sinks lower, the haze thins, and a startling view appears: dark branching channels carved into bright hills, draining toward a flat, dark plain.

Huygens lands softly on what looks like a dry riverbed, scattered with rounded pebbles up to about fifteen centimetres across, just like stones in a stream bed on Earth.

Except those pebbles are not stone. Out at Titan's distance from the Sun — about nine and a half times farther out than Earth — the surface sits at minus one hundred and seventy-nine degrees Celsius. It is so cold that water ice plays the role of rock.

So why were the pebbles rounded, as if by running liquid? Because at minus one hundred and seventy-nine degrees, methane and ethane play the part water plays on Earth: forming clouds, falling as rain, carving river channels and filling lakes.

For seventy-two minutes on the surface, Huygens beams back data before its relay, Cassini, sinks below the horizon. It is still the most distant landing humanity has ever made. Yet if you stepped out beside it in a heated suit, what would you feel when the rain began to fall?

[CHAPTER CARD: Rain in Slow Motion]

Stand on Titan's surface in a suit heated against the deep freeze, and your first surprise is what would not hurt you. The air presses on you only about one and a half times as hard as Earth's.

Titan's air is about ninety-five per cent nitrogen — the same gas that makes up most of the air you are breathing right now — and about five per cent methane. It is about four times denser than Earth's air, while gravity is only about one-seventh as strong.

All you would need outside is a thick heated suit and your own oxygen, because Titan's sky has almost none, only traces of other carbon-rich gases.

What would you see if a storm rolled overhead? On Earth, the biggest raindrops are about six and a half millimetres across and fall at about nine metres a second. On Titan, methane raindrops can grow to about nine and a half millimetres — nearly a centimetre, as big as a marble — and drift down at just one point six metres a second, about as slowly as falling snowflakes.

But Titan's rain is rare. At any one spot, it may be centuries between showers. Case closed? Is Titan just a dry desert?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Seas of Kraken Mare]

Wrong. The equator may be lined with dunes, but when Cassini swept its radar across Titan's north pole, it uncovered a drowned coastline of bays and islands, filled with liquid natural gas.

Several hundred lakes and three great seas fill the northern basins. The largest, Kraken Mare, covers about four hundred thousand square kilometres — almost five times the size of Lake Superior, the largest of North America's Great Lakes.

Next door sits Ligeia Mare. When Cassini's radar shone down into it in 2013, the beam passed through the liquid and bounced off the seabed about one hundred and sixty metres down. Kraken Mare is deeper still: at least three hundred metres near its centre.

And far beneath Titan's icy crust, tens of kilometres down, Cassini's gravity measurements revealed a second, hidden ocean — of liquid water, likely mixed with salts and ammonia.

Wait — that raises a terrifying question. What if a spacecraft landed near those seas of liquid natural gas and fired its hot thrusters or struck a spark?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why a Match Won't Burn]

That is the scariest idea people repeat about landing on Titan: with hundreds of times more liquid hydrocarbons than all the known oil and natural gas reserves on Earth, wouldn't a single spark blow the lander apart?

Let us test that fear with simple chemistry.

On Earth, methane burns fiercely because our air is about twenty-one per cent oxygen. Fire needs two partners: fuel and oxygen.

Titan has lakes of fuel, and methane in its sky, but almost no oxygen. Its air is mostly nitrogen. If you struck a match or fired a rocket thruster in Titan's atmosphere, the fuel around you would not catch fire. Without oxygen, a sea of liquid methane is no more explosive than a pond of cold water.

In fact, Titan flips fire backwards. On Earth, you carry a tank of gas and burn it in the oxygen around you. On Titan, if you wanted a flame, you would have to carry a tank of oxygen on your back and spray it out into the methane sky.

So if Titan will not explode, and its thick nitrogen blanket cushions anything moving through it, how will humanity explore this strange world next?

[CHAPTER CARD: The World Where You Could Fly]

Put Titan's two strangest numbers together — air about four times denser than Earth's, and gravity only about one-seventh as strong — and flying becomes easy.

Lift comes from the air, and weight comes from gravity. Four times the air, and one-seventh of the weight: by our own rough sum, that makes it about twenty-eight times easier to stay up than on Earth. NASA's Dragonfly team puts it simply: the dense, calm air and low gravity make flying an ideal way to travel there.

So before any person goes, a robot is going to fly there first.

No earlier than July 2028, NASA plans to launch Dragonfly, a car-sized, nuclear-powered rotorcraft with eight rotors. Arriving in late 2034, it will fly for miles at a time, working through Titan's eight-Earth-day daytimes and recharging through the long nights, stopping at dunes along the way.

One of its targets is Selk Crater, where scientists think liquid water and organic material may once have mixed in a "primordial soup" — a model for how the chemistry that led to life may have begun on the young Earth.

Titan is not a dead frozen rock. It is a time capsule of early Earth locked in a deep freeze, waiting for wings to cross its sky. Next, we travel one planet farther out to Uranus, a giant world tipped onto its side, perhaps by an ancient collision, so that half of it plunges into a dark winter that lasts twenty-one years.
