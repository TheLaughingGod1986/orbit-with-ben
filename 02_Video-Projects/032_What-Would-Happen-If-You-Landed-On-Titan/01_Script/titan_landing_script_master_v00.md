# What Would Happen If You Landed on Titan?

If you stepped out of a lander onto Saturn's largest moon, Titan, without a pressurised spacesuit, would you survive? Your body would not explode, and your blood would not boil.

In fact, while spacecraft in orbit around Saturn fly through a vacuum, Titan wraps itself in an orange nitrogen sky fifty per cent thicker at the surface than the air in your room right now, pressing against your skin like water five metres down in a swimming pool.

So why would a single deep breath put you to sleep, why are the pebbles under your boots made of frozen water harder than granite, and why is this mysterious world the one place in the solar system where you could strap on wings, flap your arms, and fly?

In this film I will take you down through the golden haze that hid Titan's secret surface for three centuries, ride aboard the European probe that parachuted onto an alien riverbed in 2005, and show you what you would see beside its dark hydrocarbon seas. Why did it take so long to discover that a moon could hold onto a sky of its own?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Moon With a Sky]

25 March 1655, The Hague. A twenty-five-year-old Dutch astronomer named Christiaan Huygens points a handmade telescope with a lens barely five centimetres wide toward Saturn, and spots a bright point of light circling the ringed planet every sixteen days.

For nearly three hundred years, astronomers assumed Titan was just another airless ball of rock and ice, like our own Moon, only bigger — wider, in fact, than the planet Mercury.

Then, in the winter of 1944, an astronomer named Gerard Kuiper works through freezing nights at the McDonald Observatory in Texas, splitting the sunlight reflected off Titan into a rainbow on photographic plates. Across the red and infrared wavelengths, dark vertical shadows cut through the spectrum. That signature belongs to methane gas. For the first time, humanity realises a moon in the outer solar system is wrapped in a thick atmosphere.

When NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft flies past Titan in November 1980, just four thousand kilometres above the cloud tops, engineers wait for the cameras to reveal continents, craters, or oceans. Instead, every photograph comes back as a featureless orange sphere. Smog cooked by sunlight out of nitrogen and methane hides the ground completely.

Why was Titan making fresh methane smog when sunlight should have destroyed all its methane in just a few tens of millions of years — and what secret was hiding beneath the clouds?

[CHAPTER CARD: Where Water Is Rock]

Fourteen January 2005. After riding aboard NASA's Cassini spacecraft for seven years, a saucer-shaped European lander named Huygens plunges into Titan's upper haze and opens its parachutes.

For two hours and twenty-seven minutes, the probe drifts down through murky orange clouds. As its cameras drop below thirty kilometres altitude, the haze thins, and a startling mystery appears: dark branching river channels carved into bright hills, draining toward a flat, dark shoreline.

When Huygens thuds down onto the plain, it does not sink. It lands with a soft crunch on damp sand, surrounded by rounded cobbles ten to fifteen centimetres across, just like stones in a stream bed on Earth.

Except those pebbles are not stone. At Titan's distance from the Sun — ten times farther out than Earth — the surface temperature sits at minus one hundred and seventy-nine degrees Celsius, nearly three times colder than the coldest winter night in Antarctica. At that temperature, ordinary water ice is frozen harder than granite bedrock.

So why were the pebbles rounded by running liquid? Because at minus one hundred and seventy-nine degrees, methane and ethane sit near their triple point, playing the exact role water plays on Earth: evaporating into clouds, falling as rain, carving canyons into water-ice bedrock, and pooling into lakes.

For seventy-two minutes on the surface, Huygens beams back pictures before its relay spacecraft, Cassini, drops below the horizon. It remains the farthest landing from Earth humanity has ever made. Yet if you stepped out beside that lander in a heated suit, what would you feel when the rain began to fall?

[CHAPTER CARD: Rain in Slow Motion]

Stand on Titan's surface in a suit heated against the deep freeze, and your first surprise is what you do not need. You do not need a rigid pressure suit like an astronaut on the Moon.

Titan's atmosphere is ninety-five per cent nitrogen — the same gas that makes up most of the air you are breathing right now — with about five per cent methane. Because gravity is only one-seventh as strong as Earth's, Titan stacks a deep column of cold gas overhead, making the surface air about four times denser than Earth's air at sea level.

All you would need to walk outside is a thick thermal suit and an oxygen mask covering your face, because there is no breathable oxygen in the sky, and trace clouds of hydrogen cyanide drift down from the upper haze.

What would you see if a storm rolled overhead? On Earth, a large raindrop is about six millimetres across and falls at nine metres a second. On Titan, weak gravity lets methane raindrops grow to nearly a centimetre wide — as big as marbles — and they drift down through the dense air at just one point six metres a second, as slowly as snowflakes floating past a streetlamp.

However, major rainstorms at any one spot may happen only once every few Titan years — and a single Titan year takes twenty-nine and a half Earth years. Case closed? Is Titan mostly an empty desert waiting decades between showers?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Seas of Kraken Mare]

Wrong. The equator may be lined with dunes of dark organic sand, but when Cassini swept its radar beam across Titan's north pole, it uncovered a secret coast that looks like Scotland drowned in liquid natural gas.

Hundreds of lakes and three great seas fill the northern basins. The largest, Kraken Mare, covers four hundred thousand square kilometres — larger than the Caspian Sea, the biggest inland body of water on Earth.

Next door sits Ligeia Mare, filled with liquid methane and ethane so clear that in 2014 Cassini's radar beam punched straight through the liquid and bounced off the seabed one hundred and sixty metres down. In Kraken Mare's central basin, the liquid was so deep the radar never even reached the bottom, meaning the sea is more than three hundred metres deep.

And deep beneath Titan's icy crust, tens of kilometres below those hydrocarbon seas, gravity measurements from Cassini revealed a second, hidden ocean — a global sea of liquid water mixed with ammonia, holding more water than all of Earth's oceans put together.

Wait — that raises a terrifying question. What if a spacecraft landed near those seas of liquid natural gas and fired its hot thrusters or struck a spark?

[CHAPTER CARD: Why a Match Won't Burn]

That is the scariest idea people repeat about landing on Titan: with hundreds of times more liquid methane and ethane on its surface than all the oil and gas reserves on Earth, wouldn't a single spark blow the lander apart?

Let us test that fear with simple chemistry.

On Earth, methane burns fiercely because our air is twenty-one per cent free oxygen, constantly replenished by green plants. Fire needs two partners: fuel and an oxidiser.

Titan has lakes of fuel, and five per cent methane in its sky, but virtually zero free oxygen. Its air is ninety-five per cent nitrogen. If you struck a match or fired a rocket thruster in Titan's atmosphere, nothing would catch fire. Without oxygen, a sea of liquid methane is no more explosive than a pond of cold water.

In fact, Titan flips fire backwards. On Earth, you carry a tank of gas and burn it in the oxygen around you. On Titan, if you wanted a flame, you would have to carry a tank of pure oxygen on your back and spray it out into the methane sky.

So if Titan will not explode, and its thick nitrogen blanket cushions anything moving through it, how will humanity explore this strange world next?

[CHAPTER CARD: The World Where You Could Fly]

Put Titan's two strangest numbers together — air four times denser than Earth's at the surface, and gravity only one-seventh as strong — and something wonderful happens to the physics of flight.

Aerodynamic lift depends on air density, while weight depends on gravity. On Titan, it is twenty-eight times easier to stay aloft than it is on Earth. Engineers have calculated that a human wearing a heated suit and wings on their arms could literally flap and fly through Titan's sky under their own muscle power.

And before astronauts ever strap on wings, a robot is going to fly there first.

In 2028, NASA plans to launch Dragonfly, a car-sized nuclear-powered rotorcraft with eight spinning blades. Arriving at Titan in 2034, Dragonfly will land among the dark equatorial dunes, warm itself with a plutonium generator through the long eight-Earth-day Titan nights, and leap across the landscape kilometres at a time.

Its destination is Selk Crater, an ancient impact basin where liquid water once melted through the icy bedrock and mixed with organic carbon from the sky — the exact recipe life needed when it started on the young Earth.

Titan is not a dead frozen rock. It is a time capsule of early Earth locked in a deep freeze, waiting for wings to cross its sky. Next, we travel one planet farther out to Uranus, where an ancient collision knocked an entire giant world onto its side and locked its poles into winters that last twenty-one years.
