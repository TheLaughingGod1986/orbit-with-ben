# Could AI Help Humanity Reach the Stars, and How Do Autonomous Probes Explore Deep Space?

Look out far beyond Earth orbit on November 18, 2026, where NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft — launched in 1977 and cruising at 61,000 kilometers per hour, or 38,000 miles per hour — becomes the first human-made object to cross one full light-day from Earth, at 25.9 billion kilometers, or 16.1 billion miles, after 49 years, 2 months, and 11 days of flight. Why does that distance create an unbreakable speed-of-light wall for every radio signal, and what secret lets robotic probes survive where no human pilot can reach? Because radio waves travel at the speed of light — 300,000 kilometers per second, or 186,000 miles per second — a single command from Earth takes 24 hours to reach Voyager 1, and 48 hours for a reply.

Not because our transmitters are slow, but because the physical mystery of the cosmos is that light itself takes 4.24 years one-way — and 8.5 years round-trip — to cross the 40.15 trillion kilometers, or 24.95 trillion miles, to our nearest star, Proxima Centauri.

What would you see and feel if your spacecraft had to dodge Martian boulders, hit an asteroid moonlet at 6.1 kilometers per second, or fly past a star 4.24 light-years away with zero help from Earth? Even though Voyager 1 speeds through vacuum at 61,000 kilometers per hour, it took nearly 50 years to travel the distance radio waves cross in 24 hours. Now how do autonomous probes think for themselves in deep space, and could AI help humanity reach the stars?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Spacecraft That Knew Where It Was]

For the first four decades of spaceflight, probes relied on navigators on Earth to calculate their position and radio up every turn.

Then, on October 24, 1998, NASA launched Deep Space 1 to test 12 technologies that had never flown on an interplanetary mission before.

Alongside a xenon ion engine whose blue beam pushed with a gentle thrust equal to the weight of a piece of paper on Earth, Deep Space 1 carried two artificial-intelligence breakthroughs: Remote Agent, which made its own planning decisions and diagnosed onboard problems, and Autonomous Optical Navigation, or AutoNav.

However, instead of waiting for Earth, AutoNav imaged distant asteroids and background stars until Deep Space 1 became the first spacecraft to know where it was in the solar system and change its own course. On July 29, 1999, Deep Space 1 used AutoNav to fly within 26 kilometers — 16 miles — of asteroid 9969 Braille at 15.5 kilometers per second, and on September 22, 2001, it tracked Comet Borrelly from 2,171 kilometers, or 1,350 miles, at 16.58 kilometers per second before shutting down its ion engine on December 18, 2001 after 16,265 hours of thrust and 4.3 kilometers per second of velocity change. Now how did that autonomous brain become a self-driving scientist on Mars?

[CHAPTER CARD: Thinking While Driving Across Mars]

When a rover explores Mars, radio signals take 3 to 22 minutes one way, creating a control paradox where real-time human driving is impossible.

To solve that mystery, NASA gave the Perseverance rover an AutoNav system that images terrain while rolling, builds a 3D hazard map, and steers up to 412 meters — 1,351 feet — in a single Martian day.

On October 11, 2026 — its 2,006th Martian sol — Perseverance reached 45.22 kilometers, or 28.10 miles, beating Opportunity's 45.16-kilometer record from 2004 to 2018 in 5.5 years versus nearly 15 years, and passing Apollo 17's 35.7 kilometers in 1972 and Lunokhod 2's 39 kilometers in 1973. Then Jet Propulsion Laboratory project manager Steven Lee noted that Perseverance's original plan called for 12.4 miles of driving, while deputy robotic chief Mark Maimone explained that Perseverance drove more than 90 percent of its distance using AutoNav — versus 5 percent on Spirit and Opportunity — processing 600,000 points per look compared with 20 points on NASA's microwave-oven-sized Sojourner rover across 100 meters and 83 sols in 1997.

Beyond driving, since May 2016, researchers Raymond Francis and Tara Estlin have run the AEGIS autonomous targeting system on Curiosity — published in *Science Robotics* in 2017 — firing its ChemCam laser without Earth in the loop and lifting geological target hits across 2.5 kilometers from 24 percent to over 93 percent. [CHAPTER CARD: Seventy-Three Minutes to Lock Onto an Asteroid]

You might assume that autonomous navigation only works on Mars, and that hitting an asteroid still requires pilots on Earth.

Wrong.

On September 26, 2022, NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test — or DART — hurtled toward Didymos 11 million kilometers, or 6.8 million miles, from Earth at 6.1 kilometers per second, or 3.8 miles per second.

As planetary scientists Terik Daly and Nancy Chabot reported in *Nature* in April 2023, DART's target was the 170-meter — 560-foot — moonlet Dimorphos orbiting the 805-meter asteroid Didymos every 11 hours and 55 minutes, but Dimorphos stayed hidden in the glare until the final approach.

At 4 hours and 5 minutes before impact, controllers handed DART to its Small-body Maneuvering Autonomous Real Time Navigation system, or SMART Nav. Processing DRACO camera frames onboard, SMART Nav first detected Dimorphos 73 minutes before impact, began firing thrusters toward Dimorphos 50 minutes before impact, finished steering 2.5 minutes before impact, and slammed into the moonlet — shortening its orbit by 32 to 33 minutes, more than 25 times NASA's 73-second benchmark. [CHAPTER CARD: Laser Sails and Self-Replicating Probes]

Beyond our solar system, Alpha Centauri and Proxima Centauri sit 4.24 light-years — 40.15 trillion kilometers — away, where a one-way radio signal takes 4.24 years.

In 2018, engineer Kevin Parkin published the system model in *Acta Astronautica* for Breakthrough Starshot — an unbuilt concept where photon pressure from a 1.06-micrometer ground laser accelerates a 4.1-meter circular dielectric light sail for 9 minutes to 20 percent of the speed of light, or 0.2 c, alongside a 1 percent light-speed precursor using a 19-centimeter sail.

At 20 percent of light speed, a gram-scale nanocraft crosses 4.24 light-years in about 21 years and flashes past the planets in hours — so onboard AI must find the worlds, aim its cameras, and align its downlink 4.24 years before Earth sees a single photon.

However, what if humanity wants to explore the entire Milky Way? Building on Hungarian-American mathematician John von Neumann's 1903 to 1957 theory of self-reproducing automata, astrophysicists Arwen Nicholson and Duncan Forgan proved in the *International Journal of Astrobiology* in 2013 that while stellar slingshots let a single Voyager-like probe explore the galaxy 100 times faster than powered flight, autonomous self-replicating probes cut galactic exploration time by 2 to 3 orders of magnitude — 100 to 1,000 times faster. [CHAPTER CARD: Why AI Is Humanity's Bridge to the Stars]

You might worry that sending autonomous probes toward the stars means machines will replace human explorers.

However, every mission proves the opposite: autonomous probes are the scouts that map the hazards and locate the water that make human exploration possible.

Across seven decades — from John von Neumann's 1903 to 1957 self-reproducing automata and Voyager 1's 1977 launch to its 1-light-day milestone at 25.9 billion kilometers on November 18, 2026, through Deep Space 1's 1998 to 2001 AutoNav flight across 16,265 hours, Nicholson and Forgan's 2013 slingshot math, Curiosity's May 2016 AEGIS laser hitting over 93 percent of targets, Kevin Parkin's 2018 0.2 c sail model, DART's September 26, 2022 SMART Nav impact spotting Dimorphos 73 minutes out, and Perseverance's October 11, 2026 record of 45.22 kilometers with over 90 percent AutoNav — autonomy is how human curiosity defeats the speed-of-light delay.
