# Could AI Help Humanity Reach the Stars, and How Do Autonomous Probes Explore Deep Space?

Look out beyond Earth orbit on November 18, 2026, where NASA's Voyager 1 — launched in 1977 and cruising at 61,000 kilometers per hour, or 38,000 miles per hour — becomes the first spacecraft to cross one light-day from Earth, at 25.9 billion kilometers, or 16.1 billion miles, after 49 years, 2 months, and 11 days. Why does that distance create a speed-of-light wall for every radio signal, and what secret lets probes survive where no pilot can reach? Because radio waves travel at the speed of light — 300,000 kilometers per second, or 186,000 miles per second — a command faces a 24-hour radio delay one way to Voyager 1, and 48 hours for a reply.

Not because transmitters are slow, but because the physical mystery of space is that one light-year equals 9.46 trillion kilometers, or 5.88 trillion miles, and light takes 4.24 years one-way — 8.5 years round-trip — to cross 40.15 trillion kilometers, or 24.95 trillion miles, to Proxima Centauri.

What would you see and feel if your probe had to dodge Martian boulders, hit an asteroid at 6.1 kilometers per second, or fly past a star 4.24 light-years away with zero help from Earth? Even though Voyager 1 speeds through vacuum at 61,000 kilometers per hour, that is a crawl compared to light, taking nearly 50 years to cross what radio waves cover in 24 hours. Now how do autonomous probes think for themselves in deep space, and could AI help humanity reach the stars?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Spacecraft That Knew Where It Was]

For four decades of spaceflight, probes relied on navigators on Earth to calculate their position and radio up every turn.

Then on October 24, 1998, NASA launched Deep Space 1 to test 12 technologies that had never flown on an interplanetary mission before.

Alongside a xenon ion engine pushing with a thrust equal to the weight of a piece of paper on Earth, Deep Space 1 carried two artificial-intelligence breakthroughs: Remote Agent, which planned tasks and diagnosed faults, and Autonomous Optical Navigation, or AutoNav.

However, instead of waiting for Earth, AutoNav imaged asteroids and background stars until Deep Space 1 became the first spacecraft to know where it was in the solar system and change its own course. On July 29, 1999, Deep Space 1 used AutoNav to fly within 26 kilometers — 16 miles — of asteroid 9969 Braille at 15.5 kilometers per second, and on September 22, 2001, it tracked Comet Borrelly from 2,171 kilometers, or 1,350 miles, at 16.58 kilometers per second before shutting down its ion engine on December 18, 2001 after 16,265 hours of thrust and 4.3 kilometers per second — or 3 miles per second — of velocity change. [CHAPTER CARD: Thinking While Driving Across Mars]

At an average distance of 228 million kilometers — 142 million miles — where sunlight takes 13 minutes to reach Mars, there is no joystick for real-time rover driving, so engineers send commands overnight.

To solve that mystery, NASA gave Perseverance an AutoNav system that images terrain while rolling, builds a 3D hazard map, and steers up to 412 meters — 1,351 feet — in a single sol.

On October 11, 2026 — its 2,006th sol — Perseverance reached 45.22 kilometers, or 28.10 miles, beating Opportunity's 45.16 kilometers, or 28.06 miles, from 2004 to 2018 in 5.5 years versus nearly 15 years, and passing Apollo 17's 35.7 kilometers in 1972 and Lunokhod 2's 39 kilometers in 1973. Then project manager Steven Lee noted that Perseverance's original plan called for 12.4 miles, while deputy robotic chief Mark Maimone explained that Perseverance drove over 90 percent of its distance on AutoNav — versus 5 percent on Spirit and Opportunity — processing 600,000 points per look compared with 20 points on microwave-oven-sized Sojourner across 100 meters, or 330 feet, and 83 sols in 1997.

Beyond driving, since May 2016, Raymond Francis and Tara Estlin have run AEGIS on Curiosity — published in *Science Robotics* in 2017 — firing its ChemCam laser without Earth in the loop and lifting target hits across 2.5 kilometers from 24 percent to over 93 percent. [CHAPTER CARD: Seventy-Three Minutes to Lock Onto an Asteroid]

You might assume that autonomous navigation only works on Mars, and that hitting an asteroid requires pilots on Earth.

Wrong.

On September 26, 2022, NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test — DART — hurtled toward Didymos 11 million kilometers, or 6.8 million miles, from Earth at 6.1 kilometers per second, or 3.8 miles per second.

As Terik Daly and Nancy Chabot reported in *Nature* in April 2023, DART's target was the 170-meter — 560-foot — moonlet Dimorphos orbiting the 805-meter, or half-mile, asteroid Didymos every 11 hours and 55 minutes, hidden in glare until final approach.

At 4 hours and 5 minutes before impact, controllers handed DART to its Small-body Maneuvering Autonomous Real Time Navigation system, or SMART Nav. Processing DRACO frames onboard, SMART Nav first detected Dimorphos 73 minutes before impact, fired thrusters 50 minutes out, finished steering 2.5 minutes out, and slammed into Dimorphos — shortening its 11-hour 55-minute orbit to 11 hours and 23 minutes, a 32-to-33-minute shift beating NASA's 73-second benchmark by over 25 times. [CHAPTER CARD: Laser Sails and Self-Replicating Probes]

Beyond our solar system, Alpha Centauri and Proxima Centauri sit 4.24 light-years — 40.15 trillion kilometers — away, while Voyager 1 will take 40,000 years just to pass within 1.7 light-years of Gliese 445.

In 2018, engineer Kevin Parkin published the system model in *Acta Astronautica* for Breakthrough Starshot — an unbuilt concept where photon pressure from a 1.06-micrometer laser accelerates a 4.1-meter circular light sail for 9 minutes to 20 percent of light speed, or 0.2 c, alongside a 1 percent light-speed precursor using a 19-centimeter sail.

At 20 percent of light speed, a nanocraft crosses 4.24 light-years in about 21 years — so onboard AI must find the planets, aim its cameras, and align its downlink 4.24 years before Earth sees a photon.

However, what if humanity wants to explore the Milky Way? Building on mathematician John von Neumann's 1903 to 1957 theory of self-reproducing automata, astrophysicists Arwen Nicholson and Duncan Forgan — born in 1984 — proved in the *International Journal of Astrobiology* in 2013 that while stellar slingshots let a single Voyager-like probe explore the galaxy 100 times faster than powered flight, autonomous self-replicating probes cut galactic exploration time by 2 to 3 orders of magnitude — 100 to 1,000 times faster. [CHAPTER CARD: Why AI Is Humanity's Bridge to the Stars]

You might worry that sending autonomous probes toward the stars means machines will replace astronauts.

However, every mission proves the opposite: autonomous probes are the scouts that map hazards and locate water for human explorers.

Across seven decades — from John von Neumann's 1903 to 1957 self-reproducing automata and Voyager 1's 1977 launch to its 1-light-day milestone at 25.9 billion kilometers on November 18, 2026, through Deep Space 1's October 24, 1998 to December 18, 2001 AutoNav flight across 16,265 hours, Nicholson and Forgan's 2013 slingshot math, Curiosity's May 2016 AEGIS laser hitting over 93 percent of targets, Kevin Parkin's 2018 0.2 c sail model, DART's September 26, 2022 SMART Nav impact spotting Dimorphos 73 minutes out, and Perseverance's October 11, 2026 record of 45.22 kilometers with over 90 percent AutoNav — autonomy is how curiosity defeats the speed-of-light delay.
