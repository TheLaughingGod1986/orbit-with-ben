# Why Is the 330-Million-Light-Year Boötes Void Almost Empty?

High in the northern sky beyond the red giant Arcturus in the constellation Boötes, as you cross the horizon into a spherical expanse of intergalactic space up to three hundred and thirty million light-years across where two thousand galaxies should shine, why have astronomers found only sixty galaxies inside the Boötes Void — and what mystery is pushing the universe's empty bubbles apart?

Across the observable universe, up to two trillion galaxies are woven by gravity and dark matter into a glowing cosmic web of filaments and walls surrounding giant underdense bubbles called cosmic voids — including the Boötes Void discovered in 1981, where the two-point-five-million-light-year gap between the Milky Way and Andromeda covers just one percent of the void and density drops toward one atom per cubic metre.

Why would humanity have missed every other galaxy until the 1960s if the Milky Way sat at the centre of that void, what secret did the Hubble Space Telescope uncover when two dwarf galaxies escaped our Local Void, and how will NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope map tens of thousands of voids across twelve thousand full moons?

[CHAPTER CARD: Threads and Bubbles of the Cosmic Web]

Nearly ten thousand galaxies gleam in a single Hubble Ultra Deep Field snapshot, and astronomers estimate up to two trillion galaxies fill the observable universe — but those galaxies are not scattered at random.

Gravity gathers galaxies into groups of one hundred or fewer — like our Local Group of over fifty galaxies on the edge of the Laniakea supercluster — and then into clusters like the Coma Cluster, where over one thousand galaxies span twenty million light-years inside gas heated to tens of millions of degrees.

In 1933, Swiss-born astronomer Fritz Zwicky noticed Coma Cluster galaxies moving too fast for visible matter to hold them and proposed an unknown dark matter — confirmed in the 1970s by American astronomer Vera Rubin in spiral galaxies — which forms filaments and twenty-million-light-year-thin walls from the five-hundred-million-light-year Coma Wall to the one-point-four-billion-light-year Sloan Great Wall.

Between stars sit one thousand atoms per cubic foot, in the intergalactic medium sits one atom per cubic foot, and deeper inside the voids between filaments sits just one atom per cubic metre. What happens right next door to the Milky Way?

[CHAPTER CARD: Fleeing the Local Void]

Right on the edge of our Local Group sits the closest cosmic desert to Earth: the Local Void, spanning one hundred and fifty million light-years across with its centre at least seventy-five million light-years away.

Because surrounding galaxy clusters pull harder than an empty bubble, the Milky Way and our neighbouring galaxies are moving away from the Local Void at around one hundred and seventy-five miles per second, while a lonely dwarf galaxy inside it, ESO 461-36, flees outward at one hundred and thirty-five miles per second.

When NASA's Hubble Space Telescope observed two dwarf galaxies emerging from the Local Void — Pisces A at nineteen million light-years from Earth and Pisces B at thirty million light-years, each holding only about ten million stars — it caught them entering a dense gas filament.

Until less than one hundred million years ago, billions of years in the void kept their hydrogen primitive, but crossing into that filament suddenly doubled their star-formation rate and ignited twenty to thirty bright blue young stars. What if you entered a void more than twice as wide?

[CHAPTER CARD: Sixty Galaxies Where Two Thousand Should Sit]

In 1981, astronomers mapping redshifts in the constellation Boötes discovered a far larger chasm — the Boötes Void, or the Great Void — stretching between two hundred and fifty and three hundred and thirty million light-years across.

While sunlight takes roughly eight minutes to cross over ninety-two million miles to Earth, light inside the Boötes Void takes hundreds of millions of years to travel from one side to the other.

Compare that with our closest galactic neighbour, the Andromeda galaxy: at about two point five million light-years from Earth, the span between the Milky Way and Andromeda covers only about one percent of the Boötes Void.

If we use a rough estimate of one galaxy every ten million light-years — four times farther than Andromeda — the Boötes Void should hold approximately two thousand galaxies, but astronomers have found only sixty galaxies inside it, all in a tube-shaped filament where smaller voids merged. [CHAPTER CARD: The Loneliest Galaxy in the Universe]

When the Advanced Camera for Surveys on NASA's Hubble Space Telescope photographed the spiral galaxy MCG+01-02-015, the background looked crowded with galaxies floating like microbes in a drop of water behind three local foreground stars.

However, that crowded backdrop is an optical paradox: MCG+01-02-015 actually sits alone inside a desolate cosmic void where matter drops to about one atom per cubic metre, earning it the title of the loneliest of galaxies.

Here on Earth, Belgian astronomer Georges Lemaître showed in 1927 that general relativity allows an expanding cosmos, and then in 1929 American astronomer Edwin Hubble used Mount Wilson redshift observations to confirm that galaxies are racing away from us.

However, as NASA and Hubble astronomers noted, if our Milky Way had been situated at the centre of the Boötes Void or in MCG+01-02-015's seat, we would not have known other galaxies existed until strong telescopes arrived in the 1960s. Why did such vast empty bubbles open across the cosmos?

[CHAPTER CARD: Dark Energy Inside the Empty Bubbles]

Wrong. A cosmic void is not a hole punched through space by an explosion — it is a region where gravity lost a tug-of-war to dark energy.

Scientists estimate that ordinary matter makes up only about five percent of the universe, while dark matter makes up about twenty-seven percent — about eighty-five percent of all matter, seen through gravitational lensing in the 2006 Bullet Cluster collision three point eight billion light-years away.

The remaining sixty-eight percent is dark energy — the mystery discovered in 1998 when distant Type Ia supernovae proved the universe's expansion is accelerating.

As gravity pulls normal and dark matter out of underdense regions into filaments, cosmic voids become dominated by dark energy, expanding into supervoids — including a theorized five-hundred-million-light-year Eridanus Supervoid six to ten billion light-years away toward the Cosmic Microwave Background Cold Spot. [CHAPTER CARD: Twelve Thousand Full Moons of Voids]

Because Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope study deep targets like pinholes, mapping giant voids requires NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope — named for astronomer Nancy Grace Roman, who served as NASA's first Chief of Astronomy.

Roman's infrared field of view is one hundred times larger than Hubble's, allowing it to map the universe up to one thousand times faster and capture around one hundred thousand pictures every year.

Across a portion of its survey covering two thousand four hundred square degrees — twelve thousand full moons of sky — Roman will measure tens of thousands of cosmic voids down to twenty million light-years across, stacking their shapes into a sphere to test dark energy.
