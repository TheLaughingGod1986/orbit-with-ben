# Why Does Dwarf Planet Ceres Have Glowing Salt Domes in the Asteroid Belt?

Orbiting two hundred and fifty-seven million miles from the Sun where sunlight takes twenty-two minutes to arrive and the largest object in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter spins once every nine hours, as you cross the main belt with NASA's Dawn spacecraft under twenty-two miles above its dark crust, why does dwarf planet Ceres have glowing salt domes in the asteroid belt?

Before Dawn arrived at this five-hundred-and-eighty-five-mile-wide world in 2015, telescopes spotted diffuse bright regions that many expected to be surface ice — yet inside the fifty-seven-mile-wide Occator Crater, Dawn discovered that more than three hundred bright features include the largest deposit of carbonate minerals outside Earth.

Now why do water-bearing salts still gleam where salts dehydrate within hundreds of years, what secret did Dawn's gravity data uncover when it revealed a brine reservoir twenty-five miles deep and a three-mile-high volcano called Ahuna Mons, and what happens next to our search for life after models showed Ceres once hosted a two-billion-year chemical feast for microbes?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Embryonic Planet Between Giants]

In 1801, Italian astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi spotted the first member of the asteroid belt ever discovered between Mars and Jupiter, named Ceres for the Roman goddess of corn and harvests.

Classified as a planet first and later as an asteroid, Ceres was designated a dwarf planet in 2006 along with Pluto and Eris.

Formed four point five billion years ago as an "embryonic planet" when Jupiter's gravity prevented it from finishing its growth, Ceres settled into the asteroid belt four billion years ago at two hundred and fifty-seven million miles — four hundred and thirteen million kilometres, or two point eight astronomical units — orbiting every one thousand six hundred and eighty-two Earth days, or four point six Earth years.

With a radius of two hundred and ninety-six miles — four hundred and seventy-six kilometres, or one-thirteenth the radius of Earth, like a poppy seed beside a nickel — Ceres spins every nine hours at a four-degree tilt and comprises twenty-five to thirty-five percent of the asteroid belt's mass, though Pluto is fourteen times more massive. Now why did astronomers suspect it hid water reserves?

[CHAPTER CARD: Twenty-Five Percent Water and Dawn]

Before Dawn reached Ceres, its low density showed the dwarf planet is about twenty-five percent ice by mass — the most water-rich body in the inner solar system after Earth, holding more water than Earth if composed of twenty-five percent water.

In 2003 and 2004, NASA's Hubble Space Telescope — named for American astronomer Edwin Hubble — showed Ceres has an icy layer around a rocky mantle, until in 2012 and 2013 the Herschel Space Observatory — named for British astronomer William Herschel — found water vapour from sublimating surface ice.

Then NASA's Dawn spacecraft pushed ion engines to six years in powered flight and a velocity change of twenty-five thousand seven hundred miles per hour, orbiting Vesta in 2011 to map its Rheasilvia basin — over three hundred and ten miles across and twelve miles deep — and two-hundred-and-ninety-mile canyons rivalling the Grand Canyon.

When Dawn entered orbit around Ceres in 2015 as the first spacecraft to visit a dwarf planet and orbit two deep-space destinations, you could see ammonia from beyond Jupiter and more than three hundred bright features called faculae. [CHAPTER CARD: Twenty-Two Miles Above Occator Crater]

Wrong. The glowing bright areas inside Ceres's craters are not exposed patches of pure surface ice from ancient impacts.

From 2017 until October 2018, Dawn dipped under twenty-two miles — thirty-five kilometres — above Ceres to inspect the fifty-seven-mile-wide Occator Crater and its reflective areas, Cerealia Facula and Vinalia Faculae.

What you see on that floor is mostly sodium carbonate — a compound of sodium, carbon, and oxygen forming the largest deposit of carbonate minerals outside Earth — alongside ammonium chloride and sodium chloride bound with water, left when liquid percolated up and evaporated into a salt crust.

Published on August 10, 2020 in *Nature Astronomy*, *Nature Geoscience*, and *Nature Communications*, Dawn's data proved that although the Occator impact occurred twenty million years ago, some bright regions are under two million years old — and because salts bearing water dehydrate within hundreds of years yet still hold water today, the fluids reached the surface very recently. Now where did that liquid come from?

[CHAPTER CARD: A Brine Reservoir Twenty-Five Miles Deep]

Unlike icy moons of the outer solar system, Ceres gets no internal heating from gravitational tugs by a large planet.

For Cerealia Facula, the Dawn team found the bulk of the salts came from a slushy area beneath the surface melted by the Occator impact twenty million years ago, before that heat subsided after a few million years.

However by studying Ceres's gravity and mapping crustal density with depth — a first for an ice-rich body — scientists discovered that impact fractures also reached a deep reservoir of brine, or salt-enriched water, about twenty-five miles deep, or forty kilometres, and hundreds of miles wide.

While craters up to one hundred and seventy miles across show a strong crust of hydrated salts, rocks, and clathrate gas cages, salts depress water's freezing temperature — forming conical hills like Earth's polar pingos, spotted on a dwarf planet for the first time, as salt and mud freeze into the lower crust. Now did that liquid build mountains elsewhere on Ceres?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Three-Mile-High Lonely Ice Volcano]

During its four-point-five-billion-year lifetime, Ceres was hit by many large asteroids, yet it is covered in small, young craters with none larger than one hundred and seventy-five miles — two hundred and eighty kilometres — across.

Instead of keeping giant basins like Vesta, Ceres smoothed out its surface features over time because layers of ice or salt lie just below the surface, while hydrothermal activity such as ice volcanoes erased large craters.

Rising above that landscape stands an enormous lonely mountain dubbed Ahuna Mons — three miles high, or five kilometres, on its steepest side — which the Dawn team believes formed as a cryovolcano.

That dome suggests liquid may still lie beneath Ceres's surface, driven by salt-enriched liquids that lower the freezing point, while shadowed crater "cold traps" hold water ice that sublimates into a thin water-vapour atmosphere. Now could this cold world ever have had the energy for life?

[CHAPTER CARD: Two Billion Years of Chemical Energy]

Around Ernutet Crater, Dawn found abundant aliphatic organic molecules — chains of carbon and hydrogen — alongside global surface carbon, requiring NASA to place Dawn in a decades-long quarantine orbit when its hydrazine fuel ran out in October 2018.

Today Ceres is cooler, with more ice and concentrated brine, because radioactive decay inside its rocky interior no longer supplies enough heat to keep its deep water from freezing.

However in a study published in *Science Advances* on August 20, 2025, researchers at Arizona State University and NASA built thermal and chemical models showing that between half a billion and two billion years after Ceres formed — two point five billion to four billion years ago — radioactive decay heated its rocky core to peak temperature, sending hot water with dissolved gases into its subsurface ocean like a "buffet for microbes."

That discovery means other five-hundred-and-eighty-five-mile-wide worlds without tidal heating could also have had a habitable period — and next, we travel inward from Ceres to ask why NASA's OSIRIS-REx spacecraft tapped asteroid Bennu on October 20, 2020 and delivered a one-hundred-and-twenty-one-point-six-gram sample on September 24, 2023 containing clays and phosphates from a primitive ocean world.
