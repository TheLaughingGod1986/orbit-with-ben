# Why Does Dwarf Planet Ceres Have Glowing Salt Domes in the Asteroid Belt?

Orbiting two hundred and fifty-seven million miles from the Sun where sunlight takes twenty-two minutes to arrive and the largest object in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter spins once every nine hours, as you cross the main belt with NASA's Dawn spacecraft under twenty-two miles above its dark crust, why does dwarf planet Ceres have glowing salt domes in the asteroid belt?

Before Dawn arrived at this five-hundred-and-eighty-five-mile-wide world in 2015, telescopes spotted diffuse bright patches that many expected to be exposed surface ice — yet inside the fifty-seven-mile-wide Occator Crater, Dawn discovered that more than three hundred gleaming regions include the largest deposit of carbonate salt minerals ever seen outside Earth.

Why do water-bearing salts still gleam on an airless surface where salts dehydrate within hundreds of years, what secret did Dawn's gravity measurements uncover when they revealed a brine reservoir twenty-five miles deep and a lonely three-mile-high ice volcano called Ahuna Mons, and what happens next to our search for life after models showed Ceres once hosted a two-billion-year chemical buffet for microbes?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Embryonic Planet Between Giants]

In 1801, Italian astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi spotted the first object ever found in the main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, named Ceres for the Roman goddess of corn and harvests.

Called a planet first and an asteroid for many years, Ceres is so much larger and more layered than its rocky neighbours that astronomers classified it as a dwarf planet in 2006 alongside Pluto and Eris.

Formed four point five billion years ago as an "embryonic planet" when Jupiter's strong gravity prevented it from finishing its growth, Ceres settled into the asteroid belt four billion years ago at two hundred and fifty-seven million miles — four hundred and thirteen million kilometres, or two point eight astronomical units — from the Sun, taking one thousand six hundred and eighty-two Earth days, or four point six years, to complete one orbit.

With a radius of two hundred and ninety-six miles — four hundred and seventy-six kilometres, one-thirteenth the radius of Earth, like a poppy seed beside a nickel — Ceres spins every nine hours at a four-degree tilt and holds twenty-five to thirty-five percent of the asteroid belt's mass, though Pluto is fourteen times more massive. Now why did astronomers suspect this world hid vast water reserves before you could ever see it up close?

[CHAPTER CARD: Twenty-Five Percent Water and Dawn]

Before Dawn reached Ceres, its low density showed the dwarf planet is about twenty-five percent water ice by mass — making it the most water-rich body in the inner solar system after Earth, and if composed of twenty-five percent water, holding even more water than Earth does.

In 2003 and 2004, observations by NASA's Hubble Space Telescope — named for American astronomer Edwin Hubble — showed Ceres is a nearly spherical body with an icy outer layer wrapped around a rocky mantle, and in 2012 and 2013 the Herschel Space Observatory — named for British astronomer William Herschel — detected water vapour sublimating into space.

Then NASA's Dawn spacecraft pushed ion propulsion to a record six years of powered flight and a velocity change of twenty-five thousand seven hundred miles per hour, or forty-one thousand four hundred kilometres per hour, orbiting giant asteroid Vesta in 2011 to map its three-hundred-and-ten-mile-wide, twelve-mile-deep Rheasilvia basin and canyons two hundred and ninety miles long.

When Dawn entered orbit around Ceres in 2015 as the first spacecraft to visit a dwarf planet and the only one to orbit two extraterrestrial worlds, you could see ammonia clays from beyond Jupiter and a glittering array of over three hundred bright spots called faculae. Now what did Dawn find when it dived under twenty-two miles above the brightest crater on Ceres?

[CHAPTER CARD: Twenty-Two Miles Above Occator Crater]

Wrong. The glowing bright spots inside Ceres's craters are not exposed patches of pure surface ice left over from ancient impacts.

From 2017 until October 2018, Dawn dipped under twenty-two miles — thirty-five kilometres — above Ceres to inspect the fifty-seven-mile-wide, or ninety-two-kilometre-wide, Occator Crater and its two reflective regions, Cerealia Facula at the centre and Vinalia Faculae to the east.

What you see on that crater floor is mostly sodium carbonate — a compound of sodium, carbon, and oxygen forming the largest deposit of carbonate minerals outside Earth — alongside ammonium chloride and sodium chloride chemically bound with water, left behind when salty liquid percolated up and evaporated into a bright salt crust.

Published on August 10, 2020 in *Nature Astronomy*, *Nature Geoscience*, and *Nature Communications*, Dawn's data proved that although Occator Crater formed twenty million years ago, some bright deposits are under two million years old, and because water-bearing salts dehydrate within hundreds of years yet still hold water today, briny liquid reached the surface very recently. Now where could liquid water come from on a cold world with no giant neighbour to heat it?

[CHAPTER CARD: A Brine Reservoir Twenty-Five Miles Deep]

Unlike Jupiter's moon Europa or Saturn's moon Enceladus, Ceres orbits alone without a giant planet's gravitational tug to warm its interior.

For Cerealia Facula, the Dawn team found the bulk of the salts originally came from a slushy subsurface pocket melted by the Occator impact twenty million years ago, before that impact heat subsided after a few million years.

However by mapping Ceres's gravity and measuring how crustal density increases with depth — the first such density profile for an ice-rich planetary body — researchers discovered that deep impact fractures also reached a long-lived reservoir of brine, or salt-enriched water, twenty-five miles, or forty kilometres, deep and hundreds of miles wide.

Because salts depress the freezing point of water while clathrate cages insulate the crust, that twenty-five-mile-deep brine reservoir stayed liquid long after the impact cooled, pushing pressurized groundwater up to build conical ice hills like Earth's polar pingos — spotted on a dwarf planet for the first time — while salt and mud incorporated into the freezing lower crust. Now did that salty plumbing build mountains elsewhere on Ceres?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Three-Mile-High Lonely Ice Volcano]

Across its four-point-five-billion-year lifetime, Ceres was struck by many large asteroids, yet Dawn found no impact craters larger than one hundred and seventy-five miles — two hundred and eighty kilometres — in diameter anywhere on the dwarf planet.

Instead of keeping giant ancient basins like Vesta, Ceres smoothed out its largest scars over time because layers of ice and lower-density salt lie just beneath the surface, while past hydrothermal and ice-volcano activity erased older craters.

Rising above that landscape stands a solitary, steep-sided mountain named Ahuna Mons — three miles, or five kilometres, high on its steepest side, with bright streaks of carbonate salts running down its slopes.

Dawn scientists concluded that Ahuna Mons is an enormous cryovolcano — an ice volcano that extruded a slurry of salt-enriched water, mud, and ice from Ceres's interior, while water ice in shadowed crater cold traps sublimates into a thin water-vapour atmosphere. Now could this cold, salty world in the asteroid belt ever have had the energy to support life?

[CHAPTER CARD: Two Billion Years of Chemical Energy]

Around Ernutet Crater, Dawn found abundant aliphatic organic molecules — chains of carbon and hydrogen that are building blocks of life — atop a carbon-rich crust, prompting NASA to place Dawn in a decades-long quarantine orbit when its hydrazine ran out in October 2018.

Today Ceres is cooler, with more ice and concentrated brine than in the past, because radioactive decay inside its rocky core no longer generates enough heat to keep a global ocean from freezing.

However in a study published in *Science Advances* on August 20, 2025, researchers at Arizona State University and NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory built thermal and chemical models proving that between half a billion and two billion years after Ceres formed — about two point five billion to four billion years ago — radioactive decay heated its rocky core to peak temperatures, driving hot hydrothermal fluids carrying carbon dioxide and methane into its underground ocean like a "buffet for microbes."

That discovery means other five-hundred-and-eighty-five-mile-wide dwarf planets and icy moons without planetary tidal heating could also have been habitable in their past — and next, we travel inward from Ceres to near-Earth space to ask why NASA's OSIRIS-REx spacecraft brought back a one-hundred-and-twenty-one-point-six-gram sample of carbon-rich asteroid Bennu containing water-locked clays and phosphates from a lost ocean world.
