# Is Betelgeuse About to Explode?

Look at Orion this winter, at the orange star on its shoulder. That star is dying. So is Betelgeuse about to explode, and what would you see if it did?

It is called Betelgeuse. One day it will blow itself apart, and for a few weeks it will shine so brightly that you could see it in the daytime sky.

In this film I will show you what kind of star it is, why it scared astronomers in 2019, how a giant star actually dies, what the night it goes would look like from your garden, and the honest answer to when.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Star in Orion's Shoulder]

Betelgeuse is a red supergiant. It is enormous. Put it where the Sun is, and its surface would reach out past the orbit of Mars, and out beyond the asteroid belt.

It is somewhere between five and seven hundred light-years away. Even measuring that is hard, because the star is so big and so restless that its centre is difficult to pin down.

And it is young. Our Sun is four and a half billion years old and halfway through its life. Betelgeuse is only about ten million years old, and it is already near the end of its life.

Why so fast? Because it is heavy. With somewhere around fifteen to twenty times the Sun's mass, it burns its fuel ferociously. It gives out around a hundred thousand times as much light as the Sun. Big stars live fast and die young.

You can find it yourself. On a winter evening, look for the three stars of Orion's belt, then go up and to the left. Its surface is cooler than the Sun's, and that is why it glows orange instead of white.

[TEACH: Luminosity roughly 1 × 10⁵ solar (Joyce et al. 2020); surface about 3,500 K against the Sun's 5,772 K. [CHAPTER CARD: The Great Dimming]

At the end of 2019, something strange happened. Betelgeuse, normally one of the brightest stars in the sky, began to fade.

By February 2020 it had dropped to about a third of its usual brightness. It was the faintest anyone had measured it in more than fifty years. The question went all round the world: was this it? Was Betelgeuse about to explode?

It was not. Telescopes caught the answer. A huge bubble of gas had burst out from the star's surface and drifted away. As it cooled, it turned into a cloud of dust, and the dust blocked part of the star from our view. A cool patch on the surface made the dimming deeper.

Why did it fool everyone? Because from Earth, all we see is a point of light. When that point fades, the obvious guess is that something terrible is happening inside.

By the spring, Betelgeuse was back. But the scare taught astronomers something. A dying giant does not fade quietly. It throws off pieces of itself as it goes.

[CHAPTER CARD: How a Giant Star Dies]

So how does a star like this die?

All its life, a star is a balance. Gravity pulls everything in. The heat of nuclear fusion in the core pushes everything out.

For most of its life, Betelgeuse fused hydrogen into helium. Now it is fusing helium into carbon. After that will come heavier and heavier elements, each stage burning faster than the last. The final stages take only years, then days.

The end of the line is iron. Fusing iron does not release energy. It costs energy. So when the core fills with iron, the fire goes out, and gravity wins.

In less than a second, a core heavier than the Sun collapses into a ball the size of a city. The outer layers fall in, bounce off it, and are blasted out into space. That is a supernova.

What is left behind is a neutron star, so dense that a teaspoon of it would weigh as much as a mountain. If you saw our film on neutron stars, you know how strange that place is. Or, if the core is heavy enough, a black hole.

[TEACH: Massive-star fusion stages: H → He → C → Ne → O → Si → Fe; the last (silicon burning) lasts about a day. Core collapse takes under a second; the remnant is a neutron star (about 20 km across; a teaspoon would weigh about a billion tonnes, roughly a mountain; callback to film 007) or a black hole. Betelgeuse is thought to be in core helium burning (Joyce et al. 2020).]

[CHAPTER CARD: The Night It Goes]

So imagine the night Betelgeuse explodes.

The first sign would not be light at all. It would be a flood of ghostly particles called neutrinos, streaming out of the collapsing core. They pass straight through the Earth. Detectors deep underground would catch a sudden burst, and astronomers would get the alert hours before the star brightened.

Then the light arrives. Within days, Betelgeuse would blaze as bright as the Moon at half phase. You could see it in broad daylight. At night, it would outshine everything in the sky except the Moon.

It would stay that bright for weeks, then fade slowly over months and years, until the shoulder of Orion was simply gone. The hunter would lose a shoulder, and every person who looked up afterwards would see a different Orion from the one we know.

Should anyone worry? No. A supernova only harms life on Earth if it is within a few dozen light-years. Betelgeuse is far beyond that. We would get the show, not the danger.

And here is a strange thought. The light from Betelgeuse takes centuries to reach us. If it had exploded some time in the late Middle Ages, more than five hundred years ago, we would only be finding out about now.

[CHAPTER CARD: So When?]

So when will it happen?

The best estimates say Betelgeuse has around a hundred thousand years left. That is the answer most astronomers would give you: not in our lifetimes, almost certainly.

Why can't we just look and tell? Because the changes that matter happen deep in the core, and the surface barely shows them until the very end. From outside, a star with a hundred thousand years left and a star with a hundred years left can look almost the same.

And the star keeps surprising us. A few researchers have argued that it could be much further along than we think, and might go within centuries. Most do not agree. The honest answer is that nobody can give you a date.

And in 2025, astronomers reported something new: a faint glimmer right where a hidden companion star was predicted to be, a small star circling Betelgeuse every six years or so, lost in its glare. If it is real, it explains a slow pulse in Betelgeuse's light that has puzzled astronomers for decades. The sighting is still tentative. The best chance to check comes in late 2027, when the two stars are furthest apart.

We have watched stars explode before. In the year 1054, astronomers in China recorded a new star so bright that it could be seen in daylight for more than three weeks. Today, at that spot in the sky, there is the Crab Nebula, still expanding.

So is Betelgeuse about to explode? Not tonight, and probably not for a very long time. But it will. And when it does, it will give something back.

Much of the oxygen you breathe and the calcium in your bones were made inside giant stars like this one, and scattered across space when they exploded. Betelgeuse will do the same, seeding the clouds that will become new stars and new worlds.

What if the most important thing about a dying star is not how it ends, but what it leaves behind?

The bigger question is closer to home. Long ago, giant stars near a cloud of gas and dust seeded it with new elements. That cloud became our Sun, our Earth and us. We are made of stars that lived and died before the Sun was born.
