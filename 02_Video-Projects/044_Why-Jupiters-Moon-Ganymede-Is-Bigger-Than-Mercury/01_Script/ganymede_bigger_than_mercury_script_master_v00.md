# Why Is Jupiter's Moon Ganymede Bigger Than the Planet Mercury?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Moon Larger Than a Planet]

On January 7, 1610, Italian astronomer Galileo Galilei pointed his telescope at Jupiter and discovered Ganymede alongside three other large moons — the first time a moon was seen orbiting a planet other than Earth, proving that our solar system does not revolve around Earth.

Named for the mythological cupbearer carried to Olympus by Jupiter in the form of an eagle, Ganymede spans about three thousand two hundred and seventy miles, or five thousand two hundred and sixty-two kilometres across — forty-one point three percent of Earth's diameter, and wider than the planet Mercury at four thousand eight hundred and seventy-nine kilometres.

However, comparing their masses reveals a striking physical paradox: Mercury contains three hundred and thirty point two sextillion kilograms at a density of five thousand four hundred and twenty-seven kilograms per cubic metre because its metallic core fills about eighty-five percent of its radius, whereas Ganymede holds one hundred and forty-eight point two sextillion kilograms at a density of just one thousand nine hundred and forty kilograms per cubic metre.

Formed about four point five billion years ago from gas and dust left over after Jupiter condensed, Ganymede grew wider than Mercury yet less than half as massive because a spherical shell of low-density water ice surrounds its rocky mantle and metallic iron core. [CHAPTER CARD: Grooves and Resonances at Jupiter]

Ganymede orbits six hundred and sixty-five thousand miles — one point zero seven million kilometres — from Jupiter, while Jupiter orbits four hundred and eighty-four million miles from the Sun, five times farther than Earth, where sunlight takes forty-three minutes to arrive at less than one-thirtieth of Earth's brightness and daytime temperatures range from minus two hundred and ninety-seven to minus one hundred and seventy-one degrees Fahrenheit.

Tidally locked to Jupiter, Ganymede completes one orbit every seven point one five five Earth days at ten point nine kilometres per second in a resonance with Europa and Io: each time Ganymede orbits Jupiter once, Europa orbits twice and Io orbits four times, while the whole system circles the Sun every twelve Earth years.

Across Ganymede's globe, forty percent of the surface is dark, heavily cratered terrain, while sixty percent is light grooved terrain where ridges rise up to two thousand feet high and run for thousands of miles, and where in 2004 scientists detected irregular subsurface lumps supported by the icy shell for billions of years.

In 1950, American writer Robert A. Heinlein imagined homesteading this moon in *Farmer in the Sky*, and after Voyager 2 photographed its grooved ridges, British writer Arthur C. Clarke created Ganymede City for *3001: The Final Odyssey* just days before NASA's Galileo spacecraft arrived in June 1996. What did Galileo's instruments uncover when it flew past?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Only Moon With a Magnetosphere]

Launched on Space Shuttle Atlantis on October 18, 1989 and entering Jupiter orbit on December 7, 1995 for thirty-four orbits and eight Ganymede flybys, NASA's two-thousand-two-hundred-and-twenty-three-kilogram Galileo spacecraft extended an eleven-metre magnetometer boom led by principal investigator Margaret Kivelson.

During its first encounter on June 26, 1996, resolving surface details eight miles across, Galileo discovered something never seen at any other moon: Ganymede generates its own magnetic field and magnetosphere inside Jupiter's magnetosphere, producing radio plasma waves that Galileo converted into whistling and static sounds.

Deep inside Ganymede, that permanent magnetic bubble is driven by a metallic iron core at the moon's centre, surrounded by a spherical rocky mantle and an outer shell of mostly ice.

Funneling charged particles into the poles, Ganymede's magnetic field creates ribbons of glowing, electrified gas — ultraviolet auroral ovals that NASA's Hubble Space Telescope photographed in 1998 while detecting a thin molecular oxygen atmosphere. [CHAPTER CARD: An Ocean Ten Times Deeper Than Earth's]

Wrong. Ganymede is not frozen solid down to its rocky mantle — its rocking auroras proved that a vast saltwater ocean lies beneath the ice.

Because Ganymede orbits inside Jupiter's tilting magnetic field, Jupiter's changing field pushes Ganymede's north and south auroral ribbons back and forth.

Scientists first suspected an ocean in the 1970s and Galileo took twenty-minute magnetic snapshots in 2002, before astronomers using NASA's Hubble Space Telescope announced on March 12, 2015 — a milestone highlighted by NASA associate administrator John Grunsfeld — that an electrically conducting saltwater ocean generates a secondary magnetic field whose magnetic friction reduces the auroral rocking to two degrees instead of the six degrees expected if no ocean were present.

From that two-degree rocking, scientists estimate Ganymede's ocean is sixty miles — one hundred kilometres — thick, ten times deeper than Earth's oceans, buried under a ninety-five-mile crust of mostly ice and holding more water than all of Earth's surface, with 2014 models suggesting water and ice may stack in layers like a club sandwich down to the rocky sea bottom. [CHAPTER CARD: Ocean Brine and Noon Water Vapour]

On June 7, 2021, NASA's Juno spacecraft flew just six hundred and fifty miles — one thousand and forty-six kilometres — above Ganymede, using its Jovian InfraRed Auroral Mapper spectrometer to map surface chemistry at better than zero point six two miles, or one kilometre, per pixel.

Published on October 30, 2023 in *Nature Astronomy*, Juno detected hydrated sodium chloride, ammonium chloride, sodium bicarbonate, and possible aliphatic aldehydes across latitudes ten to thirty degrees north — inside the equatorial belt up to forty degrees latitude where Ganymede's magnetic field shields the ice from Jupiter's radiation, preserving deep ocean brine.

Meanwhile, a July 26, 2021 study combining 2018 Hubble Cosmic Origins Spectrograph data with 1998 to 2010 images revealed that around noon near the equator, surface ice warms enough to sublimate directly from solid ice into water vapour.

Together, Juno's one-kilometre infrared maps and Hubble's ultraviolet spectra proved that Ganymede's surface preserves ocean salts and releases midday water vapour. How will humanity finally enter orbit around this ocean world?

[CHAPTER CARD: First Spacecraft to Orbit Another Moon]

On April 14, 2023, the European Space Agency, with NASA and JAXA instruments, launched the six-thousand-kilogram Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer — JUICE — from Kourou, French Guiana, unfolding ten solar panels spanning eighty-five square metres to power ten science instruments.

Across an eight-year cruise with gravity assists at Venus, Earth, and the Earth–Moon system, JUICE will reach Jupiter in July 2031, fly past Europa, Callisto, and Ganymede, and then enter orbit around Ganymede — becoming the first spacecraft ever to orbit a moon in the outer solar system.

During its four-year science mission, JUICE will probe Ganymede's icy crust, its hundred-kilometre saltwater ocean, and its magnetic dynamo as a laboratory for habitable ocean worlds across the cosmos.
