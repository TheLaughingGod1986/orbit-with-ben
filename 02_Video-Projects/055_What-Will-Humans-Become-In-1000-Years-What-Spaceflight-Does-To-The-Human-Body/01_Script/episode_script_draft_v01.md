# Why Does Spaceflight Rewrite the Human Body, and What Will Humans Become in 1,000 Years?

Look inside the International Space Station between March 2015 and March 2016, where astronaut Scott Kelly spent 340 days in orbit while his identical twin brother Mark Kelly stayed on Earth. Why did that single year in weightlessness trigger a biological mystery and send a hidden signal across his chromosomes, and what if humans never came back down? Without gravity pulling blood downward, fluids surge toward the head, weight-bearing bones dissolve at 1 to 2 percent per month, and thousands of genes switch on and off like fireworks.

Not because the human body is defective in space, but because a biological secret of every bone, blood vessel, and DNA repair pathway in our cells is that it was tuned for Earth's 9.82 meters per second squared of surface gravity and protective atmosphere.

What would you see and feel if your descendants lived beyond Earth for 1,000 years — about 35 generations — across orbital stations and Mars? In weightlessness, an astronaut loses as much lower-body bone strength in 6 months as an older adult on Earth loses in a decade — while a round-trip flight to Mars adds 0.66 sieverts of radiation before stepping onto a world with 38 percent of Earth's gravity. Now how do scientists measure what spaceflight does to the human body, and why will 1,000 years in space reshape us?

[CHAPTER CARD: Three Hundred and Forty Days in Orbit]

In March 2015, NASA launched an experiment that had never been possible before: sending astronaut Scott Kelly aboard the International Space Station for 340 days while his identical twin brother, retired astronaut Mark Kelly, remained on Earth as a baseline.

To track every molecular mystery across that 1-year mission, NASA coordinated 10 research teams who analysed blood, urine, microbiome, ultrasound, and cognitive samples before, during, and for 6 months after Scott landed in March 2016, publishing their integrated findings in *Science* in April 2019.

Then Weill Cornell Medicine principal investigator Christopher Mason described the genetic response as an explosion like fireworks taking off as soon as the human body enters space: thousands of genes changed how they turned on and off to cope with DNA damage, immune stress, and a 7 percent body-mass drop while Scott consumed 30 percent fewer calories than expected.

But the biggest paradox appeared at the ends of his chromosomes: his protective telomere caps — which normally shorten as we age — lengthened in orbit until he landed, then shrank within days, leaving more short telomeres even after 91.3 percent of his gene-expression changes returned to baseline within 6 months. [CHAPTER CARD: Dissolving Bones and Shifting Fluids]

On Earth, every step sends mechanical shocks through your heels, shins, and hips, signalling bone cells to replace the calcium your body constantly dissolves.

In microgravity, however, that loading vanishes, and two physical shifts reshape the astronaut body.

First, blood and water that gravity normally pools in your legs shift upward toward your chest and head, elevating intracranial pressure, thickening the carotid artery wall, raising the water-regulating protein AQP2, and flattening the back of the eyeball in Spaceflight-Associated Neuro-Ocular Syndrome.

Then, in June 2022, bone researcher Leigh Gabel and her colleagues used high-resolution 3D computed tomography at 61 micrometers — finer than a human hair — to image the shinbones of 17 astronauts with a mean age of 47 years before and for 12 months after flight. They proved why weightlessness is a deeper skeletal risk: while weight-bearing bone mineral density falls 1 to 2 percent per month in space, 9 of 17 astronauts still had not fully recovered tibial bone mineral density 12 months after landing, with missions over 6 months leaving a 3.9 percent median deficit in bone strength compared with 0.4 percent on missions under 6 months. Now what happens when astronauts fly across deep space to Mars?

[CHAPTER CARD: Five Hundred and Sixty Million Kilometers of Cosmic Rays]

You might assume that once astronauts strap inside a metal spacecraft on the way to Mars, its hull will shield their DNA from deep-space radiation the way the Space Station is shielded near Earth.

Wrong.

On November 26, 2011, NASA launched the Mars Science Laboratory carrying the Curiosity rover on a 253-day, 560-million-kilometer cruise to Mars, and physicists Cary Zeitlin and Donald Hassler used Curiosity's Radiation Assessment Detector — or RAD — to uncover the hidden secret of radiation inside a shielded interplanetary spacecraft.

Out beyond Earth's magnetosphere, heavy atomic nuclei stripped of their electrons — galactic cosmic rays forged in supernovae — slam through spacecraft walls and shatter into secondary particle showers that slice across living DNA strands.

When Cary Zeitlin and Donald Hassler published their cruise findings in *Science* in May 2013, and followed them in 2014 with 300 days of surface measurements after Curiosity landed on Mars on August 7, 2012, they proved that even the shortest round-trip Mars transit with current propulsion and shielding delivers 0.66 plus or minus 0.12 sieverts of radiation — about 660 millisieverts, equivalent to more than 30 full-body CT scans before spending a single day on Mars. Now what if human families stay on Mars for 1,000 years under 38 percent gravity?

[CHAPTER CARD: Evolution Across Thirty-Five Generations]

If a self-sustaining human settlement takes root on Mars — where mean surface gravity is 3.73 meters per second squared, or 38 percent of Earth's 9.82 meters per second squared — how fast could 1,000 years rewrite human DNA?

Ever since English naturalist Charles Darwin published his theory of natural selection in 1859, and American geneticist Hermann Joseph Muller proved in 1927 that ionizing radiation accelerates mutation rates, biologists have investigated the mystery of how quickly an extreme environment can shift a human genome.

Then, in July 2010, genomics researchers Xin Yi and Rasmus Nielsen answered that question in *Science* by sequencing the protein-coding exomes of 50 ethnic Tibetans — encompassing 92 percent of human genes at 18-fold coverage — to see how humans adapted to low-oxygen air on the Tibetan Plateau.

They discovered that a single-letter DNA variant in the gene *EPAS1*, which regulates red blood cell production under hypoxia, showed a 78 percent frequency difference between Tibetan and lowland Han samples — the fastest allele frequency change ever observed at any human gene, accomplished in just a few thousand years. Across 1,000 years on Mars — roughly 35 generations — living in subsurface habitats under 0.38 g gravity would select for DNA-repair and fluid-balance variants. [CHAPTER CARD: What Humans Will Actually Become in 1,000 Years]

You might worry that between 0.66 sieverts of interplanetary cosmic rays, 1 to 2 percent monthly bone loss in microgravity, and 38 percent gravity on Mars, humans who leave Earth will mutate within 1,000 years into fragile, unrecognizable creatures who can never come home.

However, the biology and physics we have measured tell a far more grounded story: in NASA's Twins Study, 91.3 percent of Scott Kelly's gene-expression shifts returned to baseline after 340 days in space, proving that most spaceflight changes are flexible epigenetic adjustments rather than permanent genetic damage.

Across every milestone between 1859 and June 2022 — from Darwin's 1859 natural selection and Yi and Nielsen's July 2010 *EPAS1* discovery to Curiosity's 2011–2012 RAD radiation findings, the 2015–2016 Twins Study, and Gabel's 2022 61-micrometer bone scans — we see how humanity will pair natural adaptation in DNA repair and fluid balance with rotating habitats and rock-shielded Martian cities.

Humans in 1,000 years will still be unmistakably human — slightly taller and lighter-boned in 0.38 g Martian towns, carrying higher frequencies of radiation-resilient and fluid-regulating genes — and next, we look ahead to the intelligent machines scouting the path before us to ask whether AI can help humanity reach the stars, and how autonomous probes explore deep space.
