# Space Mysteries Explained in 10 Minutes (6 Cosmic Puzzles)

Why don't the outer stars in orbit around our Milky Way escape into deep space? Right now they race far too fast for visible gravity to hold them, yet our galaxy stays intact. Ordinary atoms make up only five per cent of the universe, while an invisible mystery called dark matter makes up twenty-seven per cent — more than five times as much unseen mass as every star, planet and person combined.

In 1933, Swiss-born astronomer Fritz Zwicky spotted galaxies in the Coma Cluster moving far too quickly for their observed ordinary matter to bind them, and dubbed the hidden pull dark matter. Then, in the 1970s, American astronomer Vera Rubin measured stars near the edges of spiral galaxies and proved their rapid outer orbits required a colossal invisible halo organising galaxies on the largest scales.

In 2006, telescopes observed two giant galaxy clusters colliding in the Bullet Cluster three point eight billion light-years away, delivering some of the best direct evidence for dark matter as unseen gravity separated from glowing gas. Yet nearly a century after Zwicky's clue, what dark matter is made of remains unknown. What happens when deep space fires a signal faster than a blink?

[CHAPTER CARD: Fast Radio Bursts — Millisecond Signals From Deep Space]

In 2007, astronomers combing archival radio survey data uncovered a bright signal lasting under five milliseconds that arrived from beyond our galaxy. What secret cosmic engine could fire a radio flash that powerful across intergalactic distance?

For thirteen years, every fast radio burst arrived from other galaxies, leaving researchers debating exotic bursts with no local witness. However, on 28 April 2020, space observatories including Swift, Fermi, NICER and Wind caught an intense X-ray storm while radio arrays CHIME and STARE2 recorded a simultaneous radio flash from inside our own Milky Way — fourteen thousand to forty-one thousand light-years away.

The source was magnetar SGR 1935+2154, the city-sized crushed core of a dead star whose magnetic field can be ten trillion times stronger than a refrigerator magnet, and up to a thousand times stronger than a typical neutron star. Its radio burst lasted only a thousandth of a second — thousands of times brighter than any magnetar radio signal seen before in our galaxy — while the paired half-second X-ray flare released as much energy as our Sun produces in a month. Still, why far brighter repeating bursts blaze from distant galaxies remains a mystery. Where does the universe hide its emptiest deserts?

[CHAPTER CARD: Cosmic Voids — The Bubbles Where Galaxies Vanish]

You might picture space as an even carpet of stars. Instead, gravity draws up to two trillion galaxies into twisting filaments called the cosmic web, leaving colossal empty bubbles between them as if galaxies rested on hollow shells — cosmic voids stretching several hundred million light-years across, thousands of times wider than our hundred-thousand-light-year Milky Way.

In 1981, a deep redshift survey pointed toward the constellation Boötes and uncovered a million-cubic-megaparsec void: out of one hundred and thirty-three measured galaxies, only one appeared inside the central gap where uniform space should have peaked.

What would you see floating inside a desolate void like the one around lonely galaxy MCG+01-02-015? Almost nothing — matter thins to about one atom per cubic metre, whereas the space between stars inside a galaxy can hold a thousand atoms in a single cubic foot. What if our Milky Way sat inside such a wasteland? Humanity would not have known other galaxies existed until strong telescopes arrived in the 1960s. Because voids are relatively empty of matter, dark energy dominates inside them, so NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will map tens of thousands of cosmic voids down to twenty million light-years wide. Why can't our sharpest telescopes agree on how fast space itself is stretching?

[CHAPTER CARD: The Hubble Tension — The Universe Disagrees With Itself]

In 1929, American astronomer Edwin Hubble confirmed that the universe is expanding, with distant galaxies moving away faster the farther out they sit. Nearly a century later, a stubborn cosmic paradox refuses to vanish because two gold-standard speedometers disagree.

Reading the afterglow of the Big Bang from thirteen point eight billion years ago, Europe's Planck satellite predicts space should expand today at sixty-seven point four kilometres per second per megaparsec. Yet climbing the cosmic distance ladder with pulsating Cepheid variable stars and exploding supernovae nearby, Hubble and Gaia measure seventy-three point five kilometres per second per megaparsec — roughly nine per cent faster.

Case closed? Wrong. Many scientists suspected stellar crowding had blurred Hubble's older starlight measurements as astronomers looked deeper into space. However, in 2023 and 2024, the James Webb Space Telescope zoomed in with sharper infrared vision on one thousand Cepheids across five supernova host galaxies out to one hundred and thirty million light-years — and confirmed Hubble was accurate all along. With measurement error ruled out, this mystery may point to exotic dark energy, exotic dark matter, or a revision to gravity itself. Deeper in time, Webb uncovered an even earlier puzzle.

[CHAPTER CARD: UHZ1 — The Black Hole That Grew Too Fast]

How big could a black hole possibly get when the universe was only three per cent of its current age? In November 2023, NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory and the James Webb Space Telescope peered behind galaxy cluster Abell 2744, whose gravity bent and magnified faint background light by about a factor of four.

Hidden thirteen point two billion light-years away sat galaxy UHZ1, seen just four hundred and seventy million years after the Big Bang. Over two weeks of Chandra observations caught superheated X-ray gas blazing around the most distant black hole ever detected in X-rays, already weighing between ten million and one hundred million Suns.

In nearby galaxies today, a central supermassive black hole typically holds only about a tenth of a per cent of its host galaxy's stellar mass. UHZ1's black hole rivals the mass of every star in its galaxy combined — roughly a thousand times heavier in proportion. Because physical limits cap how quickly a black hole can grow, dying first stars leaving seeds of ten to one hundred Suns look far too small. Instead, UHZ1 points to heavy seeds of ten thousand to one hundred thousand Suns collapsing straight from colossal gas clouds — like planting a sapling instead of a seed. Yet even giant black holes are dwarfed by the biggest unknown of all.

[CHAPTER CARD: Dark Energy — The Force Pushing Space Apart]

For decades, astronomers expected gravity to slow cosmic expansion down over time. Then in 1998, two competing teams led by Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess — work awarded the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics — measured distant Type Ia supernovae, whose known brightness lets scientists gauge cosmic distance. Those explosions looked dimmer than expected, revealing an astonishing secret: the universe was not slowing down at all; its expansion is speeding up.

Today, NASA estimates dark energy accounts for roughly sixty-eight point three to seventy per cent of the entire universe. Combine dark energy with dark matter, and ninety-five per cent of everything out there remains unseen — leaving every star, ocean and person you have ever known inside the last five per cent.

Does an accelerating universe mean space is about to tear our world apart? Not here. On local scales, gravity still wins easily: Earth stays bound to our Sun, and our Milky Way stays locked alongside Andromeda and over fifty other galaxies inside our Local Group for billions of years, leaving a sky rich with wonder still waiting to be mapped.
