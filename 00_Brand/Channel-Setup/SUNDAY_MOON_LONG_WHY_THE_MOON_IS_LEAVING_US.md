[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: The Moon drifts. First 3 seconds: the Moon slowly moving farther from a night-side Earth, one Moon only, no title card, no logo, no Orbit. The drift continues over a quiet coast, starry sky, no Earth globe.]
[TEACH: The familiar Moon is already drifting outward; this film will show the cause, the closer past, and the sky that follows.]

The Moon is leaving us. In this film I will show you why it is drifting, what Earth was like when it was close, and what happens when it is gone.

How would you notice?

You would not feel it tonight. The escape sits past the horizon of one lifetime. What I will show you is a laser timing the gap, a young Earth under a much nearer Moon, and an orbit grown too wide to cover the Sun. Gravity still holds the Moon. The distance still grows.

[CHAPTER CARD: A Measured Gap]
<!-- HOOK -->

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: 3.8 centimetres. A dark observatory sends a thin laser to the Moon. Corner-cube mirrors in the lunar dust return the flash. A timer on Earth creeps longer, year by year. No Orbit. No readable instruments beyond the phone name.]
[TEACH: Lunar laser ranging times photon pulses off the Apollo and Luna mirrors and finds the Moon recedes by about 3.8 centimetres a year.]

The proof is already sitting in the lunar dust.

Astronauts on Apollo, and the Luna landers, set down small mirrors. Corner cubes. A photon pulse that hits them comes straight back. Observatories here fire that light and time the journey. The journey keeps taking a fraction longer. Put the extra time into distance, and the Moon is about 3.8 centimetres farther away each year. About the width of your fingernail.

Why trust a change your eyes will never catch?

Because the same mirrors answer, year after year. Gravity has not dropped the Moon. The orbit has taken one more quiet step outward. Live a whole life under that step and you still would not fill a small room with the extra distance. But let geology keep the count, and a fingernail becomes a different sky. Until the laser speaks, the motion is invisible. Now you have it in centimetres.

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: 38 kilometres. A simple growing gap between Earth and the Moon, today’s slow rate drawn out across deep time, one Earth and one Moon, no Orbit, no countdown clock.]
[TEACH: At today’s rate of about 3.8 centimetres a year, a million years is about 38 kilometres — a scale, not a promise that the rate never changes.]

At the rate measured today, a million years is about 38 kilometres. That is a scale, not a timetable. The drift was quicker when the Moon sat nearer and the pull was stronger. A lifetime is a gap you would walk past. An age of rock is a new place in the sky.

[CHAPTER CARD: A Nearer Moon]
<!-- ESCALATION -->

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: A huge Moon. The young Earth, debris from a giant impact settling into one Moon, that Moon much nearer and huge in the sky. One Moon only. No Orbit.]
[TEACH: The leading account is that the Moon formed from impact debris, then orbited far closer than it does now.]

Run the story backwards.

The Moon did not start where your window puts it. The leading idea is a giant impact. A collision flung debris into orbit around the young Earth, and that debris gathered into the Moon. Soon after, the Moon was much closer. Same mass you know. A much shorter leash. In that sky the lunar disc would have taken a vast share of the view, a wide face of rock rather than a small lamp.

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: Wilder tides. Camera on a young shore under ONE Moon, starry sky, NO Earth globe, NO planet Earth in the background. Huge tides climb the coast. Mid-plate still shows no Earth in the sky. Orbit is small on the rocks, looking up.]
[ORBIT ACTS: Orbit stands small on the shore and looks up at the huge Moon. Cream eyes widen. He does not point, fly, or explain. One Orbit, one face, underside glow only, reaction only.]
[TEACH: A closer Moon raised much stronger ocean tides than the coasts you know.]

Then the water stops being polite.

A nearer Moon pulls harder. Tides that now creep up a beach would have come in as weather. What if you stood on that shore? You would feel the ocean arrive with a weight no modern harbour is built to meet. The coastline would be redrawn on a short clock. Orbit only looks up. The tide is the lesson.

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: A shorter day. Young Earth turning quickly, daylight and darkness chasing each other across open ocean, the huge Moon still close. No Orbit. One Earth, one Moon.]
[TEACH: Early Earth spun faster, so a day could last a handful of hours instead of twenty-four.]

The day was shorter too. Earth was spinning faster. A day could last a handful of hours — bright, then dark, then bright again — instead of the long morning you are used to. What would you recognise? Perhaps the feel of water. Perhaps the air. Not the clock, and not that enormous Moon. Deeper in the past, the planet was still deciding how long a day was allowed to be.

[CHAPTER CARD: The Bulge That Leads]
<!-- DISCOVERY -->

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: The bulge leads. A clear moving diagram. Earth turns. Two ocean bulges sit a little ahead of the Moon. One Earth, one Moon. No Orbit. No labels except the phone name.]
[TEACH: Earth spins faster than the Moon completes an orbit, so friction drags the tidal bulge ahead of the Moon.]

Why does the distance grow at all? The engine is still running, and you are standing on it.

The Moon’s gravity lifts tides on Earth. Most of that lift is the ocean. A little of it is the solid ground itself. Earth’s gravity lifts a tide on the Moon as well. Those ocean bulges do not rest directly under the Moon. Earth turns faster than the Moon finishes an orbit, so friction tows the bulge slightly ahead. That is the whole hinge.

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: A wider orbit. The same diagram. The leading bulge pulls the Moon forward and the path around Earth slowly widens. No Orbit.]
[TEACH: The leading bulge pulls the Moon forward, so the orbit gains energy and the Moon climbs outward.]

Then the bulge ahead tugs the Moon forward along its path. A forward tug feeds energy into the orbit. A richer orbit is a wider one. The Moon climbs, centimetre by centimetre, the same 3.8 centimetres the laser already timed. You could not film the shove from a pier in a single night. You can watch the shape of it: water first, then a wider path.

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: Days lengthen. The diagram shows Earth turning a little more slowly while the Moon sits farther out. No Orbit.]
[TEACH: Earth loses a little spin while the Moon gains orbital energy, so the length of a day slowly increases.]

Earth pays for that climb with spin. Your day grows a little longer. Not so you would miss a bus. Beyond a human span, the added time becomes hours on a single turn. The mass of the oceans and the pull of gravity keep a plain account. Distance goes to the Moon. Rotation leaves Earth.

Could the drift reverse while the oceans are still here?

Not while Earth still spins faster than the Moon goes round its orbit. Keep that order, and the bulge stays ahead. However uneven the coasts, the basins, and the tide in the rock, the net direction is outward. The answer is not one pure note. It is a spectrum of pulls with one result.

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: Fossil days. A dry, matte fossil coral on dark stone, growth bands clear, museum light, no glow, no Orbit.]
[TEACH: Ancient corals and shells preserve more days in a year, evidence that Earth once spun faster.]

The stone kept the count. Ancient corals and shells put down more days in a year when the spin was faster. Bands in calcium, from before clocks. Suddenly the laser and the fossil are telling one story. A quicker Earth was not a fable.

[CHAPTER CARD: A Cover That Fits]

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: Same size sky. From Earth, the Sun and the Moon appear the same width. One Sun, one Moon, black space, no Orbit, no caption except the phone name.]
[TEACH: The Moon and the Sun have nearly the same angular size in Earth’s sky because the Sun’s greater size is balanced by its greater distance.]

Now the gift, and the paradox inside it.

The Sun is enormously larger than the Moon. It is also enormously farther away. From your ground, the two discs match. A small near body and a huge far body take the same bite of sky. That match is a coincidence of distance and size. It is also why a Moon can hide a star.

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: Perfect cover. Totality: the Moon’s disc exactly hides the Sun, a pale corona around the black edge, one ridge on Earth. Orbit is small on the ridge, watching. One Moon, one Sun.]
[ORBIT ACTS: Orbit stands on the ridge and watches the light fail. Cream eyes widen as the air cools. He does not speak, point, or fly. One Orbit, one face, reaction only.]
[TEACH: Matching angular size lets the Moon cover the Sun’s bright face in a total solar eclipse and leave the corona visible.]

This is why the day can go briefly wrong, in the gentlest sense. The bright face of the Sun is covered. A pale corona stands in a ring of absence. The atmosphere cools. Birds settle. You feel the temperature drop on your skin. Orbit feels the change and does nothing clever. The alignment is the event.

It does not stay this precise.

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: A bright ring. A future annular eclipse. The Moon’s disc is too small to cover the Sun, so a hoop of sunlight remains. Same kind of ridge, no Orbit, one Moon, one Sun.]
[TEACH: As the Moon’s orbit widens, its apparent size shrinks, and total eclipses give way to annular eclipses — a ring of sunlight instead of a full cover.]

As the orbit widens, the Moon looks smaller. The line-up still happens. The cover does not. The eclipse turns annular: a bright ring, and daylight that never fully leaves. Early on, the Moon was too close for this delicate fit. Later, it is too far to erase the Sun. You are in the middle years, the stretch when the cover can be perfect.

What if the wonder is that window?

[CHAPTER CARD: Still Look Up]

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: This window. A present-day night. One familiar Moon, calm water, a soft tide, starry sky, no Earth globe, no Orbit.]
[TEACH: The recession is slow and still underway; we live in the interval when tides, day length, and a perfect solar cover still match ordinary life.]

The Moon is leaving by about the width of your fingernail each year.

That is small enough to ignore at a bus stop. It is large enough, given time, to end the perfect cover and to stretch the day. You cannot switch it off. You were not asked to. The human part is to notice a machine that was already running.

What else have you treated as fixed?

The photon’s round trip shows the drift. The fossils show a faster spin. The bulge shows the trade: spin for distance. The eclipse shows a date, and that date is written in geology, not in next year’s diary. The exact far century is unknown from any calendar on your wall. Until you measure, “forever” is only the reach of your eyes.

And when the cover is gone, the Moon is not. It is smaller in the sky, still on its orbit, still there to look at. Gone, here, means gone as the close body that can hide the Sun. The heavens do not lose it.

[VISUAL MUST: ON SCREEN: The Sun’s tide. A future shore under one smaller Moon, starry sky, no Earth globe, no planet Earth in the background. A modest tide rises. No Orbit. Gate a mid-plate frame.]
[TEACH: Even with a more distant Moon, the Sun still raises a tide; what eases is the Moon’s added share of the rise and fall.]

The sea does not go quiet either. The Sun still raises a tide of its own. What eases is the Moon’s extra share, the part that lifts a spring tide. High water and low water draw a little closer. The day, already growing longer, keeps growing. None of it arrives as a shock. It arrives as a shore you would still recognise, under a smaller Moon.

So look up when you next can. The gap is already wider than it was when you were born. For a while yet, on ground that still receives a full shadow, the Moon can still cover the Sun.
