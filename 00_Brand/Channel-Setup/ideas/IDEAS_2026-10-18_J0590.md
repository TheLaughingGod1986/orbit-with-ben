Names `Dwarf Planet Ceres` and `Asteroid Belt` in the title per `OWB_2026-10-18.md` §4 item 1, and builds a 6-chapter planetary-geology and spacecraft investigation:
   - **Chapter 1 (`The Embryonic Planet Between Mars and Jupiter`):** How Italian astronomerGiuseppe Piazzi (1746–1826) spotted the first object in the main asteroid belt in 1801 — named for the Roman goddess of corn and harvests — and how Ceres was first called a planet, then classified as an asteroid for many years, and designated a dwarf planet in 2006 alongside Pluto and Eris; how Ceres formed 4.5 billion years ago as an "embryonic planet" stunted by nearby Jupiter's strong gravity before settling at 257 million miles (413 million km, 2.8 AU, where sunlight takes 22 minutes to arrive) about 4 billion years ago; and how with a radius of **296 miles (476 km)** — about **585 miles (940 km)** across, **1/13 the radius of Earth** (like a poppy seed next to a nickel) — it spins once every **9 hours** (tilted just **4 degrees**) and orbits the Sun every **1,682 Earth days (4.6 Earth years)** while holding **25% to 35% of the asteroid belt's total mass** (though Pluto is still 14 times more massive).
   - **Chapter 2 (`Up to Twenty-Five Percent Water and a Twin Ion Spaceship`):** Why astronomers suspected Ceres was no ordinary dry asteroid even before a spacecraft arrived: its low density indicates it is **about 25 percent water ice by mass** — making it the most water-rich body in the inner solar system after Earth in absolute amount of water, and potentially holding more water than Earth's oceans if composed of 25% water; how observations by the NASA/ESA Hubble Space Telescope (named for American astronomer Edwin Hubble, 1889–1953) in **2003 and 2004** showed a nearly spherical body with an icy layer wrapped around a rocky mantle, while the Herschel Space Observatory (named for British astronomer William Herschel, 1738–1822) detected water vapour plumes in **2012 and 2013**; and how NASA's *Dawn* spacecraft — achieving **6 years of powered flight** and a record **25,700 mph (41,400 km/h)** velocity change with its ion engines — orbited giant asteroid Vesta in **2011** (mapping its 310-mile / 500-km Rheasilvia basin and 290-mile / 465-km equatorial canyons) before arriving at Ceres in **2015** as the first spacecraft ever to visit a dwarf planet and the only spacecraft to orbit two extraterrestrial destinations, revealing a glittering array of **more than 300 bright features (`faculae`)** and ammonia clays from beyond Jupiter's orbit.
   - **Chapter 3 (`Twenty-Two Miles Above Occator Crater's Salt Crust` — Midpoint Twist `"Wrong."`):** Flips the early assumption that Ceres's gleaming white spots were exposed patches of pure surface water ice on a dead world: dipping to **less than 22 miles (35 km)** above the surface during its final extended mission from **2017 to October 2018**, *Dawn* mapped the **57-mile-wide (92-km-wide) Occator Crater** and its two reflective zones — central **Cerealia Facula** and eastern **Vinalia Faculae** — proving they contain the **largest deposit of carbonate minerals ever seen outside Earth**, made mostly of **sodium carbonate** (a compound of sodium, carbon, and oxygen) alongside **ammonium chloride** and **hydrated sodium chloride (`hydrohalite`, chemically bound with water)**. Because micrometeorites darken Ceres's surface over time and hydrated salts on an airless surface dehydrate **within hundreds of years**, whereas Occator's impact occurred **about 20 million years ago** and some bright deposits are **less than 2 million years old** yet still contain water today, Dawn scientists announced on **August 10, 2020** (in *Nature Astronomy*, *Nature Geoscience*, and *Nature Communications*) that briny liquid was still percolating to the surface very recently.
   - **Chapter 4 (`A Brine Reservoir Twenty-Five Miles Deep`):** Where did the salty liquid come from when a 20-million-year-old impact melt chamber would have frozen in just a few million years? By mapping Ceres's gravity and crustal density as a function of depth (a first for an ice-rich planetary body), Dawn Principal Investigator Carol Raymond and her team discovered that impact fractures reached a deep, long-lived **reservoir of brine (salt-enriched water) about 25 miles (40 km) deep and hundreds of miles wide** beneath Occator Crater, while conical hills inside the crater resemble Earth's polar **pingos** (formed by frozen pressurized groundwater, observed on a dwarf planet for the first time) and crustal density increases sharply with depth as salt and mud incorporate into the freezing lower crust.
   - **Chapter 5 (`Ahuna Mons: The Three-Mile-High Lonely Ice Volcano`):** Beyond Occator Crater, why does Ceres have no craters larger than **175 miles (280 km)** across despite 4.5 billion years of asteroid bombardment, and why does a solitary mountain rise above its craters? Dawn discovered **Ahuna Mons**, an enormous cryovolcanic dome rising **3 miles (5 km) high on its steepest side** (about 2.5 to 3 miles / 4 to 5 km high and 11 miles / 17 km wide at its base), formed when salt-enriched mud and ice — whose salts lower water's freezing point — extruded onto the surface as a **cryovolcano**, while subsurface ice and hydrothermal activity smoothed out and erased older giant craters across the globe.
   - **Chapter 6 (`Ernutet's Organics and a Two-Billion-Year Microbial Buffet`):** Answers the bigger astrobiology question: unlike Europa (`006`) or Enceladus (`046`), Ceres has no giant planet to tidally flex its interior today, yet Dawn discovered abundant **aliphatic organic molecules** (chains of carbon and hydrogen) around **Ernutet Crater** alongside global carbon; and on **August 20, 2025** (published in *Science Advances* by Sam Courville and colleagues at Arizona State University and NASA JPL), thermal and chemical models proved that between **0.5 billion and 2 billion years after Ceres formed (about 2.5 billion to 4 billion years ago)**, radioactive decay heated its rocky core enough to drive hot hydrothermal fluids carrying carbon dioxide and methane into its subsurface ocean — creating a long-lived **"buffet for microbes"** and proving that other 585-mile (940-km) dwarf planets and moons without tidal heating could also have been habitable in the past, which is why NASA placed Dawn in a decades-long quarantine orbit when its hydrazine ran out in October 2018 — before handing off inward to near-Earth carbon-rich asteroid **Bennu** (`049`, *Why Did NASA Bring Back a Sample of Asteroid Bennu?*).
