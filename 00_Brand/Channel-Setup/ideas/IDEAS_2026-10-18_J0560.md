   - **Chapter 1 (`The Whitest World in the Solar System`):** How British astronomer William Herschel (1738–1822) spotted Enceladus orbiting Saturn on August 28, 1789 and his son John Herschel (1792–1871) named it in his 1847 Cape of Good Hope observations after the mythological Greek giant; how Voyager spacecraft images in the 1980s showed a tiny moon only about 310 miles (500 km; 313 miles in Webb's release, about 4% the size of Earth and about as wide as Arizona, with mass `1.08 × 10²⁰ kg` and mean density `1,610 kg/m³`) whose surface has a visual geometric albedo of `1.0` — the whitest, most reflective body in the solar system — reflecting so much sunlight that its surface temperature drops to about −330 °F (−201 °C), with craters up to 22 miles (35 km) across in some regions yet an almost crater-free south pole littered with house-sized ice boulders.
   - **Chapter 2 (`Dione's Gravity and the 800-MPH Tiger Stripes`):** Why a 310-mile ice world isn't frozen solid: orbiting Saturn at 148,000 miles (238,000 km; `238.02 × 10³ km`, `3.9494` Saturnian radii) between Mimas and Tethys within the densest part of Saturn's E-ring, tidally locked Enceladus completes one orbit every 32.9 hours (`1.370218` days, ~33 hours) in a 2:1 orbital resonance with the larger moon Dione (discovered on March 21, 1684 by Italian-French astronomer Giovanni Domenico Cassini, 1625–1712, orbiting every 2.7 days at 234,500 miles / 377,400 km with a 349-mile / 562-km mean radius), whose gravity stretches Enceladus's orbit (`0.0045` eccentricity) and tidally heats its interior; and how in 2005 NASA's *Cassini* spacecraft discovered continuous jets of icy water particles and gas gushing at approximately 800 miles per hour (400 metres per second) from warm south-polar fractures informally called "tiger stripes," where organic materials are 20 times denser than expected and escaping ice droplets build Saturn's E-ring while most snow back down to keep Enceladus bright white.
   - **Chapter 3 (`The Wobble That Proved a Global Ocean` — Midpoint Twist `"Wrong."`):** Flips the early assumption that Enceladus was frozen from surface to core or held only a lens-shaped regional sea under its south pole: in September 2015, Cassini imaging team member Peter Thomas (born 1946) at Cornell University, Matthew Tiscareno at the SETI Institute, and Cassini imaging team lead Carolyn Porco (born 1953) at the Space Science Institute analysed more than seven years of Cassini images to measure Enceladus's tiny orbital wobble (libration), proving that its outer ice shell (averaging 12 to 16 miles / 20 to 25 km thick globally and thinning to half a mile to 3 miles / 1 to 5 km at the south pole) is completely detached from its rocky core by a **global liquid-water ocean**.
   - **Chapter 4 (`Thirty Miles High Through a Hydrothermal Plume`):** On October 28, 2015, *Cassini* executed its deepest-ever dive through the Enceladus plume, skimming just 30 miles (49 km) above the surface and sniffing the spray with its Ion and Neutral Mass Spectrometer (INMS, originally built to sample Titan's upper atmosphere); in April 2017, lead author Hunter Waite, Cassini project scientist Linda Spilker, and NASA science associate administrator Thomas Zurbuchen announced that the plume gas is nearly 98% water vapour, about 1% molecular hydrogen (`H₂`), and a mixture of carbon dioxide, methane, and ammonia — pairing with Cassini's Cosmic Dust Analyzer (CDA) detection of E-ring silica nanograins (formed only where liquid water and rock interact above ~200 °F / 90 °C) to prove active seafloor hydrothermal vents and methanogenesis energy ("like a candy store for microbes").
   - **Chapter 5 (`Phosphorus a Hundred Times Richer Than Earth's Seas`):** Of the six primary chemical elements required for life as we know it — carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur — phosphorus is the least abundant bio-essential element and had never been detected in an ocean beyond Earth; then on June 14, 2023, Frank Postberg and colleagues published in *Nature* their survey of 345 high-salinity Type 3 E-ring ice grains recorded by Cassini's Cosmic Dust Analyzer, identifying sodium phosphates (`Na₃PO₄` and `Na₂HPO₄` at mass peaks 125, 165, and 187 u) in 9 grains and proving through soda-lake hydrothermal experiments that Enceladus's alkaline (`pH 9.0–10.5`), carbonate-rich ocean holds orthophosphates at concentrations **at least 100-fold higher** than Earth's oceans.
   - **Chapter 6 (`Webb's 6,000-Mile Plume and the Saturn Water Torus`):** On May 30, 2023 (published May 17, 2023 in *Nature Astronomy*), NASA Goddard's Geronimo Villanueva, Stefanie Milam, and GTO program leader Heidi Hammel pointed the James Webb Space Telescope's NIRSpec Integral Field Unit from Lagrange Point 2 one million miles from Earth and discovered an Enceladus water-vapour plume spanning **more than 6,000 miles** — more than 20 times the size of the 313-mile moon itself, nearly the distance from Los Angeles to Buenos Aires — gushing at **about 79 gallons per second** (enough to fill an Olympic-sized swimming pool in a couple of hours vs more than 2 weeks with a garden hose) and feeding a planetary water torus where **30% stays within the E-ring torus and 70% escapes** to supply water across the entire Saturnian system, before operators' 2017 protection of pristine Enceladus hands off inward to Jupiter's 300-year storm (`047`, *Why Is Jupiter's Great Red Spot Shrinking After 300 Years?*).
