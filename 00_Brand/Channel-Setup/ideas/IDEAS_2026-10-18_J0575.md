   - **Chapter 2 (`580 Miles Lost Every Single Year`):** How astronomers tracked the Great Red Spot's downsizing since the 1930s: NASA's *Voyager 1* and *Voyager 2* flybys in 1979 and 1980 measured the storm at **14,500 miles (23,335 km)** across; the NASA/ESA Hubble Space Telescope (named after American astronomer Edwin Hubble, 1889–1953, launched in 1990) measured its long axis at **13,020 miles** in 1995 and **11,130 miles** in 2009; then beginning in 2012, observations revealed a sudden acceleration in shrinkage — losing **580 miles (just under 1,000 km) per year** and shifting from an oval into a circle — until planetary scientist Amy Simon (born 1971) at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center announced on May 15, 2014 (from April 21, 2014 Wide Field Camera 3 images) that the Great Red Spot had shrunk to **10,250 miles (just under 16,500 km)** across, less than half its late-1800s size, as very small eddies feed into the vortex.
   - **Chapter 3 (`Three Hundred Miles Deep Below the Clouds` — Midpoint Twist `"Wrong."`):** Flips the long-held textbook assumption that the Great Red Spot is a shallow surface weather feature confined to Jupiter's 44-mile (71 km) cloud deck (top ammonia ice, middle ammonium hydrosulfide crystals, and innermost water ice and vapour above the 40-mile / 65-km "Jovicline" transition layer): entering Jupiter's orbit in 2016 and sweeping low over the storm on July 11, 2017 and July 2019 at **130,000 mph (209,000 km/h)** across 37 orbital passes, NASA's *Juno* spacecraft used its Microwave Radiometer (MWR) and Deep Space Network gravity tracking (measuring velocity shifts as small as **0.01 millimetres per second** from more than **400 million miles / 650 million km** away, published October 28, 2021 in *Science* by Scott Bolton and Marzia Parisi) to prove that the anticyclone — colder on top, warmer at the bottom — extends over **200 miles (350 km)** and reaches a gravity-constrained depth of **about 300 miles (500 km)** below the cloud tops, while the east-west jet streams flanking it plunge **2,000 miles (3,200 km)** deep alongside eight Ferrel-like ammonia circulation cells per hemisphere (each at least 30 times larger than Earth's).
   - **Chapter 4 (`The 400-MPH Racing Lane Inside the Vortex`):** Why a shrinking storm is spinning faster on its rim: analysing 11 years of Hubble Outer Planet Atmospheres Legacy (OPAL) data from **2009 to 2020** (tracking tens to hundreds of thousands of wind vectors down to features a mere **105 miles across** — twice the length of Rhode Island — published September 27, 2021 in *Geophysical Research Letters* by UC Berkeley's Michael Wong and NASA Goddard's Amy Simon), astronomers discovered that while the storm's diameter contracted to **10,000 miles** in a tiered wedding-cake structure with high central clouds cascading down to outer layers and a lazily cruising interior, the average counterclockwise wind speed in its outer boundary ("high-speed ring") **increased by up to 8 percent** (less than **1.6 mph per Earth year**) to **exceed 400 miles per hour**, with an abrupt shift during a major nearby convective storm in 2017.
   - **Chapter 5 (`Hubble's 90-Day Stress-Ball Wobble`):** In a dedicated Hubble observing campaign spanning **90 days between December 2023 and March 2024** when Jupiter ranged from **391 million to 512 million miles** from Earth (published October 9, 2024 in *The Planetary Science Journal* by Amy Simon and Mike Wong), astronomers assembled a time-lapse movie showing that the Great Red Spot is not steady at all: it undergoes an unexpected **90-day oscillation**, squeezing in and out like a stress ball or jiggling like a bowl of gelatin at the exact same time its longitudinal motion speeds up and slows down — pushing against the northern and southern jet streams like a sandwich with too much filling in the middle — while ultraviolet images show its core grows brightest at its largest oscillating size due to reduced upper-atmosphere haze absorption.
   - **Chapter 6 (`Will the Great Red Spot Ever Disappear?`):** Answers the title question with atmospheric physics: unlike Neptune's dark spots, which lack strong bounding jet streams and drift wildly in latitude before vanishing in just a few years, or Jupiter's three white ovals that merged to form the Little Red Spot (about half the Great Red Spot's size), the Great Red Spot has been trapped in its southern latitude band for centuries; right now it is **over-filling its latitude band** relative to the wind field, and Amy Simon's Hubble team predicts that once it shrinks inside that band, the 2,000-mile-deep winds will hold it in a **stable, less-elongated shape** rather than letting it vanish — before handing off inward to the asteroid belt between Jupiter and Mars (`048`, *Why Does Dwarf Planet Ceres Have Glowing Salt Domes in the Asteroid Belt?*, discovered in 1801 by Giuseppe Piazzi, 1746–1826).
