## 3. Detailed Breakdown of the 5 Candidates

### Idea 1 (Rank #1 — Scaffolded as `049`): *Why Did NASA Bring Back a Sample of Asteroid Bennu?*
- **Title shape:** Why/How search question (`62` characters, primary keyword `Asteroid Bennu` + `NASA` front-loaded).
- **Thumbnail concept:** Split contrast — on the left, OSIRIS-REx's round sampler head plunging into dark exploding boulders at Nightingale crater; on the right, a gloved NASA Johnson curator holding the gleaming **121.6 g** black pebble and phosphate-flecked sample tray (`3 words: NOT SOLID ROCK`).
- **0:00–0:08 Hook:** On October 20, 2020, NASA's OSIRIS-REx spacecraft reached down to touch a diamond-shaped asteroid two hundred million miles from Earth — and instead of bouncing off solid stone, its robotic arm sank nearly half a metre into a surface as loose as a pit of plastic balls. Why did NASA spend seven years bringing back 121.6 grams of asteroid Bennu?
- **Midpoint twist (`"Wrong."`):** For two decades, astronomers thought Bennu was a dry, sandy pile of solar-system rubble whose boulders would act like solid concrete slabs. **Wrong.** When OSIRIS-REx arrived in December 2018, Bennu was a jagged boulder field flinging pebbles into space, its rocks were laced with microscopic crack networks, and when the sample capsule landed in Utah on September 24, 2023, its dust held magnesium-sodium phosphates, 11 evaporite salt minerals, 14 amino acids, all 5 DNA/RNA nucleobases, ribose and glucose sugars, and flexible "space gum" from a vanished saltwater parent world.
- **7 Number Objects:**
  1. **One-third of a mile (0.5 km / ~500 m)** equatorial width of Bennu — slightly wider than the height of the **Empire State Building** (`1,454 ft / 443 m`).
  2. **4.3 hours** rotation period and **186,000 miles (299,000 km)** closest six-year Earth approach — closer than the **Moon**.
  3. **72 feet (22 meters)** largest surface boulders and **more than 300** particle-ejection events observed in orbit, with Nightingale sample crater **460 feet (140 meters)** wide.
  4. **1.6 feet (48.8 centimeters)** depth the TAGSAM head sank into Bennu on **October 20, 2020** — particles packed so loosely that stepping on Bennu feels like stepping into a **pit of plastic balls**.
  5. **4.29 ounces (121.6 grams)** final sample mass announced **February 15, 2024** — more than double NASA's **2-ounce (60-gram)** requirement.
  6. **14 of 20** life amino acids, **all 5** genetic nucleobases, **ribose and six-carbon glucose** sugars, and **11** evaporite salt minerals crystallized over thousands of years.
  7. **6 times** higher abundance of presolar supernova dust than any previously studied astromaterial, inside boulders sliced to **one-thousandth the thickness of a human hair** (`30 micrometers` across).

### Idea 2 (Rank #2 — Recommended for `050`): *Why Saturn's Moon Iapetus Is Half Black and Half White*
- **Hook:** A 1,469-kilometre Saturnian moon with a dark leading hemisphere as black as fresh asphalt (albedo ~0.04), a trailing hemisphere as bright as packed snow (albedo ~0.60), and a 20-kilometre-high equatorial mountain ridge running three-quarters of the way around its equator like a walnut shell.
- **Midpoint twist:** Giovanni Domenico Cassini (1625–1712) noticed in 1671 that Iapetus was visible on only one side of Saturn, assuming one hemisphere was simply made of darker rock. **Wrong.** NASA's *Cassini* flybys (2004–2007) proved that dark dust swept inward from Saturn's outer moon Phoebe coats Iapetus's leading side, triggering a 129-K thermal runaway that sublimates water ice to the cold 113-K trailing side.

### Idea 3 (Rank #3 — Recommended for `051`): *Why NASA's DART Spacecraft Changed an Asteroid's Orbit by 33 Minutes*
- **Hook:** On September 26, 2022, NASA intentionally slammed a 570-kilogram spacecraft at 6.1 kilometres per second into the 160-metre moonlet Dimorphos.
- **Midpoint twist:** Engineers calculated that a pure kinetic punch would shorten Dimorphos's 11-hour-55-minute orbit by about 7 minutes. **Wrong.** The impact blasted more than 1,000 metric tons of rubble into a 10,000-kilometre tail, acting like a rocket exhaust that multiplied the momentum transfer by a factor of ~3.6 and shortened the orbit by **33 minutes**.

### Idea 4 (Rank #4 — Recommended for `052`): *Why Uranus's Moon Miranda Has a 12-Mile-High Ice Cliff*
- **Hook:** Just 290 miles (470 kilometres) across — one-seventh the diameter of Earth's Moon — Uranus's innermost major moon Miranda boasts Verona Rupes, a sheer fault scarp up to 12 miles (20 kilometres) high, ten times deeper than the Grand Canyon, where a rock dropped from the rim takes 12 minutes to hit the floor.
- **Midpoint twist:** After Voyager 2 flew past Miranda on January 24, 1986, planetary scientists thought the tiny moon had been shattered apart by a giant impact and reassembled inside-out. **Wrong.** Modern tectonic and tidal-heating models (including 2024 Johns Hopkins APL findings) show orbital resonance with neighbouring Uranus moons flexed a thin ice crust over a subsurface ocean up to 62 miles (100 kilometres) deep within the last 100 to 500 million years.

### Idea 5 (Rank #5 — Recommended for `053`): *Why NASA's Parker Solar Probe Flew Inside the Sun's Corona without Melting*
- **Hook:** Flying at 692,000 kilometres per hour through a solar corona heated to more than 1 million degrees Celsius, protected by a 11.4-centimetre carbon-composite heat shield that keeps the spacecraft's instruments at room temperature (~30 °C).
- **Midpoint twist:** How a million-degree plasma can fail to melt a spacecraft because the corona's particle density is so low that heat transfer is governed by radiant sunlight rather than conductive gas, while magnetic "switchbacks" whip solar wind out of coronal holes.
